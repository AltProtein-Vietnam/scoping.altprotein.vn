# Prologue: alternative protein for newcomers

**In one paragraph.** Alternative protein means protein for food or animal feed made from plants, fungi, microbes, algae or cultivated animal cells instead of from livestock and fish. Some of it is ancient (tofu, tempeh, Vietnam's own *chay* cooking); some is new (precision-fermented dairy proteins, cultivated meat). People promote it for different reasons: food and feed security, efficient use of land and feed, climate, public health, animal welfare and new industries. Each argument has serious counter-arguments, and the market has been through a boom (2019 to 2021) and a sharp correction since. Investment fell below USD 1 billion in 2025 for the first time since 2018, plant milk and familiar foods are holding up better than meat analogues, fermentation took 70% of the money raised in the first half of 2026, and a handful of countries approve new proteins while others ban some of them. This page gives newcomers enough background to read the rest of the report. It adds no new finding about Vietnam.

> **Method note.** This prologue is background from global evidence, so most claims carry the label "general". Numbers for Vietnam are taken from the chapters that follow. The research notes behind it are in `working-papers/wave10/`.

## P.1 Protein, and what "alternative" means

**Protein.** Proteins are chains of amino acids. The body needs protein for growth and repair, and nine of the amino acids are essential: the body cannot make them, so food must supply them [@PRA-02] {general|Medium}. The European Food Safety Authority estimated the adult reference intake at 0.83 g of protein per kg of body weight a day [@PRA-01] {general|High}. Foods differ in protein quality, meaning how well they supply digestible essential amino acids. The older score, PDCAAS, is capped at 100%; the newer DIAAS, which FAO recommended in 2013, is not capped and scores each amino acid separately [@PRA-02] {general|High}. Measured in pigs, soy protein isolate scores a DIAAS of about 97, pea protein concentrate about 70 and brown rice protein about 42, against 113 to 137 for pork; values measured in people can be lower [@SCI-40; @NQR-07; @SCI-42] {general|High}. Many labels give "crude protein" (nitrogen multiplied by 6.25), which also counts non-protein nitrogen; this matters for microbial proteins, which are rich in nucleic acids.

**Where protein comes from today.** Animal products supply about a third of the world's protein: 34% from livestock by FAO's count, or 37% including farmed fish in a widely cited global study [@PRB-17; @PRB-01] {general|High}. The rest comes from cereals, legumes, nuts and other plants.

**Alternative protein.** In this study, alternative protein means protein for food or feed made from plants, microorganisms, fungi, algae, insects or cultivated cells rather than from conventional livestock and fish ([[app-r3-glossary]]). The definition includes feed on purpose. About 77% of the world's soy is fed to livestock, and only about 6% is eaten directly by people [@PRA-21; @PRA-20] {general|Medium}. A protein that replaces soybean meal or fishmeal in feed is an alternative protein too.

{{kn:kn-soy-fed-to-animals}}

**Novel is a legal word.** Many alternative proteins are old. Tofu, tempeh, wheat gluten (*mì căn*) and Vietnam's *chay* (traditional vegetarian) cuisine are long-established foods, and *chay* is a long-standing periodic practice [@CHY-09; @CHY-10] {VN-direct|Medium}. In our assessment *chay* is still Vietnam's largest plant-protein eating occasion ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]). "Novel food" is a regulatory category for foods without a history of safe eating. In the European Union it means a food not eaten to a significant degree in the EU before 15 May 1997, and it needs approval before sale [@EXP-46] {general|High}. In Singapore it means a food without a 20-year history of use, and selling one without approval has been an offence since 28 November 2025 [@EXP-34; @REG-63] {VN-adjacent|High}. In the United States many new ingredients reach the market as "generally recognised as safe" (GRAS), a route with no mandatory pre-market approval; the regulator proposed tighter oversight on 10 August 2026 [@EXP-45] {general|High}. Vietnam has no novel-food category at all [@REG-01; @REG-02] {VN-direct|High}, which is one reason the report spends time on rules ([[ch07-rules]]).

## P.2 The families of alternative protein

| Family | What it is and how it is made | Examples and first sale | Where it stands in 2026 | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant-based: traditional | Whole or lightly processed legumes and grains: tofu, soy milk, tempeh, wheat gluten | Long established across Asia; tofu holds about 11 g of protein per 100 g in Vietnam's food table | Mature and cheap; the incumbent in Vietnam | [@DIE-12] {general\|Medium} |
| Plant-based: ingredients | Protein concentrates (about 60 to 80% protein) and isolates (about 90%) extracted from soy, pea, wheat or rice; textured vegetable protein made by extrusion, cooking and shaping under heat and pressure | Widely traded; the United States and China were Vietnam's largest suppliers of protein concentrates and textured protein in 2023 | Mature, cheap to make, traded as a commodity | [@FORM-01; @FORM-02; @TIC-12] {general\|Medium} |
| Plant-based: analogues | Consumer products that copy meat, milk or eggs, made from the ingredients above; high-moisture extrusion makes fibrous whole cuts | Beyond Burger (2016), Impossible Burger (27 July 2016) | Mature technology; US plant-based meat sales peaked around 2021 and have fallen since 2023 (down 10% in 2025) | [@PRA-12; @PRA-13; @GLB-01] {general\|High} |
| Fermentation: traditional | Moulds, yeasts and bacteria transform a food: koji mould makes soy sauce and miso; *Rhizopus* makes tempeh | Long established; soy sauce, miso and Vietnamese *tương* (fermented soybean paste) | Mature; a skills base for newer fermentation | [@SCI-19; @PRA-19; @RD-61] {general\|Medium} |
| Fermentation: biomass | A fungus, yeast or bacterium grown in tanks on sugar (or on gases) is itself the protein | Mycoprotein (Quorn, UK, 1985); gas-fermented Solein (Singapore approval 2022); feed protein from steel-mill gas (China, 2021) | Commercial but small; China has 10,000-tonne-class gas-fermentation feed capacity; at least one feed plant (Calysseo, China) has halted | [@PRA-03; @RGN-04; @PRC-30; @FTG-06] {general\|Medium} |
| Fermentation: precision | Engineered microbes make one specific protein, which is purified | Insulin (approved 1982) and cheese rennet (1990) are the precedents; US regulators raised no questions on Impossible's heme (2018; the burger has sold since 2016) and Perfect Day's whey (2020) | Commercial for high-value, low-dose ingredients; too costly for bulk protein so far | [@PRA-10; @PRA-07; @PRA-11; @PRA-14; @PRA-12; @SCI-01] {general\|High} |
| Cultivated meat and seafood | Animal cells are multiplied in a nutrient medium in bioreactors, sometimes on edible scaffolds | First tasting 2013 (London); first approved for sale, Singapore, November 2020 | Approved in a few countries; tiny volumes; high and debated costs | [@PRA-16; @PRA-15; @RGN-04; @RGN-03] {general\|High} |
| Microalgae and duckweed | Single-celled algae (spirulina, chlorella) or tiny floating plants grown in ponds or tanks | Spirulina supplements; duckweed protein concentrate judged safe in the EU (2023) | Niche; mostly supplements and research | [@PRA-18; @SCI-30] {general\|Medium} |
| Insects | Mainly black soldier fly larvae reared on by-products, sold as feed | A 10,000 t a year feed plant has run in Vietnam since 2023 | A benchmark in this report, not a recommendation | [@ECO-01; @ECO-03] {VN-direct\|High} |
| Blends (hybrids) | Animal protein mixed with plant, fungal or cultivated ingredients | Soy already extends processed meat (31% of one Vietnamese processor's meat recipes list soy protein); Singapore's retail cultivated chicken (2024) was 3% cultivated cells | In the report's reading, the likeliest route for new proteins into ordinary food ([[ch14-frontier-demand]]) | [@BUY-05; @NOV-10; @NOV-11] {VN-adjacent\|Medium} |

**Making it is the easy part; making it cheaply is the hard part.** Each family has a different cost problem ([[app-s13-science]], [[ch21-frontier-technology]]):

- **Plant proteins** are cheap because they reuse the soy, pea and wheat supply chains; the price of the raw flour or meal decides most of the cost ([[ch09-economics]]).
- **Biomass fermentation** works at scale for feed yeasts and for mycoprotein, but plants are expensive to build. A peer-reviewed cost model puts mycoprotein at about USD 30 per kg of protein, enough to compete with beef but not with chicken [@COST-43] {general|Medium}. Methane-fed feed protein has struggled: Calysseo's demonstration-scale methane-protein plant in Chongqing, China, halted production in 2026 while improvements are made [@FTG-06] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Precision fermentation** is proven for small, high-value molecules. Bulk food protein would need yields above about 50 g per litre by expert estimates, and the best published leghemoglobin titre is 10.1 g per litre (in a 5-litre bioreactor) [@SCI-01; @SCI-05] {general|Medium}.
- **Cultivated meat** cost estimates differ six- to ninefold, on different bases: a model commissioned by a philanthropic funder gives USD 37 to 51 per kg of wet cells and doubts food prices are reachable, while a model commissioned by an advocacy group gives USD 5.66 per kg by 2030 under optimistic assumptions [@COST-47; @SCI-14; @PRA-17] {general|Medium}. The largest reported production run is 20,000 litres [@FTB-24] {general|Medium}.

## P.3 Why people argue for alternative protein

Different actors lead with different arguments. In our reading of their documents ([[ch29-actor-check]]), governments tend to argue security and industry; development agencies, nutrition and resilience; foundations, climate and animal welfare; investors, market size. This report leads with food and feed security, industrial value and trade, and treats the other motives as real reasons some actors hold ([[front-how-to-read]]). The main arguments, with the best numbers we found:

1. **Demand for animal products is still rising.** OECD and FAO project that calories from meat, dairy, eggs and fish will rise 6% per person worldwide from 2025 to 2034, and 24% in lower-middle-income countries such as Vietnam; output of meat, dairy and eggs rises 17% [@PRB-04] {general|High} {fx:projection}. FAO projects demand for land-animal products to rise about 20% by 2050 [@PRB-07] {general|Medium} {fx:projection}. Neither supports the common claim that protein demand "doubles by 2050"; an older FAO projection (2011) was 73% more meat, and FAO's 2023 figure, for all land-animal products, is lower [@PRB-27; @PRB-07] {general|Medium}.
2. **Security and resilience.** Brazil and the United States ship about 84% of the world's soybean exports (2024/25; our calculation from USDA data) [@QNT-07] {general|High}. Animal disease can remove supply at once: African swine fever killed or forced the culling of about 40% of China's pigs in 2018 to 2019 (as cited), and Vietnam lost nearly 6 million pigs, over a fifth of its herd, in 2019 [@GEO-14; @GEO-15] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. Fishmeal, the premium aquafeed protein, is limited in supply (about 4.5 Mt was produced in 2016) and reached about USD 2,500 per tonne of fishmeal in August 2026 [@FM-06; @FM-01] {general|Medium}. More sources of protein mean fewer single points of failure.
3. **Land and feed.** Meat, farmed fish, eggs and dairy use about 83% of the world's farmland but provide 37% of its protein and 18% of its calories [@PRB-01] {general|High}. About 36% of crop calories go to animal feed, and about 12% of those come back as food [@PRB-10] {general|High}.
4. **Climate.** Food systems caused about 34% of human greenhouse gas emissions in 2015 [@PRB-09] {general|High}. For livestock FAO gives two figures: 14.5% (reference year 2005) and, in its latest estimate, about 12% or 6.2 Gt CO2e (reference year 2015); the two use different reference years, so the lower figure is not evidence that emissions fell [@PRB-08; @PRB-07] {general|High}. Per 100 g of protein, footprints differ widely by food (global averages, chart below).
5. **Public health.** Cattle, sheep, chickens and pigs received about 99,500 t of antimicrobials in 2020 (a modelled estimate with a wide range, 68,535 to 193,052 t), projected to rise to about 107,500 t by 2030; heavy use feeds antimicrobial resistance [@GEO-25] {general|High} {fx:projection}. Animals took about 73% of all antimicrobial use worldwide in 2017 (as cited in that study) and about 72% in Vietnam in 2015 [@GEO-25; @MAC-36] {general|Medium}. About 60% of new human infectious diseases from 1940 to 2004 came from animals, though about 72% of those came from wild animals rather than farm animals [@PRB-12] {general|High}. The WHO cancer agency classes processed meat as carcinogenic (Group 1) and red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A); the groups rate the strength of evidence, not the size of the risk [@PRB-14] {general|High}.
6. **Animal welfare.** At least 86 billion land animals were slaughtered for meat in 2024, 91% of them chickens (our sum of FAO data for seven species) [@PRB-15] {general|High}. Estimates for fish, from an animal-welfare advocacy project, run into the trillions but are highly uncertain [@PRB-16] {general|Low}.
7. **New industries.** Governments frame alternative protein as part of a wider bioeconomy: China's 2022 bioeconomy plan names new foods, and India and Japan set bioeconomy targets for 2030 [@PRB-26; @ECF-31; @ECF-32] {general|Medium}. These are targets, not outcomes. An advocacy group counts a cumulative total of at least USD 2.5 billion of public money for alternative protein worldwide as of 2025, up from about USD 700 million in its 2021 report [@ECF-43] {general|Low}.

## P.4 The case against, and the open debates

Each argument above has a serious answer, and readers will meet these in Vietnam:

- **Most of what livestock eat is not human food.** About 86% of global livestock feed (by dry matter) is grass, leaves, crop residues and by-products people cannot eat [@PRB-11] {general|High}. This and the "36% of crop calories" figure measure different things, and both are true.
- **Livelihoods.** FAO says livestock supports the livelihoods of at least 1.3 billion people [@PRB-17] {general|Medium}. Any shift affects farmers, traders and feed mills first.
- **Nutrition.** Meat, eggs and milk supply high-quality protein and hard-to-get iron, zinc and vitamin B12, which matter most for young children and pregnant women; more than half of the world's preschool children lack at least one key micronutrient [@PRB-18] {general|Medium}. In low-income countries people are projected to eat only about 143 kcal a day of animal-source food in 2034, below the 300 kcal FAO uses as a healthy-diet benchmark [@PRB-04; @PRB-05] {general|High} {fx:projection}. For some people the case is for more animal-source food, not less.
- **Processing and health.** Plant-based meat products often contain less protein, zinc and vitamin B12 than the meat they copy, and most are high in sodium [@PRB-21] {general|Medium}. A large UK study linked plant-based ultra-processed food to higher heart-disease risk, but most of that food was bread and snacks, not analogues; a small trial found lower LDL cholesterol when people swapped animal meat (mostly beef and pork) for plant-based meat [@PRB-19; @PRB-20] {general|Medium}. The comparison matters: against processed meats, a Hong Kong survey found plant-based products lower in salt [@PRB-22] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Cultivated meat may not be greener.** With pharmaceutical-grade growth media, one peer-reviewed life-cycle study found near-term cultivated meat could emit 4 to 25 times as much as median beef [@FTB-26] {general|High}. Its carbon dioxide is long-lived while cattle methane is short-lived, so it is "not prima facie climatically superior" [@PRB-23] {general|High}. An industry-informed study finds it beats beef and pork when powered by renewable energy [@PRB-24] {general|Medium}.
- **Consumers.** In the US, the most studied market, plant-based meat sales fell from USD 1.4 billion (2021) to USD 1.0 billion (2025), and the share of households buying it in a year fell from 20% (2021, as restated by GFI) to 11% (retail scan data published by GFI, an advocacy group) [@GLB-01; @GLB-02] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Forecasts made during the boom overshot badly: in 2019 a bank forecast a USD 140 billion alternative meat market by 2029, against USD 6.6 billion of plant-based meat and seafood retail sales in 2025 (a total market against retail sales only, but the gap is still about twentyfold) [@GLB-44; @GLB-05] {general|Medium}. This report does not use published market-size forecasts as findings ([[app-d7-global-benchmarks]]).
- **New dependencies.** Alternative protein does not end import dependence by itself. In our Vietnamese retail audit, every locally made product with 10 g or more of protein per 100 g uses textured soy, soy concentrate or wheat gluten (partly inferred from labels), inputs Vietnam imports, much of them from China [@FORM-01; @FORM-02; @TIC-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. A security case needs domestic production, not just a different product ([[ch16-business-buyers]]).
- **Politics.** Some governments restrict new proteins: Italy banned cultivated meat in 2023 and Hungary voted a ban in November 2025; seven US states had banned or paused its sale by mid-2026, and South Dakota's ban took effect on 1 July 2026; and the EU will reserve 31 meat words such as "steak" and "chicken" for animal products from 2029, while "burger" and "sausage" remain allowed [@PRC-18; @PRC-19; @PRC-16; @HSC-35; @PRC-21] {general|High}.

## P.5 The international landscape in 2026

**A boom and a correction.** The Impossible and Beyond burgers launched in 2016, and Beyond Meat listed on the Nasdaq in May 2019 [@PRC-09; @PRA-13; @PRC-08] {general|High}. Investment peaked in 2021 at about USD 7 billion (GFI's current series; it reported USD 5.0 billion at the time), fell every year to USD 881 million in 2025, the first year below USD 1 billion since 2018, and was USD 359 million in the first half of 2026 [@HSC-10; @PRC-04; @CAP-51; @PRC-01] {general|Medium}. The total since 2017 is over USD 19.5 billion. Money has moved towards fermentation, which took 252 of the 359 million raised in the first half of 2026 [@PRC-01] {general|High}. The figures come from GFI, an advocacy organisation, using a commercial deal database.

**Markets.** Euromonitor retail estimates published by GFI show global retail sales of plant-based meat, seafood, milk, yogurt, ice cream and cheese of about USD 28.9 billion in 2025, up 3%; plant-based meat and seafood were USD 6.6 billion of this, nearly 83% of it sold in Europe and North America [@GLB-06; @GLB-05] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Familiar products did better than analogues: plant milk held 13% of US milk sales in 2025 (16% in 2021), and German sales volume of tofu, tempeh and seitan rose 16.7% in 2024 [@GLB-01; @GLB-02; @GLB-09] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Beyond Meat's revenue fell from USD 464.7 million (2021) to USD 275.5 million (2025), and it swapped USD 1.1 billion of debt for new notes and shares in October 2025 [@GLB-46; @PRC-06; @PRC-07] {general|Medium}. Several cultivated and fermentation firms closed in 2024 and 2025, including one that went bankrupt weeks after its US approval [@HSC-08; @FTB-24; @FTB-17] {general|Medium}. Asia-Pacific holds over a third of global plant-based dairy sales, but private investment in alternative proteins fell again worldwide in 2025 [@GLB-49] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.

{{chart:chart-us-pbm-trend}}

**Rules.** Approvals are concentrated in a few places:

- **Singapore** had approved 18 novel foods by August 2026, including six cultivated products, from the world's first cultivated chicken (2020) to cultivated beef (2026) [@RGN-04] {VN-adjacent|High}.
- **The United States** has cleared five cultivated products, including salmon in 2025, and sent ten "no questions" letters for fermentation-made ingredients in 2025 [@RGN-03; @FTB-17] {general|High}.
- **Australia and New Zealand** approved cultivated quail in 2025, and **Israel** cultivated beef in 2024 [@RGN-18; @RGN-20] {general|Medium}.
- **The European Union** has applications for cultivated products (since 2024) but no approval; it authorised its first mycelium protein in June 2026 [@PRC-11; @PRC-12; @HSC-12] {general|Medium}. **The UK** allowed cultivated chicken in pet food (on sale February 2025) and runs a regulatory sandbox [@PRC-14; @PRC-13] {general|High}.
- **China** approved a mycoprotein and its first ingredient from a genetically modified microbe in 2025 and certified a bacterial feed protein made from steel-mill gas in 2021 [@HSC-11; @PRC-30] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. **Korea and Thailand** accept applications and **Japan** has draft guidelines (May 2026), but we found no cultivated approval in any of the three by September 2026 [@RGN-34; @FTB-20; @RGN-32; @RGN-33] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. The regional detail is in [[app-s3-regional]].

**Public strategies.** Some governments fund the field directly: Denmark's plant-based food grant (DKK 675 million for 2023 to 2030), the Netherlands' EUR 60 million in conditional National Growth Fund support for cellular agriculture (2022), and the UK's national alternative protein centre (GBP 15 million, 2024) [@VIS-01; @PRC-24; @PRC-25] {general|Medium}. China's 2025 No. 1 Central Document calls for a "diversified food supply system" and new food resources [@PRC-29] {VN-adjacent|High}. Others have stepped back: Singapore dropped its "30 by 30" production goal in November 2025 and left alternative protein out of its 2035 targets, and the US revoked its 2022 biotechnology executive order in March 2025 [@RGN-08; @RGN-09; @PRC-28] {general|High}. Thailand's 2026 to 2035 biotechnology framework names future food and alternative proteins, and Korea's Food Tech Industry Promotion Act took effect in December 2025 [@RGN-35; @RGN-21] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.

**Feed.** In feed, alternative proteins compete on price with soybean meal (about USD 880 per tonne of protein in August 2026) and fishmeal (about USD 3,846), at 46% and 65% protein (our calculation) [@FM-01; @FM-02] {general|High}. China's Shougang LanzaTech produced about 10,200 t of bacterial feed protein from steel-mill gas in 2023, while insect meal and methane-fed protein plants in Europe and China have struggled [@FTG-08; @FTG-06; @AIS-21] {general|Medium}. Fishmeal supply tightened again in 2026 when Peru's first anchovy quota fell 36% [@HSC-29] {general|Medium}.

## P.6 Where Vietnam fits

Vietnam is a large producer of meat, fish and eggs built on imported feed protein: it imported 2.61 Mt of soybeans in 2025 (98.4% of imports plus domestic output), and about 99% of the soy protein in its feed is imported (our calculation from USDA supply data) [@MAC-04; @MAC-09] {VN-direct|Medium}. It has a strong plant-protein tradition in *chay*, a large starch industry and amino-acid and yeast fermentation plants, and feed mills that already buy alternative feed ingredients, but no novel-food rules, little pilot capacity and little venture capital ([[ch01-why-vietnam]], [[ch02-alt-protein-today]]). The report's conclusion is that Vietnam's case is a supply case, and that demand pulls new protein mainly as ingredients sold to the firms that already make food and feed ([[front-exec-summary]]).

## P.7 Before you read: terms and conventions

| Term | Meaning in this report |
|---|---|
| Crude protein | Nitrogen content multiplied by 6.25; overstates true protein in microbial products |
| DIAAS | Protein-quality score based on digestible essential amino acids; 100 or more is excellent |
| Concentrate, isolate | Plant protein ingredients of about 60 to 80% and about 90% protein |
| Textured vegetable protein (TVP), extrusion | Dry protein pieces made by cooking protein flour under heat and pressure |
| Biomass fermentation | Growing a microbe that is itself the protein (mycoprotein, yeast, bacterial protein) |
| Precision fermentation | Engineered microbes making one specific protein, such as whey or heme |
| Titre (titer) | How much product a fermentation makes, in grams per litre; a key cost driver |
| Cultivated meat | Meat grown from animal cells; also called cultured or cell-based |
| Novel food | Legal category (EU, Singapore) for foods without a history of safe eating; needs approval before sale |
| GRAS | US route in which a firm, with qualified experts, concludes an ingredient is safe; notifying the regulator is voluntary |
| Blend, hybrid | A product mixing animal and alternative protein |
| *Chay* | Vietnamese vegetarian practice and cuisine, and the *đồ chay* products that serve it |
| Soybean meal (SBM), fishmeal | The two main protein feeds; the prices new feed proteins must beat |
| Frontier | The newer routes: biomass and precision fermentation, gas fermentation, cultivated meat and seafood |
| B2B ingredient | A protein sold to firms that already make food or feed, rather than a consumer brand |
| Import substitution, displacement | Replacing imported ingredients with Vietnamese ones, as against replacing meat on the plate; the report counts them separately |
| Scenario (for example S-ALT, D-BENCH) | A labelled future used to test choices, not a forecast ([[ch23-scenarios-2050]], [[ch18-demand-sizing]]) |
| Play | A specific business or programme someone could start in Vietnam ([[ch26-plays]]) |

**Units.** Mt is million tonnes, kt thousand tonnes, t tonne. The report counts protein and product separately: "19,000 t of protein" is much more product by weight. Money is in USD unless stated, at 26,000 VND per USD. Most claims carry an evidence label and confidence level, and forward-looking and demand claims carry further tags; [[front-how-to-read]] explains them. The full glossary, in English and Vietnamese, is [[app-r3-glossary]].
