# Prologue: alternative protein for newcomers

**In brief.**

- Alternative protein is protein made from plants, fungi, microbes, algae or animal cells grown in tanks, instead of from farm animals and fish. Some of it is ancient (tofu, tempeh, Vietnam's own *chay* cooking). Some is new (dairy proteins made by engineered microbes, and meat grown from cells).
- Worldwide, it is made both for food and for animal feed. This study is about protein for people. Feed appears only as context: it is the hidden import behind the meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish that people eat.
- People argue for it for different reasons: food security, better use of land and crops, climate, public health, animal welfare and new industries. Each argument has serious counter-arguments.
- The market boomed from 2019 to 2021 and has fallen sharply since. Investment fell below USD 1 billion in 2025, for the first time since 2018. Fermentation took 70% of the money raised in the first half of 2026.
- Plant milk and familiar foods are holding up better than products that copy meat. A handful of countries approve new proteins, while others ban some of them.
- This page gives newcomers enough background to read the rest of the report. It adds no new finding about Vietnam.

> **Method note.** This prologue is background from global evidence. It changes no finding of the study. Most claims carry the label "general", which means global evidence with no claim that it applies to Vietnam. Numbers for Vietnam are taken from the chapters that follow. The research notes behind this page are in `working-papers/wave10/`, and for disease risk and imported meat and dairy in `working-papers/wave11/`.

## P.1 Protein, and what "alternative" means

**Protein.** Proteins are chains of amino acids. The body needs protein for growth and repair. Nine of the amino acids are essential: the body cannot make them, so food has to supply them [@PRA-02] {general|Medium}. The European Food Safety Authority estimated the adult reference intake at 0.83 g of protein per kg of body weight a day [@PRA-01] {general|High}.

**Protein quality.** Foods differ in how well they supply essential amino acids in a form the body can digest. Two scores measure this. The older one, the protein digestibility-corrected amino acid score (PDCAAS), is capped at 100%. The newer digestible indispensable amino acid score (DIAAS) is not capped and scores each amino acid separately. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) recommended it in 2013 [@PRA-02] {general|High}.

Measured in pigs, soy protein isolate scores a DIAAS of about 97, pea protein concentrate about 70 and brown rice protein about 42. Pork scores 113 to 137. Values measured in people can be lower [@SCI-40; @NQR-07; @SCI-42] {general|High}.

Many labels give "crude protein": the nitrogen content multiplied by 6.25. This also counts nitrogen that is not part of a protein. That matters for microbial proteins, which are rich in nucleic acids (the material of DNA and RNA).

**Where protein comes from today.** Animal foods supply about a third of the world's protein. By FAO's count, livestock supply 34%; a widely cited global study gives 37%, including farmed fish [@PRB-17; @PRB-01] {general|High}. The rest comes from cereals, legumes, nuts and other plants.

**Alternative protein.** In general use, alternative protein means protein made from plants, microorganisms, fungi, algae, insects or cultivated cells, rather than from conventional livestock and fish ([[app-r3-glossary]]). Worldwide it is made for both food and animal feed. Feed takes a large share of some crops. About 77% of the world's soy is fed to livestock, and only about 6% is eaten directly by people [@PRA-21; @PRA-20] {general|Medium}.

So a protein that replaces soybean meal or fishmeal in animal feed also counts as alternative protein. Soybean meal is what is left of soybeans after the oil is pressed out; fishmeal is ground, dried fish.

{{kn:kn-soy-fed-to-animals}}

**Our subject: protein for people.** We look at protein that people eat. Alternative proteins made for animal feed appear here only as context. Section P.6 explains why.

**"Novel" is a legal word.** Many alternative proteins are old. Tofu, tempeh, wheat gluten (*mì căn*) and Vietnam's *chay* (traditional vegetarian) cuisine are long-established foods. Eating *chay* on set days is a long-standing practice [@CHY-09; @CHY-10] {VN-direct|Medium}. In our assessment, *chay* is still Vietnam's largest plant-protein eating occasion ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]).

"Novel food" is a legal category for foods without a history of safe eating. Countries define it differently:

- **European Union:** a food not eaten to a significant degree in the EU before 15 May 1997. It needs approval before sale [@EXP-46] {general|High}.
- **Singapore:** a food without a 20-year history of use. Selling one without approval has been an offence since 28 November 2025 [@EXP-34; @REG-63] {VN-adjacent|High}.
- **United States:** many new ingredients reach the market as "generally recognised as safe" (GRAS). This route has no mandatory approval before sale. The regulator proposed tighter oversight on 10 August 2026 [@EXP-45] {general|High}.
- **Vietnam:** there is no novel-food category at all [@REG-01; @REG-02] {VN-direct|High}. This is one reason the report spends time on rules ([[ch07-rules]]).

## P.2 The families of alternative protein

The table describes each family of alternative protein and where it stands in 2026.

| Family | What it is and how it is made | Examples and first sale | Where it stands in 2026 | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant-based: traditional | Whole or lightly processed legumes and grains: tofu, soy milk, tempeh, wheat gluten | Long established across Asia. Tofu holds about 11 g of protein per 100 g in Vietnam's food table | Mature and cheap; the established product in Vietnam | [@DIE-12] {general\|Medium} |
| Plant-based: ingredients | Protein concentrates (about 60 to 80% protein) and isolates (about 90%) extracted from soy, pea, wheat or rice. Textured vegetable protein (TVP) is made by extrusion: cooking and shaping protein under heat and pressure | Widely traded. The United States and China were Vietnam's largest suppliers of protein concentrates and textured protein in 2023 | Mature, cheap to make, traded as a commodity | [@FORM-01; @FORM-02; @TIC-12] {general\|Medium} |
| Plant-based: analogues | Consumer products that copy meat, milk or eggs, made from the ingredients above. High-moisture extrusion makes fibrous whole cuts | Beyond Burger (2016), Impossible Burger (27 July 2016) | Mature technology. US plant-based meat sales peaked around 2021 and have fallen since 2023 (down 10% in 2025) | [@PRA-12; @PRA-13; @GLB-01] {general\|High} |
| Fermentation: traditional | Moulds, yeasts and bacteria change a food. Koji mould makes soy sauce and miso; *Rhizopus* mould makes tempeh | Long established: soy sauce, miso and Vietnamese *tương* (fermented soybean paste) | Mature; a skills base for newer fermentation | [@SCI-19; @PRA-19; @RD-61] {general\|Medium} |
| Fermentation: biomass | A fungus, yeast or bacterium grown in tanks on sugar (or on gases) is itself the protein | Mycoprotein, a fungal protein (Quorn, UK, 1985); gas-fermented Solein (Singapore approval 2022); feed protein from steel-mill gas (China, 2021) | Commercial but small. China has 10,000-tonne-class gas-fermentation feed capacity; at least one feed plant (Calysseo, China) has halted | [@PRA-03; @RGN-04; @PRC-30; @FTG-06] {general\|Medium} |
| Fermentation: precision | Engineered microbes make one specific protein, which is then purified | Insulin (approved 1982) and cheese rennet (1990) came first. US regulators raised no questions on Impossible Foods' soy leghemoglobin, a heme-carrying protein made by yeast and used for flavour (2018; the burger has sold since 2016), and Perfect Day's whey (2020) | Commercial for high-value ingredients used in small doses. Too costly for bulk protein so far | [@PRA-10; @PRA-07; @PRA-11; @PRA-14; @PRA-12; @SCI-01] {general\|High} |
| Cultivated meat and seafood | Animal cells multiplied in a nutrient liquid inside bioreactors (closed growing tanks), sometimes on edible scaffolds that the cells grow on | First tasting 2013 (London). First approved for sale in Singapore, November 2020 | Approved in a few countries; tiny volumes; high and debated costs | [@PRA-16; @PRA-15; @RGN-04; @RGN-03] {general\|High} |
| Microalgae and duckweed | Single-celled algae (spirulina, chlorella) or tiny floating plants (duckweed) grown in ponds or tanks | Spirulina supplements. Duckweed protein concentrate judged safe in the EU (2023) | Niche; mostly supplements and research | [@PRA-18; @SCI-30] {general\|Medium} |
| Insects | Mainly black soldier fly larvae reared on by-products, sold as feed | A 10,000-tonne-a-year feed plant has run in Vietnam since 2023 | In this report, a benchmark (a point of comparison) only, not a recommendation | [@ECO-01; @ECO-03] {VN-direct\|High} |
| Blends (hybrids) | Animal protein mixed with plant, fungal or cultivated ingredients | Soy already extends processed meat: 22 of 67 (33%) of one Vietnamese processor's meat recipes list soy protein. Singapore's retail cultivated chicken (2024) was 3% cultivated cells | In the report's reading, the likeliest route for new proteins into ordinary food ([[ch14-frontier-demand]]) | [@BUY-05; @NOV-10; @NOV-11] {VN-adjacent\|Medium} |

**Feed uses are context here.** Some families are also made as animal feed, such as bacterial protein from gas and insect meal. This study records them as background only, because it is about protein for people.

**Making it is the easy part; making it cheaply is the hard part.** Each family has a different cost problem ([[app-s13-science]], [[ch21-frontier-technology]]):

- **Plant proteins** are cheap because they use the existing soy, pea and wheat supply chains. The price of the raw flour or meal decides most of the cost ([[ch09-economics]]).
- **Biomass fermentation** works at scale for feed yeasts and for mycoprotein, but the plants are expensive to build. A peer-reviewed cost model puts mycoprotein at about USD 30 per kg of protein: enough to compete with beef, but not with chicken [@COST-43] {general|Medium}.
- **Precision fermentation** is proven for small, high-value molecules. Bulk food protein would need a titre (the concentration of product in the fermenter) above about 50 g per litre, by expert estimates. The best published titre for leghemoglobin (a heme-carrying protein; Impossible Foods uses the soy version) is 10.1 g per litre, from a yeast in a 5-litre bioreactor [@SCI-01; @SCI-05; @PRA-11] {general|Medium}.
- **Cultivated meat** cost estimates differ six- to ninefold, on different bases. A model commissioned by a philanthropic funder gives USD 37 to 51 per kg of wet cells and doubts that food prices are reachable. A model commissioned by an advocacy group gives USD 5.66 per kg by 2030 under optimistic assumptions [@COST-47; @SCI-14; @PRA-17] {general|Medium}. The largest reported production run is 20,000 litres [@FTB-24] {general|Medium}.

## P.3 Why people argue for alternative protein

Different actors lead with different arguments. In our reading of their documents ([[ch29-actor-check]]):

- governments tend to argue security and industry;
- development agencies argue nutrition and resilience;
- foundations argue climate and animal welfare;
- investors argue market size.

This report leads with food security, industrial value and trade. Food security here includes the imported feed behind the meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish that people eat. The report treats the other motives as real reasons that some actors hold ([[front-how-to-read]]). These are the main arguments, with the best numbers we found:

1. **Demand for animal foods is still rising.** A joint outlook by FAO and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development looks at 2025 to 2034. It projects that calories from meat, dairy, eggs and fish will rise 6% per person worldwide. In lower-middle-income countries such as Vietnam the rise is 24%. World output of meat, dairy and eggs rises 17% by 2034 [@PRB-04] {general|High} {fx:projection}.

   FAO projects demand for land-animal products to rise about 20% by 2050 [@PRB-07] {general|Medium} {fx:projection}. Neither supports the common claim that protein demand "doubles by 2050". An older FAO projection (2011) was 73% more meat, and FAO's 2023 figure, for all land-animal products, is lower [@PRB-27; @PRB-07] {general|Medium}.
2. **Security and resilience.** More sources of protein for people mean fewer single points of failure. Animal disease can remove supply at once. African swine fever, a deadly viral pig disease, killed or forced the culling of about 40% of China's pigs in 2018 to 2019 (as cited). Vietnam lost nearly 6 million pigs, over a fifth of its herd, in 2019 [@GEO-14; @GEO-15] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.

   A second source of protein helps because it does not fail when animals fall sick. That is not the same as lowering disease risk, which depends on how animals are farmed and traded (section P.4). In Vietnam, at the scale demand supports, new protein would spare about 142,000 pigs a year in 2035, while swine fever culls in 2025 were 8.4 times that number (our calculation from [@MAC-01; @PAN-03] and [[app-d8-demand-model]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}.

   Feed is the other weak point. Brazil and the United States ship about 84% of the world's soybean exports (2024/25; our calculation from US Department of Agriculture data) [@QNT-07] {general|High}. Fishmeal, the premium protein in feed for farmed fish and shrimp, is limited in supply: about 4.5 million tonnes (Mt) was produced in 2016. It reached about USD 2,500 per tonne of fishmeal in August 2026 [@FM-06; @FM-01] {general|Medium}.
3. **Land and crops.** Meat, farmed fish, eggs and dairy use about 83% of the world's farmland but provide 37% of its protein and 18% of its calories [@PRB-01] {general|High}. About 36% of crop calories go to animal feed, and about 12% of those come back as food [@PRB-10] {general|High}.
4. **Climate.** Food systems caused about 34% of human greenhouse gas emissions in 2015 [@PRB-09] {general|High}. Per 100 g of protein, footprints differ widely by food (global averages, chart below).

   For livestock's share of emissions, FAO gives two figures. One is 14.5% (reference year 2005). The latest is about 12%, or 6.2 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (Gt CO2e; reference year 2015). Because the two use different reference years, the lower figure is not evidence that emissions fell [@PRB-08; @PRB-07] {general|High}.
5. **Public health.** Cattle, sheep, chickens and pigs received about 99,500 tonnes (t) of antimicrobials (antibiotics and similar medicines) in 2020. This is a modelled estimate with a wide range, 68,535 to 193,052 t, and it is projected to rise to about 107,500 t by 2030. Heavy use adds to antimicrobial resistance, in which germs stop responding to these medicines [@GEO-25] {general|High} {fx:projection}. Animals took about 73% of all antimicrobial use worldwide in 2017 (as cited in that study) and about 72% in Vietnam in 2015 [@GEO-25; @MAC-36] {general|Medium}.

   About 60% of new human infectious diseases from 1940 to 2004 came from animals. But about 72% of those came from wild animals, not farm animals [@PRB-12] {general|High}. These figures show where the risk comes from; they do not show that replacing meat lowers it (section P.4). The cancer agency of the World Health Organization classes processed meat as carcinogenic (Group 1): it causes cancer. It classes red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A). The groups rate how strong the evidence is, not how large the risk is [@PRB-14] {general|High}.
6. **Animal welfare.** At least 86 billion land animals were slaughtered for meat in 2024, 91% of them chickens (our sum of FAO data for seven species) [@PRB-15] {general|High}. Estimates for fish, from an animal-welfare advocacy project, run into the trillions but are highly uncertain [@PRB-16] {general|Low}.
7. **New industries.** Governments present alternative protein as part of a wider bioeconomy, meaning industries built on biology. China's 2022 bioeconomy plan names new foods, and India and Japan set bioeconomy targets for 2030 [@PRB-26; @ECF-31; @ECF-32] {general|Medium}. These are targets, not outcomes. An advocacy group counts at least USD 2.5 billion of public money for alternative protein worldwide, in total up to 2025. Its 2021 report counted about USD 700 million [@ECF-43] {general|Low}.

## P.4 The case against, and the open debates

Each argument above has a serious answer. Readers will meet these answers in Vietnam:

- **Most of what livestock eat is not human food.** About 86% of global livestock feed is grass, leaves, crop residues and by-products that people cannot eat. This share is measured by dry matter (weight without water) [@PRB-11] {general|High}. This and the "36% of crop calories" figure measure different things, and both are true.
- **Livelihoods.** FAO says livestock supports the livelihoods of at least 1.3 billion people [@PRB-17] {general|Medium}. Any shift affects farmers, traders and feed mills first.
- **Nutrition.** Meat, eggs and milk supply high-quality protein and hard-to-get iron, zinc and vitamin B12, which matter most for young children and pregnant women. More than half of the world's preschool children lack at least one important micronutrient, a vitamin or mineral needed in small amounts [@PRB-18] {general|Medium}. In low-income countries, people are projected to eat only about 143 kilocalories (kcal) a day of animal-source food in 2034. That is below the 300 kcal FAO uses as a healthy-diet benchmark [@PRB-04; @PRB-05] {general|High} {fx:projection}. For some people the case is for more animal-source food, not less.
- **Processing and health.** Plant-based meat products often contain less protein, zinc and vitamin B12 than the meat they copy, and most are high in sodium (salt) [@PRB-21] {general|Medium}. A large UK study linked plant-based ultra-processed food to higher heart-disease risk, but most of that food was bread and snacks, not meat copies. A small trial found lower LDL ("bad") cholesterol when people swapped animal meat (mostly beef and pork) for plant-based meat [@PRB-19; @PRB-20] {general|Medium}. The comparison matters: a Hong Kong survey found plant-based products lower in salt than processed meats [@PRB-22] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Disease risk depends on how animals are farmed and traded.** We found no study that measures a fall in zoonotic (animal-to-human), pandemic or antimicrobial-resistance risk from replacing animal-source food with alternative protein. Claims that it does come from reviews and opinion pieces, several by advocacy or company authors [@PAN-27; @PAN-30; @PAN-31] {general|Medium}. Published research ties these risks to the wildlife trade, farm biosecurity and antibiotic use [@PAN-28; @PAN-29] {general|Medium}. A costed study of preventing pandemics at source looks at surveillance, control of the wildlife trade and less deforestation, not at diet change [@PAN-28] {general|Medium}. In Vietnam, at the scale demand supports, the effect of new protein on these risks would be negligible (section P.3, item 2; [[ch01-why-vietnam]]).
- **Cultivated meat may not be greener.** One peer-reviewed life-cycle study counted emissions across the whole production chain. It found that near-term cultivated meat, made with pharmaceutical-grade growth media, could emit 4 to 25 times as much as median beef [@FTB-26] {general|High}. Its carbon dioxide stays in the air for a long time, while methane from cattle breaks down faster. So it is "not prima facie climatically superior", that is, not clearly better for the climate [@PRB-23] {general|High}. An industry-informed study finds it beats beef and pork when powered by renewable energy [@PRB-24] {general|Medium}.
- **Consumers.** The US is the most studied market. The Good Food Institute (GFI), an advocacy group, publishes retail scan data (sales recorded at the till) for it. Plant-based meat sales fell from USD 1.4 billion (2021) to USD 1.0 billion (2025). The share of households buying it in a year fell from 20% (2021, as restated by GFI) to 11% [@GLB-01; @GLB-02] {general|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Forecasts.** Forecasts made during the boom overshot badly. In 2019 a bank forecast a USD 140 billion alternative meat market by 2029. Retail sales of plant-based meat and seafood were USD 6.6 billion in 2025. The forecast covered a total market and the sales figure only retail, but the gap is still about twentyfold [@GLB-44; @GLB-05] {general|Medium}. This report does not use published market-size forecasts as findings ([[app-d7-global-benchmarks]]).
- **New dependencies.** Alternative protein does not end import dependence by itself. Our Vietnamese retail audit looked at locally made products with 10 g or more of protein per 100 g. Every one uses textured soy, soy concentrate or wheat gluten (partly inferred from labels). Vietnam imports these inputs, much of them from China [@FORM-01; @FORM-02; @TIC-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. China supplied about 84% of the tonnage of food plant-protein ingredients (soy isolate, textured protein and gluten) that reached Vietnam in 2025 (our calculation) [@BUY-02] {VN-direct|Medium}. A security case needs domestic inputs, not just a different product or a factory in Vietnam ([[ch01-why-vietnam]], [[ch16-business-buyers]]).
- **Politics.** Some governments restrict new proteins. Italy banned cultivated meat in 2023, and Hungary voted a ban in November 2025. Seven US states had banned or paused its sale by mid-2026, and South Dakota's ban took effect on 1 July 2026. From 2029 the EU will reserve 31 meat words such as "steak" and "chicken" for animal products, while "burger" and "sausage" remain allowed [@PRC-18; @PRC-19; @PRC-16; @HSC-35; @PRC-21] {general|High}.

## P.5 The international landscape in 2026

**A boom and a correction.** The Impossible and Beyond burgers launched in 2016, and Beyond Meat listed its shares on the Nasdaq stock exchange in May 2019 [@PRC-09; @PRA-13; @PRC-08] {general|High}. Investment peaked in 2021 at about USD 7 billion in GFI's current series (it reported USD 5.0 billion at the time). It fell every year to USD 881 million in 2025, the first year below USD 1 billion since 2018. It was USD 359 million in the first half of 2026 [@HSC-10; @PRC-04; @CAP-51; @PRC-01] {general|Medium}.

The total since 2017 is over USD 19.5 billion. Money has moved towards fermentation, which took 252 of the 359 million raised in the first half of 2026 [@PRC-01] {general|High}. The figures come from GFI, an advocacy organisation, using a commercial deal database.

**Markets.** Global retail sales of plant-based meat, seafood, milk, yogurt, ice cream and cheese were about USD 28.9 billion in 2025, up 3%. Plant-based meat and seafood were USD 6.6 billion of this, nearly 83% of it sold in Europe and North America. These figures are estimates by the market research firm Euromonitor, published by GFI [@GLB-06; @GLB-05] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Familiar products did better than products that copy meat. Plant milk held 13% of US milk sales in 2025 (16% in 2021). In Germany, sales volume of tofu, tempeh and seitan (wheat gluten) rose 16.7% in 2024 [@GLB-01; @GLB-02; @GLB-09] {general|High} {dx:revealed}.

Some firms are in trouble. Beyond Meat's revenue fell from USD 464.7 million (2021) to USD 275.5 million (2025). In October 2025 it swapped USD 1.1 billion of debt for new notes (a form of debt) and shares [@GLB-46; @PRC-06; @PRC-07] {general|Medium}. Several cultivated and fermentation firms closed in 2024 and 2025, including one that went bankrupt weeks after its US approval [@HSC-08; @FTB-24; @FTB-17] {general|Medium}. Asia-Pacific holds over a third of global plant-based dairy sales, but private investment in alternative proteins fell again worldwide in 2025 [@GLB-49] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.

{{chart:chart-us-pbm-trend}}

**Rules.** Approvals are concentrated in a few places:

- **Singapore** had approved 18 novel foods by August 2026, including six cultivated products, from the world's first cultivated chicken (2020) to cultivated beef (2026) [@RGN-04] {VN-adjacent|High}.
- **The United States** has cleared five cultivated products, including salmon in 2025. In 2025 it also sent ten "no questions" letters for fermentation-made ingredients. In such a letter, the regulator says it has no questions about a firm's own safety conclusion [@RGN-03; @FTB-17] {general|High}.
- **Australia and New Zealand** approved cultivated quail in 2025, and **Israel** cultivated beef in 2024 [@RGN-18; @RGN-20] {general|Medium}.
- **The European Union** has had applications for cultivated products since 2024 but no approval. It authorised its first mycelium (fungal) protein in June 2026 [@PRC-11; @PRC-12; @HSC-12] {general|Medium}. **The UK** allowed cultivated chicken in pet food (on sale February 2025). It runs a regulatory sandbox, a scheme in which firms test new products under the regulator's supervision [@PRC-14; @PRC-13] {general|High}.
- **China** approved a mycoprotein and its first ingredient from a genetically modified microbe in 2025. It certified a bacterial feed protein made from steel-mill gas in 2021 [@HSC-11; @PRC-30] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Korea and Thailand** accept applications, and **Japan** has draft guidelines (May 2026). But we found no cultivated approval in any of the three by September 2026 [@RGN-34; @FTB-20; @RGN-32; @RGN-33] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. The regional detail is in [[app-s3-regional]].

**Public strategies.** Some governments fund the field directly. Denmark has a plant-based food grant (DKK 675 million, in Danish kroner, for 2023 to 2030). In 2022 the Netherlands granted EUR 60 million of conditional support from its National Growth Fund for cellular agriculture, which makes animal products from cells. The UK funds a national alternative protein centre (GBP 15 million, 2024) [@VIS-01; @PRC-24; @PRC-25] {general|Medium}. China's 2025 No. 1 Central Document, its main yearly policy paper on farming, calls for a "diversified food supply system" and new food resources [@PRC-29] {VN-adjacent|High}.

Others have stepped back. Singapore dropped its "30 by 30" local production goal in November 2025 and left alternative protein out of its 2035 targets. The US revoked its 2022 biotechnology executive order in March 2025 [@RGN-08; @RGN-09; @PRC-28] {general|High}. Thailand's 2026 to 2035 biotechnology framework names future food and alternative proteins, and Korea's Food Tech Industry Promotion Act took effect in December 2025 [@RGN-35; @RGN-21] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.

**Feed: background only.** Alternative proteins are also sold as animal feed, which this study treats only as context (section P.6; detail in [[app-s6-feed-market]]). In feed, they compete on price with soybean meal and fishmeal. Per tonne of protein, soybean meal cost about USD 880 in August 2026 and fishmeal about USD 3,846, at 46% and 65% protein (our calculation) [@FM-01; @FM-02] {general|High}. China's Shougang LanzaTech produced about 10,200 t of bacterial feed protein from steel-mill gas in 2023. Insect meal and methane-fed protein plants in Europe and China have struggled [@FTG-08; @FTG-06; @AIS-21] {general|Medium}.

## P.6 Where Vietnam fits, and what this study covers

**Vietnam makes much of its animal protein on imported feed.** It is a large producer of meat, fish and eggs. In 2025 it imported 2.61 Mt of soybeans (98.4% of imports plus domestic output). About 99% of the soy protein in its feed is imported (our calculation from US Department of Agriculture supply data) [@MAC-04; @MAC-09] {VN-direct|Medium}.

**Vietnam also imports finished meat and dairy, but origin subsidies do not drive it.** It imported 978,000 t of meat and meat products worth USD 2.0 billion in 2025, and USD 1.44 billion of dairy [@MIM-04; @MIM-05; @MIM-12] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Among the largest lines by value in 2023 were frozen beef and buffalo meat (mostly Indian buffalo meat) and skim milk powder [@MIM-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. For most of what Vietnam imports, the origin gives no price support, and India taxes its buffalo meat rather than supporting it [@MIM-15; @MIM-16] {general|High}.

{{kn:kn-soy-protein-import}}

Vietnam has strengths: a strong plant-protein tradition in *chay*, a large starch industry, and plants that make amino acids and yeast by fermentation. It also has gaps: no novel-food rules, little pilot capacity (small plants for trial production) and little venture capital ([[ch01-why-vietnam]], [[ch02-alt-protein-today]]). The report concludes that Vietnam's case is a supply case: it rests on how the country makes its protein, not on consumer fashion. Demand pulls new protein mainly as ingredients sold to the firms that already make food, not as consumer brands ([[front-exec-summary]]).

**This study is about protein for people.** It asks five questions, for now and to 2050:

- What could Vietnam make at home for people to eat?
- From what raw materials?
- At what cost?
- Under which rules?
- Who would buy it?

Feed appears only as context. It is the hidden import behind the meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish that people eat, and so part of the food-security picture. The study does not treat feed ingredients (fishmeal replacements, or microbial, insect or duckweed feed) as something to make. Earlier drafts assessed them, and that record stays in [[app-s6-feed-market]], [[app-f6-aquafeed-feedstock-futures]] and the changelog ([[app-m5-changelog]]).

**What Vietnam could make first.** The report's plays, meaning specific businesses or programmes someone could start, are all about food. Three lead ([[ch26-plays]]):

- domestic textured plant protein, to replace imports from China;
- a hub (a cluster of plants on one site) that turns cassava starch into fermentation sugar for food fermentation;
- shared pilot and tolling fermentation: trial-scale fermenters that firms share, or rent time on.

## P.7 Before you read: terms and conventions

| Term | Meaning in this report |
|---|---|
| Protein for people | The study's subject: protein that people eat. Feed is context only |
| Feed | Food for farm animals and farmed fish. In this study, context: the hidden import behind meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish |
| Crude protein | Nitrogen content multiplied by 6.25; overstates true protein in microbial products |
| DIAAS | Protein-quality score based on digestible essential amino acids; 100 or more is excellent |
| Concentrate, isolate | Plant protein ingredients of about 60 to 80% and about 90% protein |
| Textured vegetable protein (TVP), extrusion | Dry protein pieces made by cooking protein flour under heat and pressure |
| Biomass fermentation | Growing a microbe that is itself the protein (mycoprotein, yeast, bacterial protein) |
| Mycoprotein | Protein-rich fungus grown in tanks, as in Quorn |
| Precision fermentation | Engineered microbes making one specific protein, such as whey or soy leghemoglobin (a heme-carrying protein) |
| Titre (titer) | How much product a fermentation makes, in grams per litre; a main factor in cost |
| Tolling | Renting time on someone else's fermenter or production line |
| Cultivated meat | Meat grown from animal cells; also called cultured or cell-based |
| Novel food | Legal category (EU, Singapore) for foods without a history of safe eating; needs approval before sale |
| GRAS | "Generally recognised as safe": a US route in which a firm, with qualified experts, concludes an ingredient is safe; telling the regulator is voluntary |
| Blend, hybrid | A product mixing animal and alternative protein |
| *Chay* | Vietnamese vegetarian practice and cuisine, and the *đồ chay* (vegetarian food) products that serve it |
| Soybean meal (SBM), fishmeal | The two main protein feeds. In this study they are context: the study does not aim to make feed that replaces them |
| Frontier | The newer routes: biomass and precision fermentation, gas fermentation, cultivated meat and seafood |
| Business-to-business (B2B) ingredient | A protein sold to firms that already make food, rather than to shoppers under a consumer brand |
| Import substitution, displacement | Import substitution replaces imported ingredients with Vietnamese ones; displacement replaces meat on the plate. The report counts them separately |
| Scenario (for example S-ALT, D-BENCH) | A labelled future used to test choices, not a forecast ([[ch23-scenarios-2050]]). S-ALT is the alternative-protein scenario of the 2050 protein balance ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]); D-BENCH is the benchmark demand path ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]) |
| Play | A specific business or programme someone could start in Vietnam. The report has seven, all about protein for people ([[ch26-plays]]) |

**Units.** Mt is million tonnes, kt thousand tonnes, t tonne. The report counts protein and product separately: "19,000 t of protein" is much more product by weight. Money is in USD unless stated, at 26,000 VND per USD. Most claims carry an evidence label and confidence level, and forward-looking and demand claims carry further tags. [[front-how-to-read]] explains them. The full glossary, in English and Vietnamese, is [[app-r3-glossary]].
