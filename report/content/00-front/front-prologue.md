# Prologue: alternative protein for newcomers

**In brief.**

- Alternative protein is protein made from plants, fungi, microbes, algae or animal cells, instead of from farm animals and fish. Some is ancient, like tofu and Vietnam's *chay* cooking. Some is new, like meat grown from cells.
- This study is about protein for people. Feed for animals appears only as context.
- People argue for alternative protein on food security, land, climate, health, animal welfare and new industry. Each argument has serious counter-arguments.
- The market boomed from 2019 to 2021 and has fallen since. Investment dropped below USD 1 billion in 2025.
- This page gives background from global evidence. It adds no new finding about Vietnam.

## P.1 Protein, and what "alternative" means

**Protein.** Proteins are chains of amino acids. The body needs nine essential amino acids that it cannot make, so food has to supply them [@PRA-02] {general|Medium}. Adults need about 0.83 g of protein per kg of body weight a day [@PRA-01] {general|High}.

**Protein quality.** Foods differ in how well they supply those amino acids in a form the body can digest. The current score is DIAAS (digestible indispensable amino acid score), recommended by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) in 2013 [@PRA-02] {general|High}. Measured in pigs, soy protein isolate scores about 97, pea protein about 70 and brown rice protein about 42. Pork scores 113 to 137 [@SCI-40; @NQR-07; @SCI-42] {general|High}.

**Where protein comes from today.** Animal foods supply about a third of the world's protein: 34% by FAO's count, or 37% including farmed fish [@PRB-17; @PRB-01] {general|High}. The rest comes from cereals, legumes, nuts and other plants.

**Alternative protein.** It means protein made from plants, microorganisms, fungi, algae or cultivated cells, rather than from livestock and fish ([[app-r3-glossary]]). Worldwide it is made for food and for animal feed. About 77% of the world's soy is fed to livestock, and only about 6% is eaten directly by people [@PRA-21; @PRA-20] {general|Medium}.

{{kn:kn-soy-fed-to-animals}}

**Our subject: protein for people.** Alternative proteins made for animal feed appear here only as context. Section P.6 explains why.

**"Novel" is a legal word.** Many alternative proteins are old: tofu, tempeh, wheat gluten (*mì căn*) and *chay* (traditional vegetarian) cooking. *Chay* is still Vietnam's largest plant-protein eating occasion ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]). "Novel food" is a legal category for foods without a history of safe eating:

- **European Union:** a food not eaten to a significant degree before 15 May 1997. It needs approval before sale [@EXP-46] {general|High}.
- **Singapore:** a food without a 20-year history of use. Selling one without approval has been an offence since November 2025 [@EXP-34; @REG-63] {VN-adjacent|High}.
- **United States:** many new ingredients reach the market as "generally recognised as safe" (GRAS), with no mandatory approval [@EXP-45] {general|High}.
- **Vietnam:** there is no novel-food category at all [@REG-01; @REG-02] {VN-direct|High}. That is why the report spends time on rules ([[ch07-rules]]).

## P.2 The families of alternative protein

| Family | What it is | Examples | Where it stands in 2026 | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plant-based: traditional | Whole or lightly processed legumes and grains | Tofu, soy milk, tempeh, wheat gluten | Mature and cheap; the established product in Vietnam | [@DIE-12] {general\|Medium} |
| Plant-based: ingredients | Protein concentrates (60% to 80% protein) and isolates (about 90%). Textured vegetable protein (TVP) is made by extrusion: cooking protein under heat and pressure | Traded worldwide; China and the US supply Vietnam | Mature and cheap; a commodity | [@FORM-01; @FORM-02; @TIC-12] {general\|Medium} |
| Plant-based: analogues | Consumer products that copy meat, milk or eggs | Beyond Burger, Impossible Burger (2016) | US plant-based meat sales peaked around 2021 and fell 10% in 2025 | [@PRA-12; @PRA-13; @GLB-01] {general\|High} |
| Fermentation: traditional | Moulds, yeasts and bacteria change a food | Soy sauce, miso, tempeh, Vietnamese *tương* (fermented soybean paste) | Mature; a skills base for newer fermentation | [@SCI-19; @PRA-19; @RD-61] {general\|Medium} |
| Fermentation: biomass | A fungus, yeast or bacterium grown in tanks is itself the protein | Mycoprotein (Quorn, 1985); gas-fermented Solein | Commercial but small | [@PRA-03; @RGN-04; @PRC-30; @FTG-06] {general\|Medium} |
| Fermentation: precision | Engineered microbes make one specific protein, which is then purified | Insulin (1982), cheese rennet (1990), Perfect Day's whey (2020) | Commercial for high-value ingredients in small doses; too costly for bulk protein | [@PRA-10; @PRA-07; @PRA-11; @PRA-14; @PRA-12; @SCI-01] {general\|High} |
| Cultivated meat and seafood | Animal cells grown in closed tanks (bioreactors) | First approved in Singapore, 2020 | Approved in a few countries; tiny volumes; high costs | [@PRA-16; @PRA-15; @RGN-04; @RGN-03] {general\|High} |
| Microalgae and duckweed | Single-celled algae or tiny floating plants grown in ponds or tanks | Spirulina; duckweed protein (judged safe in the EU, 2023) | Niche; mostly supplements | [@PRA-18; @SCI-30] {general\|Medium} |
| Blends (hybrids) | Animal protein mixed with plant, fungal or cultivated ingredients | One Vietnamese processor lists soy protein in a third of its meat recipes | The likeliest route for new protein into everyday food ([[ch14-frontier-demand]]) | [@BUY-05; @NOV-10; @NOV-11] {VN-adjacent\|Medium} |

**Making it is easy; making it cheaply is hard.** Each family has a different cost problem ([[ch21-frontier-technology]]):

- **Plant proteins** are cheap because they use existing soy, pea and wheat supply chains. The price of the raw flour decides most of the cost ([[ch09-economics]]).
- **Biomass fermentation** works at scale, but the plants are expensive to build. One cost model puts mycoprotein at about USD 30 per kg of protein: able to compete with beef, not chicken [@COST-43] {general|Medium}.
- **Precision fermentation** is proven for small, high-value molecules. Bulk food protein would need yields above about 50 g per litre; the best published yield for one such protein is 10.1 g per litre [@SCI-01; @SCI-05; @PRA-11] {general|Medium}.
- **Cultivated meat** cost estimates differ six- to ninefold. One model gives USD 37 to 51 per kg of wet cells; an advocacy-funded model gives USD 5.66 per kg by 2030 [@COST-47; @SCI-14; @PRA-17] {general|Medium}.

## P.3 Why people argue for alternative protein

Governments tend to argue security and industry; development agencies, nutrition; foundations, climate and animal welfare; investors, market size ([[ch29-actor-check]]). This report leads with food security, industrial value and trade. The main arguments:

1. **Demand for animal foods is still rising.** FAO and the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) project calories from animal foods to rise 24% per person in lower-middle-income countries such as Vietnam, from 2025 to 2034 [@PRB-04] {general|High} {fx:projection}. Worldwide, FAO projects demand for land-animal products to rise about 20% by 2050. That does not support the common claim that protein demand "doubles by 2050" [@PRB-07] {general|Medium} {fx:projection}.
2. **Security and resilience.** A second source of protein does not fail when animals fall sick. African swine fever, a deadly pig disease, cost Vietnam nearly 6 million pigs in 2019, over a fifth of its herd [@GEO-14; @GEO-15] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. But at the scale demand supports, new protein would spare about 142,000 pigs a year in 2035. Swine fever culls in 2025 were 8.4 times that (our calculation from [@MAC-01; @PAN-03]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. Feed is the other weak point: Brazil and the US ship about 84% of the world's soybean exports [@QNT-07] {general|High}.
3. **Land and crops.** Meat, farmed fish, eggs and dairy use about 83% of the world's farmland but provide 37% of its protein [@PRB-01] {general|High}. About 36% of crop calories go to animal feed [@PRB-10] {general|High}.
4. **Climate.** Food systems caused about 34% of human greenhouse gas emissions in 2015 [@PRB-09] {general|High}. FAO puts livestock's share at about 12% for 2015. Its older 14.5% figure used a different base year, so the drop is not evidence that emissions fell [@PRB-08; @PRB-07] {general|High}.
5. **Public health.** Farm animals took about 73% of the world's antimicrobial medicines in 2017, and heavy use adds to drug resistance [@GEO-25; @MAC-36] {general|Medium}. About 60% of new human infectious diseases came from animals, but mostly from wild animals, not farm animals [@PRB-12] {general|High}. The World Health Organization's cancer agency classes processed meat as causing cancer [@PRB-14] {general|High}.
6. **Animal welfare.** At least 86 billion land animals were slaughtered for meat in 2024, 91% of them chickens (our sum of FAO data) [@PRB-15] {general|High}.
7. **New industries.** China, India and Japan include new foods in their plans for industries built on biology [@PRB-26; @ECF-31; @ECF-32] {general|Medium}. These are targets, not outcomes.

## P.4 The case against, and the open debates

Each argument has a serious answer, and readers will meet these answers in Vietnam:

- **Most of what livestock eat is not human food.** About 86% of livestock feed, by dry weight, is grass, leaves, crop residues and by-products [@PRB-11] {general|High}.
- **Livelihoods.** Livestock supports at least 1.3 billion people [@PRB-17] {general|Medium}. Any shift affects farmers, traders and feed mills first.
- **Nutrition.** Meat, eggs and milk supply high-quality protein and hard-to-get iron, zinc and vitamin B12 [@PRB-18] {general|Medium}. In low-income countries, people are projected to eat less animal-source food than a healthy diet needs [@PRB-04; @PRB-05] {general|High} {fx:projection}. For some people the case is for more animal food, not less.
- **Processing and health.** Plant-based meat often has less protein, zinc and vitamin B12 than the meat it copies, and most is high in salt [@PRB-21] {general|Medium}. A small trial found lower "bad" cholesterol when people swapped meat for plant-based meat [@PRB-19; @PRB-20] {general|Medium}.
- **Disease risk depends on farming and trade.** We found no study that measures lower pandemic or drug-resistance risk from replacing meat. Claims that it does come from reviews and opinion pieces [@PAN-27; @PAN-30; @PAN-31] {general|Medium}. Research ties these risks to the wildlife trade, farm biosecurity and antibiotic use [@PAN-28; @PAN-29] {general|Medium}.
- **Cultivated meat may not be greener.** One life-cycle study found near-term cultivated meat could emit 4 to 25 times as much as median beef [@FTB-26] {general|High}. Another finds it beats beef and pork on renewable power [@PRB-24] {general|Medium}.
- **Consumers.** US plant-based meat sales fell from USD 1.4 billion in 2021 to USD 1.0 billion in 2025. The share of households buying it fell from 20% to 11% [@GLB-01; @GLB-02] {general|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Forecasts overshot.** In 2019 a bank forecast a USD 140 billion alternative meat market by 2029. Retail sales were USD 6.6 billion in 2025 [@GLB-44; @GLB-05] {general|Medium}. This report does not use published market-size forecasts as findings.
- **New dependencies.** Every locally made product we audited with 10 g or more of protein per 100 g used imported soy or gluten. China supplied about 84% of these ingredients reaching Vietnam in 2025 (our calculation) [@FORM-01; @FORM-02; @TIC-12; @BUY-02] {VN-direct|Medium}. A security case needs domestic inputs, not just a factory in Vietnam ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).
- **Politics.** Italy and Hungary banned cultivated meat, and seven US states had banned or paused it by mid-2026. From 2029 the EU will reserve 31 meat words such as "steak" for animal products [@PRC-18; @PRC-19; @PRC-16; @HSC-35; @PRC-21] {general|High}.

## P.5 The international landscape in 2026

**A boom and a correction.** Investment peaked in 2021 at about USD 7 billion. It fell every year to USD 881 million in 2025, the first year below USD 1 billion since 2018 [@HSC-10; @PRC-04; @CAP-51; @PRC-01] {general|Medium}. Money has moved to fermentation, which took 252 of the USD 359 million raised in the first half of 2026 [@PRC-01] {general|High}.

**Markets.** Global retail sales of plant-based meat, seafood and dairy were about USD 28.9 billion in 2025. Meat and seafood were USD 6.6 billion of this, mostly in Europe and North America [@GLB-06; @GLB-05] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Familiar products did better than meat copies. Plant milk held 13% of US milk sales in 2025. In Germany, sales of tofu, tempeh and seitan rose 16.7% in 2024 [@GLB-01; @GLB-02; @GLB-09] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Beyond Meat's revenue fell from USD 465 million in 2021 to USD 276 million in 2025 [@GLB-46; @PRC-06; @PRC-07] {general|Medium}.

{{chart:chart-us-pbm-trend}}

**Rules.** Approvals are concentrated in a few places:

- **Singapore** had approved 18 novel foods by August 2026, including six cultivated products [@RGN-04] {VN-adjacent|High}.
- **The United States** has cleared five cultivated products, including salmon in 2025 [@RGN-03; @FTB-17] {general|High}.
- **The European Union** authorised its first fungal protein in June 2026, but no cultivated product [@PRC-11; @PRC-12; @HSC-12] {general|Medium}.
- **China** approved a mycoprotein and its first ingredient from a genetically modified microbe in 2025 [@HSC-11; @PRC-30] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Korea, Thailand and Japan** accept applications or have draft guidelines, but no cultivated approval by September 2026 [@RGN-34; @FTB-20; @RGN-32; @RGN-33] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.

**Public strategies.** Denmark, the Netherlands and the UK fund the field directly [@VIS-01; @PRC-24; @PRC-25] {general|Medium}. China's 2025 farm policy calls for a "diversified food supply system" [@PRC-29] {VN-adjacent|High}. Others have stepped back: Singapore dropped its local production goal in 2025 [@RGN-08; @RGN-09; @PRC-28] {general|High}.

## P.6 Where Vietnam fits, and what this study covers

**Vietnam makes much of its animal protein on imported feed.** In 2025 it imported 2.61 Mt (million tonnes) of soybeans. About 99% of the soy protein in its feed is imported (our calculation) [@MAC-04; @MAC-09] {VN-direct|Medium}.

**It also imports meat and dairy, but subsidies do not drive this.** Vietnam imported 978,000 t of meat worth USD 2.0 billion in 2025, and USD 1.44 billion of dairy [@MIM-04; @MIM-05; @MIM-12] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. For most of it, the exporting country gives no price support [@MIM-15; @MIM-16] {general|High}.

{{kn:kn-soy-protein-import}}

Vietnam has strengths: a plant-protein tradition in *chay*, a large starch industry, and fermentation plants making amino acids and yeast. It has gaps: no novel-food rules, little pilot capacity and little venture capital ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]). Its case rests on how it makes its protein, not on consumer fashion. Demand pulls new protein mainly as ingredients sold to firms that already make food.

**This study asks five questions, for now and to 2050.** What could Vietnam make at home for people to eat? From what raw materials? At what cost? Under which rules? Who would buy it? Feed appears only as the hidden import behind meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish.

**What Vietnam could make first.** Three plays lead ([[ch26-plays]]):

- domestic textured plant protein, to replace imports from China;
- a hub that turns cassava starch into fermentation sugar;
- shared pilot and tolling fermentation: small fermenters that firms share or rent time on.

## P.7 Before you read: terms and conventions

| Term | Meaning in this report |
|---|---|
| Protein for people | The study's subject. Feed is context only |
| Crude protein | Nitrogen content multiplied by 6.25; overstates true protein in microbial products |
| DIAAS | Protein-quality score; 100 or more is excellent |
| Concentrate, isolate | Plant protein ingredients of about 60% to 80% and about 90% protein |
| Textured vegetable protein (TVP) | Dry protein pieces made by cooking protein flour under heat and pressure (extrusion) |
| Biomass fermentation | Growing a microbe that is itself the protein |
| Mycoprotein | Protein-rich fungus grown in tanks, as in Quorn |
| Precision fermentation | Engineered microbes making one specific protein, such as whey |
| Titre | How much product a fermentation makes, in grams per litre; a main driver of cost |
| Tolling | Renting time on someone else's fermenter or production line |
| Cultivated meat | Meat grown from animal cells |
| Novel food | Legal category for foods without a history of safe eating; needs approval before sale |
| Blend, hybrid | A product mixing animal and alternative protein |
| *Chay* | Vietnamese vegetarian practice and cooking, and the *đồ chay* products that serve it |
| Soybean meal, fishmeal | The two main protein feeds; context only in this study |
| Frontier | The newer routes: biomass and precision fermentation, gas fermentation, cultivated meat |
| Business-to-business (B2B) ingredient | Protein sold to firms that make food, not to shoppers |
| Import substitution, displacement | Replacing imported ingredients, versus replacing meat on the plate. Counted separately |
| Scenario (S-ALT, D-BENCH) | A labelled future used to test choices, not a forecast. S-ALT is the alternative-protein scenario for 2050 ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]); D-BENCH is the benchmark demand path ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]) |
| Play | A specific business or programme someone could start in Vietnam ([[ch26-plays]]) |

**Units.** Mt is million tonnes, kt thousand tonnes, t tonne. Protein and product are counted separately: "19,000 t of protein" is much more product by weight. Money is in USD unless stated, at 26,000 VND per USD. [[front-how-to-read]] explains the evidence tags. The full glossary, in English and Vietnamese, is [[app-r3-glossary]].
