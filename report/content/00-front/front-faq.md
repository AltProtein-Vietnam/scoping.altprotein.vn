# Frequently asked questions

> **Draft for review.** Version 0.8. Not for citation.

## About this report

**What is this report?**
A scoping study of what alternative protein Vietnam could make for people to eat. It asks from which raw materials, under which rules, at what cost and with whose money. It also asks who would buy it, how things might look in 2035 and 2050, and what each type of actor could do next. Feed appears only as context. AltProtein Vietnam publishes it with its data and models ([[front-cover]]).

**Who is it for?**
Investors, policy makers, startups, food manufacturers, universities and research institutes, and international organisations. Each has a short brief; the list is on the [[front-cover]].

**Why is it a draft, and what is version 1.0?**
This is version 0.8, shared for review. Numbers and wording may still change, so please do not cite it. Version 1.0 will be the public launch. Every change between drafts is recorded in [[app-m5-changelog]].

**Why is it called a supply-side study when it also covers demand?**
The question it started from is what Vietnam can make. It treats demand as a design input for that question: who would buy, at what price and in what form. The report does not plan consumer marketing and gives no market-size forecast ([[front-how-to-read]]).

**How was it researched?**
By AltProtein Vietnam, with AI research assistants working as parallel research streams. Each stream had a written brief, a shared protocol and its own source prefix. The work ran in eleven rounds of desk research; the eleventh tested food-security claims. It added a retail audit of 186 products in Nha Trang and Ho Chi Minh City and a check against the published priorities of 80 named actors. Independent review passes re-checked the headline numbers before each version. We have not yet held interviews or workshops ([[app-m1-method]]).

**How reliable are the numbers?**
Every claim carries a label for where the evidence comes from (Vietnam itself, a comparable country, or global) and how strong it is (High, Medium or Low). Numbers we calculated say so. Where sources disagree, we give both and never average them ([[app-r2-disagreements]]). The weakest numbers are prices nobody publishes, such as delivered soy flour, and costs of plants that do not yet exist in Vietnam ([[ch30-unknowns]]).

**Can I reuse the data?**
The data behind the chapters (194 tables, 2,480 sources and two reproducible models) are part of the package. The licence will be set before version 1.0; until then, please ask AltProtein Vietnam before reusing them.

**How do I send a correction or comment?**
Send it to AltProtein Vietnam through the contact who shared this draft with you. Please give the page, the sentence and your source.

## About alternative protein

**What is alternative protein?**
Protein made from plants, fungi, microbes, algae, insects or cultivated animal cells instead of from livestock and fish. It includes old foods such as tofu and Vietnam's *chay* (vegetarian) cooking, and new ones such as precision-fermented dairy proteins, made by microbes engineered to produce one specific protein. This study looks at alternative protein for people to eat. The prologue explains the families and how each is made ([[front-prologue]], [[front-prologue-vi]]).

**Is it the same as "fake meat" or "lab-grown meat"?**
No. Meat look-alikes are one small part, and cultivated ("lab-grown") meat an even smaller one. In Vietnam most of the opportunity is in ingredients sold to food makers ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]], [[ch26-plays]]).

**Is it safe?**
Foods with a long history of safe use are regulated like any other food. Genuinely new foods need a safety assessment before sale in places such as the European Union and Singapore. Vietnam has no procedure for new foods yet, which is one of the main gaps the report identifies ([[ch07-rules]]).

**Is it genetically modified?**
Most is not. Proteins made by engineered microbes (precision fermentation) probably count as products of a genetically modified organism (GMO) under a January 2026 decree, even when the final protein is purified. The GMO label applies only when such ingredients make up more than 5% of the ingredients ([[ch07-rules]], [[ch27-policy-options]]).

**Is it healthier than meat?**
It depends on the product and what it replaces. Some plant-based meats have less protein, zinc and vitamin B12 than the meat they copy, and many are high in salt; blends keep protein quality high. The prologue sets out both sides ([[front-prologue]]).

**Why does the report not recommend insect protein?**
Insect meal is already made in Vietnam, for animal feed. The report uses it as an incumbent and a benchmark. Recommending insect plays is outside the report's scope, and feed is now context only ([[ch10-technology-fit]]).

**Why does the study no longer recommend feed ingredients?**
Because the study is now about protein for people: what Vietnam could make at home for people to eat, and who would buy it. Feed still matters as context. It is the hidden import behind the meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish that people eat, and so part of the food-security picture. But the study no longer treats feed ingredients (fishmeal replacements, or microbial, insect or duckweed feed) as something to make. Earlier drafts assessed them; that record stays in Appendices S6 and F6 and in the changelog ([[app-s6-feed-market]], [[app-f6-aquafeed-feedstock-futures]], [[app-m5-changelog]]).

## About Vietnam

**Why Vietnam?**
Vietnamese people eat more meat each year. Most of that meat, and the eggs and farmed fish, is produced in Vietnam, but almost all of the plant protein that feeds the animals is imported. Counting that feed, about two thirds of the animal protein people eat rests on imports. Disease shocks such as African swine fever also hit pork supply and prices. That makes protein for people a question of food security, industrial value and trade. The security case is real but narrow: only protein made from domestic inputs reduces import dependence ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).

**Does Vietnam depend on subsidised imported meat?**
No, on the evidence we found. Vietnam does import more meat each year: 978,000 t worth USD 2.0 billion in 2025, mostly poultry by-products, Indian buffalo meat, frozen pork and offal. Still, about 90% of the meat people eat is produced at home; beef, buffalo meat and milk rely most on imports. For most of the meat and milk Vietnam buys, the origin gives no price support, and India taxes its buffalo meat rather than supporting it. Agricultural export subsidies ended under World Trade Organization rules. Where support does exist (EU poultry, Russian pork and, in 2024, Brazilian pigmeat), it works mainly through border protection at home. Imported meat is cheaper because farm costs abroad are lower: Vietnamese pig farm prices in 2024 were 1.6 to 2.8 times those in Brazil, Russia, the United States and the EU ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).

**Would alternative protein lower the risk of pandemics or animal disease?**
We found no evidence that it would. No study we found measures a fall in zoonotic (animal-to-human), pandemic or antimicrobial-resistance risk from replacing meat with alternative protein. The claims that it does come from reviews and opinion pieces, several by advocacy or company authors. Research ties these risks to how animals are farmed and traded: the wildlife trade, live-bird markets, farm biosecurity and antibiotic use. In Vietnam, at the scale demand supports, new protein would spare about 142,000 pigs a year in 2035; swine fever culls in 2025 were 8.4 times that (our calculation). What it can offer is a second source of protein that does not fail when animals fall sick ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).

**What is the best opportunity?**
The study describes seven plays, and three lead under almost every weighting. Two are in the top three under every weighting: domestic textured plant protein to replace Chinese imports (T1), and a cassava-starch-to-fermentation-sugar hub, a cluster of plants on one site (T4), which is first or second under every weighting. The third is shared pilot and tolling fermentation for food (T5), which most other fermentation plays need first; it is in the top three under five of the seven weightings. Tolling means renting time on someone else's fermenter ([[ch26-plays]]).

**Will Vietnamese consumers buy plant-based meat?**
They say yes more often than they buy. In an urban online survey 74% would choose plant-based meat at the same price as meat, but only 3% eat it weekly. Imported analogues cost 12 to 18 times as much per gram of protein as eggs and chicken. Plant milk, with soy milk priced at or below dairy and nut milk at premium-dairy prices, is the one modern plant category at scale ([[ch13-consumers]]).

**How big is the market?**
We give no market forecast. On a benchmark path, the demand routes we describe add up to about 19,000 t of protein a year by 2035. About 11,000 to 17,000 t of that would be made in Vietnam for the home market, mostly replacing imported ingredients. That would displace about 0.2% of the meat protein Vietnam eats ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]).

**Does chay make Vietnam a natural market?**
*Chay* is the incumbent, not a new market. Existing chay days already avoid about 2.5% of meat protein and are part of today's diet. The opening is upgrading chay foods, for example higher-protein products sold under retailers' own labels ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]).

**What about cultivated meat in Vietnam?**
It is a research topic for this decade. Only a few countries have approved it, volumes everywhere are tiny, and modelled costs are several times the retail price of meat. Vietnam has no approval route ([[ch10-technology-fit]], [[ch21-frontier-technology]], [[app-s3-regional]]).

**What should the government do first?**
Three steps that need no new agency; the first two also cost little:

- add a clause on new foods to the Food Safety Law while the draft is revised between the October 2026 first reading and the vote considered in May 2027;
- issue a joint note on the GMO status of purified precision-fermented proteins;
- write a proportionate novel-food procedure into the law's implementing decree.

The larger outlays are a shared food-grade pilot fermentation line and a protein-quality laboratory ([[ch27-policy-options]]).

**Is there money for this?**
Not much yet. One alternative-protein deal in Vietnam has exceeded USD 5 million: Entobel's USD 32.5 million insect-meal plant, which makes feed and so sits outside this study's scope. Agritech venture capital in Vietnam was about USD 1 million in 2025. In our assessment, only 4 of the original 19 demand moves have an outside funder whose rules fit and that works in Vietnam ([[ch08-capital]], [[ch29-actor-check]]).

**Would domestic production make Vietnam self-sufficient?**
Not by itself. A domestic textured-protein plant would still run on imported soy flour, and Chinese and ASEAN protein ingredients enter duty-free, so the plant has to compete on cost. Only protein made from domestic inputs, such as fermentation on cassava or sugar, or rice protein, reduces import dependence ([[ch01-why-vietnam]], [[ch09-economics]], [[ch16-business-buyers]]).

**What happens by 2050?**
On trend, the meat protein Vietnamese people eat rises from about 1.01 Mt in 2025 to about 1.45 Mt in 2050 (our model). The imported soybean meal behind it rises from 7.2 Mt to about 10.4 Mt. At the scale demand supports, new protein adds a second source rather than replacing meat. We describe four possible worlds for 2050 and seven moves that pay off in all of them ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]], [[ch23-scenarios-2050]], [[ch28-robust-moves]]).

**What would change the conclusions?**
For example: a Food Safety Law with no route for new foods, cheap delivered soy flour, an incumbent fermenter offering tolling, or a tested Vietnamese study showing repeat buying of a blend or analogue at a premium over meat. The full list is at the end of the [[front-exec-summary]].

## Using the report

**Where should I start?**
Two minutes: [[front-two-minute]]. Fourteen minutes: [[front-exec-summary]] (Vietnamese: [[front-exec-summary-vi]], 17 minutes). New to the field: [[front-prologue]]. For your role: the six briefs listed on the [[front-cover]].

**What do codes such as T1, PO-001, DMV-05 or S-ALT mean?**
They are plays, policy options, demand moves and scenarios. [[front-how-to-read]] has a table that decodes every code family.

**What changed from earlier drafts?**
Earlier drafts were numbered editions 1.0 to 1.4; they are now versions 0.1 to 0.5. The largest recent change is the scope: the study now concentrates on protein for people, with feed only as context. Every change and correction is listed in [[app-m5-changelog]].
