# Frequently asked questions

## About this report

**What is this report?**
A scoping study of what alternative protein Vietnam could make for people to eat: from which raw materials, under which rules, at what cost and with whose money. It also asks who would buy it, what 2035 and 2050 could look like, and what each type of actor could do next ([[front-cover]]).

**Who is it for?**
Investors, policy makers, startups, food manufacturers, researchers and international organisations. Each has a short brief, listed on the [[front-cover]].

**What is version 1.0?**
The first public version, published in November 2026. It follows ten review drafts. Where a figure replaces one that circulated earlier, a **Correction** box says so.

**Why is it called a supply-side study when it also covers demand?**
It started from what Vietnam can make. Demand is a design input for that question: who would buy, at what price and in what form. The report gives no market-size forecast and is not a marketing plan ([[front-how-to-read]]).

**How was it researched?**
AltProtein Vietnam ran thirteen rounds of desk research, using AI research assistants as parallel research streams under a shared protocol. It added a retail audit of 186 products in Nha Trang and Ho Chi Minh City, and a check against the published priorities of 80 named actors. Independent review passes re-checked the headline numbers. There were no interviews or workshops.

**How reliable are the numbers?**
Every claim says where its evidence comes from (Vietnam, a comparable country, or global) and how strong it is (High, Medium or Low). Numbers we calculated say so. Where sources disagree, we give both and never average them. The weakest numbers are prices nobody publishes, such as delivered soy flour, and costs of plants that do not yet exist ([[ch30-unknowns]]).

**Can I reuse the data?**
The data tables and models behind the chapters are not yet published on this site. Please ask AltProtein Vietnam before reusing them.

**How do I send a correction or comment?**
Write to AltProtein Vietnam. Please give the page, the sentence and your source.

## About alternative protein

**What is alternative protein?**
Protein made from plants, fungi, microbes, algae or cultivated animal cells instead of from livestock and fish. It includes old foods such as tofu and *chay* (vegetarian) cooking, and new ones such as dairy proteins made by engineered microbes ([[front-prologue]]).

**Is it the same as "fake meat" or "lab-grown meat"?**
No. Meat look-alikes are a small part, and cultivated meat an even smaller one. In Vietnam most of the opportunity is in ingredients sold to food makers ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]], [[ch26-plays]]).

**Is it safe?**
Foods with a long history of safe use are regulated like any other food. New foods need a safety assessment before sale in places such as the European Union and Singapore. Vietnam has no procedure for new foods yet, one of the main gaps the report finds ([[ch07-rules]]).

**Is it genetically modified?**
Most is not. Proteins made by engineered microbes (precision fermentation) probably count as products of a genetically modified organism (GMO) under a January 2026 decree. The GMO label applies only above 5% of the ingredients ([[ch07-rules]], [[ch27-policy-options]]).

**Is it healthier than meat?**
It depends on the product and what it replaces. Some plant-based meats have less protein, zinc and vitamin B12 than the meat they copy, and many are salty. Blends keep protein quality high ([[front-prologue]]).

**Why does the study not recommend feed ingredients?**
Because it is about protein for people. Feed matters as context, as the hidden import behind meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish. But the study does not treat feed ingredients as something to make.

## About Vietnam

**Why Vietnam?**
Vietnamese people eat more meat each year. Most of it is produced at home, but almost all of the plant protein that feeds the animals is imported. Counting that feed, about two thirds of the animal protein people eat rests on imports. Only protein made from domestic inputs reduces that ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).

**Does Vietnam depend on subsidised imported meat?**
No, on the evidence we found. Meat imports are growing: 978,000 t worth USD 2.0 billion in 2025. But about 90% of the meat people eat is produced at home. Imported meat is cheaper because farm costs abroad are lower, not because of subsidies. Vietnamese pig farm prices in 2024 were 1.6 to 2.8 times those in Brazil, Russia, the United States and the EU ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).

**Would alternative protein lower the risk of pandemics or animal disease?**
We found no evidence that it would. Research ties these risks to the wildlife trade, live-bird markets, farm biosecurity and antibiotic use. At the scale demand supports, new protein would spare about 142,000 pigs a year in 2035. Swine fever culls in 2025 were 8.4 times that (our calculation). What it offers is a second source of protein that does not fail when animals fall sick ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).

**What is the best opportunity?**
Of seven plays, three lead under almost every weighting ([[ch26-plays]]):

- domestic textured plant protein to replace Chinese imports (T1);
- a hub that turns cassava starch into fermentation sugar (T4);
- shared pilot and tolling fermentation, where firms rent time on someone else's fermenter (T5).

**Will Vietnamese consumers buy plant-based meat?**
They say yes more often than they buy. In an urban online survey, 74% would choose plant-based meat at the same price as meat, but only 3% eat it weekly. Imported meat analogues cost 12 to 18 times as much per gram of protein as eggs and chicken. Plant milk is the one modern plant category at scale ([[ch13-consumers]]).

**How big is the market?**
We give no market forecast. On a benchmark path, the demand routes add up to about 19,000 t of protein a year by 2035. That would displace about 0.2% of the meat protein Vietnam eats ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]).

**Does chay make Vietnam a natural market?**
*Chay* is the incumbent, not a new market. Chay days already avoid about 2.5% of meat protein. The opening is better chay foods, such as higher-protein products sold under store brands ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]).

**What about cultivated meat in Vietnam?**
It is a research topic for this decade. Modelled factory costs are 7 to 15 times the shop price of lean pork or chicken breast (our calculation). Vietnam has no approval route ([[ch10-technology-fit]], [[ch21-frontier-technology]]).

**What should the government do first?**
Three steps that need no new agency ([[ch27-policy-options]]):

- add a clause on new foods to the Food Safety Law before the vote considered for May 2027;
- issue a joint note on the GMO status of purified precision-fermented proteins;
- write a proportionate novel-food procedure into the law's implementing decree.

The larger outlays are a shared food-grade pilot fermentation line and a protein-quality laboratory.

**Is there money for this?**
Not much yet. No disclosed deal in these protein families in Vietnam has exceeded USD 5 million. Agritech venture capital in Vietnam was about USD 1 million in 2025 ([[ch08-capital]], [[ch29-actor-check]]).

**Would domestic production make Vietnam self-sufficient?**
Not by itself. A domestic textured-protein plant would still run on imported soy flour. Only protein made from domestic inputs, such as fermentation on cassava or sugar, or rice protein, reduces import dependence ([[ch01-why-vietnam]], [[ch09-economics]]).

**What happens by 2050?**
On trend, the meat protein Vietnamese people eat rises from about 1.01 Mt in 2025 to about 1.45 Mt in 2050 (our model). New protein adds a second source rather than replacing meat. We describe four possible worlds for 2050, and seven moves that pay off in all of them ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]], [[ch23-scenarios-2050]], [[ch28-robust-moves]]).

**What would change the conclusions?**
For example: a Food Safety Law with no route for new foods, cheap delivered soy flour, an incumbent fermenter offering tolling, or a Vietnamese study showing repeat buying of a blend at a premium over meat. The full list ends the [[front-exec-summary]].
