# Executive summary

> **Draft for review.** Version 0.7. Not for citation.

*New to alternative protein? Read the prologue first: [[front-prologue]]. Short on time? Read [[front-two-minute]].*

**Vietnam's case for alternative protein is a supply case.** Vietnam is one of Asia's largest producers of pork, poultry, eggs, farmed fish and shrimp, and exported USD 11.3 billion of seafood in 2025. Almost all of the plant protein that feeds those animals is imported: about 99% of the soy protein in Vietnamese feed and about three-quarters of the maize supply, for a feed-ingredient import bill of roughly USD 10 billion in 2025. In August 2026 fishmeal reached USD 2,500 a tonne, its highest level this decade. The strongest argument for alternative protein in Vietnam is therefore food and feed security, industrial value and trade, not consumer fashion. Environmental and animal-welfare benefits are real motivations for some actors, but narrower ones ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).

This report scopes the **supply side**: what Vietnam could make, from what, with which equipment, under which rules, at what cost, and who could do it. It also covers the **demand side** as a design input (who would buy new protein, at what price, in what form and through which channel), looks ahead to 2035 and 2050, and checks its conclusions against what 80 named funders, investors, food makers and public bodies need to know. It covers food and feed; cultivated meat is fully scoped; insects appear as the incumbent benchmark, not as a recommendation.

---

## Context and supply: ten findings

1. **The real non-animal protein economy is traditional.** Soy milk, tofu and industrial *đồ chay* (vegetarian food) dominate. In our audit of 186 products in 11 stores, the median label protein of the 43 foods with a label value was 4.7 g per 100 g, and every locally made product with 10 g or more ran on imported textured soy, soy protein or gluten, much of it from China. Modern plant-based meat is nearly absent. The most advanced alternative-protein market is feed: an insect-meal plant sells to a seafood exporter, and Chinese microbial protein is already imported ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]).

2. **Cheap, but not unique, and unready on rules.** A regional study scores Vietnam 100 on labour and 0 on regulatory readiness. Indonesia is as cheap; Thailand is nearly as cheap with better rules and sugar. Labour and power are a small share of fermentation cost, so cheap inputs alone will not bring plants. Vietnam exported more than 2 Mt of cassava starch in 2023 and again in 2025, mostly to China, while China imported only USD 0.16 million of glucose syrup from Vietnam in 2024 ([[ch03-regional]]).

3. **Carbon-rich, protein-poor.** Vietnam grows about 10 Mt of cassava roots and processes about 8 Mt more imported roots, harvests 43.5 Mt of paddy rice and has a sugar surplus. Its protein-rich side streams (brewer's grain, okara) are small next to 7.2 Mt of soybean meal fed each year. The largest residue, cassava pulp, is about 6 Mt wet a year, concentrated in Tay Ninh, but it is low in protein. Cassava roots rose about 50 to 90% in price, depending on province, in the year to April 2026 after a supply shortfall, and the E10 fuel-ethanol mandate, in force from June 2026, adds a new buyer ([[ch04-asset-map]]).

4. **Fermentation skills exist but are captive.** Vedan, Ajinomoto and Daesang run large aerobic fermentation (our model: 11,000 to 34,000 m³ of tanks) for amino acids. We found no published offer of contract manufacturing. We found no open pilot fermenter, no protein-isolate plant and no domestic textured-protein extruder. Feed mills (269, about half used) and frozen-food co-packers are ready partners ([[ch05-industrial-base]]).

5. **Research sits next to alternative protein, not inside it.** Vietnam is strong in aquafeed trials, yeast protein expression, stem-cell culture, koji genetics and microalgae, and near zero in texturisation, mycoprotein, precision fermentation and food cell lines. Its novel-food research intensity is about a quarter to a third of Thailand's. The binding gap is shared infrastructure ([[ch06-knowledge-talent]]).

6. **Food rules have an open door but no gate.** New protein foods are sold through self-declaration, but the law also treats unpermitted "new-technology food" as recallable, and there is no procedure to obtain permission. Precision-fermented proteins probably count as GMO products under a January 2026 decree. Feed is more open: insects, duckweed, algae and yeasts are already on the livestock feed list, though the aquafeed list is unclear. A new Food Safety Law goes to the National Assembly for first comments in October 2026, with a vote considered in May 2027: a one-off window between those two sessions ([[ch07-rules]]).

7. **Capital is thin.** One alternative-protein deal has exceeded USD 5 million: Entobel's USD 32.5 million insect-meal plant, financed by local private equity, a development-finance ticket and a seafood exporter's offtake. Agritech venture capital in Vietnam was about USD 1 million in 2025. New state venture funds exist but write small tickets ([[ch08-capital]]).

8. **Capital, not operating cost, decides fermentation economics.** On indicative numbers, fungal feed protein made in Vietnam costs USD 4,050 to 14,700 per tonne of protein, against USD 2,625 to 3,846 for fishmeal protein and about USD 880 for soybean meal. It is marginal as bulk fishmeal replacement, plausible as a functional aquafeed ingredient at 1 to 5% of the diet, and not viable against soy. Textured plant protein costs a small fraction of the retail price of meat protein to make, and Chinese extrusion lines are cheap; but Chinese product enters duty-free at about USD 1.29 to 1.38 per kg, so the delivered price of food-grade soy flour decides whether a Vietnamese line pays ([[ch09-economics]]).

9. **Two technology families fit now.** Textured plant protein, and functional microbial ingredients for shrimp and fish feed. Local legume and rice proteins, fungal foods and duckweed feed are worth proving. Precision fermentation fits only as high-value, low-dose products made under contract. Cultivated meat and seafood are research topics for this decade ([[ch10-technology-fit]]).

10. **The calendar is front-loaded.** Almost every relevant rule change lands in 2025 to 2027, so the direction of the next decade will be visible within about 18 months ([[ch07-rules]], [[ch19-outlook-2035]]).

## Demand: who would buy it

Part III ([[ch11-protein-diet]] to [[ch18-demand-sizing]]) asks what demand exists that would pull new protein, what products must be to win it, who buys first and how large it could be. Every demand claim carries a second badge saying whether it is stated (surveys), revealed (sales, prices, trade), tested (tastings, auctions, trials) or inferred; stated evidence usually overstates behaviour.

1. **A high-meat diet that is still rising.** Meat intake was 136.4 g per person per day in 2020 against a recommendation of 50 to 80 g; animal foods supply 42% of protein; meat per person is projected to rise 25% by 2035, led by poultry. Adults already eat enough protein ([[ch11-protein-diet]]).
2. **Cheap protein sets the bar.** Eggs and chicken cost VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein and lean pork about VND 71,000; imported plant-based analogues cost 12 to 18 times eggs and chicken per gram of protein. When pork prices jumped 70% after African swine fever, households switched to chicken, not tofu.
3. **Chay is the incumbent, and the wrong impact count.** Chay sales roughly double in specialist channels in the seventh lunar month (one shop reported three times); existing chay days already avoid an estimated 2.5% of meat protein, as much as Part IV's alternative-protein scenario assumes for 2030 to 2035, but they are already in the meat baseline ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]).
4. **People say yes and buy little.** In an urban online survey 74% would choose plant-based meat at the same price as meat, but only 15% intend to eat less meat and 3% eat it weekly. Imported analogues came and went through gourmet channels; plant milk from local incumbents, with soy milk priced at or below dairy and nut milk at premium-dairy prices, is the one modern plant category at scale ([[ch13-consumers]]).
5. **Blends are the entry route for the frontier, and soy is the price to beat.** Cultivated meat has sold tens of thousands of servings in Singapore in almost six years; a GMO label carries a large price penalty in Vietnam (an auction found an 84% premium for soymilk labelled non-GM); one large processor already lists soy protein in 31% of its meat formulations. Soy extension saved money at every annual hog price from 2019 to 2024, so a new extender must beat hydrated soy, not meat, and be named as an ingredient, never as *độn* (filler) ([[ch14-frontier-demand]]).
6. **Kitchens and factories before shelves.** Traditional markets take 83% of food retail and about 80% of fresh meat. Factory canteens serve up to 1.2 billion meals a year at VND 20,000 to 35,000, where only an ingredient fits the protein budget. Schools already reserve plant-protein days, at least two a week (Decision 3958/QĐ-BYT, 2025), but tofu fills them beside meat; only a labelled blend in minced dishes would remove meat there. Retailers' own labels are a route to shelf for upgraded chay. Tourist hotels are a showcase, not a volume channel ([[ch15-channels]]).
7. **The clearest revealed demand is business-to-business.** Plant proteins shipped from China to Vietnam nearly tripled from 2019 to 2025, to about 54,000 t, at about USD 0.9 to 1.3 per kg (mostly HS 3504, much of it priced like feed; textured protein rose 54%); chay makers are the most switchable buyers; Vinamilk buys pea protein for a soy-free high-protein milk. These imports pay no duty under ACFTA and RCEP, so a domestic maker has no tariff shelter ([[ch16-business-buyers]]).
8. **Export: a tariff edge, falling analogue demand, and a feed pull.** Vietnam enters the EU, the UK and Japan duty-free on lines where Thai and Chinese goods pay 6 to 13%, but finished plant-based meat demand is falling in the US (where Vietnamese goods now pay an extra 12.5%) and flat in Europe. The export base is wrapped foods (USD 128 million of dumplings and similar in 2023); in Europe it sells through importers and brand owners. Aquafeed standards do not require microbial protein, and only BAP gives any credit (for algae and other aquatic microorganisms); the feed pull is domestic, in about 9,200 t of inactive yeast imported for food and feed, 95% of it at about USD 1.1 per kg ([[ch17-export-demand]]).
9. **How much.** On a benchmark path, these routes deliver about 19,000 t of protein a year by 2035, of which about 11,000 to 17,000 t would be made in Vietnam for the home market, mostly replacing imported ingredients, and displace about 0.2% of meat protein; even a stretch path displaces 0.7% in 2035, against 3% in the alternative-protein scenario of the 2050 balance model (S-ALT), whose food side is exploratory ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]).

## Futures: 2035 and 2050

Part IV ([[ch19-outlook-2035]] to [[ch24-vision-2050]]) looks to 2035 and 2050. It separates what is near-certain from what is open, labels every forward-looking claim by type (trend, published projection or official target, our estimate, signal, wildcard or vision) and never presents a scenario as a forecast.

1. **The pressures point to more imported protein and more compound feed.** The working-age population peaks around 2035 to 2040, smallholder pig farms keep disappearing, African swine fever stays endemic, agriculture must cut its emissions to 56 MtCO2e by 2050, and the Mekong Delta keeps sinking by about 1.1 cm a year. No official meat, feed or protein number reaches beyond 2030 ([[ch20-drivers-2050]]).
2. **Soybean-meal need rises from 7.2 Mt (2025) to about 10.4 Mt (2050) on trend,** nearly all imported; across four scenarios the 2050 range is 7.0 to 11.7 Mt (our model). Vietnam already uses about 4 million hectares of farmland abroad for its feed ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]).
3. **Efficiency does twice the work of alternative protein.** Better feed conversion and lower soybean-meal inclusion cut 2050 need by 3.4 Mt; a large alternative-protein build-out cuts it by 1.6 Mt. The two work through different channels and do not compete, though we did not model them combined. Aquafeed is short of omega-3 more than of protein.
4. **Frontier technology works, but costs fall slowly.** Fermentation industries have cut costs by 13 to 20% per doubling of output, far slower than solar power. Protein from renewable power made in Vietnam could reach the price of fishmeal protein around 2050 at best, and soybean meal's in no year; its window opens not before about 2040 to 2045, and only if five conditions align ([[ch21-frontier-technology]]).
5. **Two external questions decide most:** whether imported feed protein comes under chronic stress, and whether Asia's fermentation market stays open or falls under Chinese price dominance. Crossing them gives four worlds for 2050: Regional workshop, Comfortable price-taker, Security build-out and Squeezed importer ([[ch23-scenarios-2050]]).
> **Vision, not forecast.** Our proposed Vietnam 2050 vision is a protein system that is measured, efficient, diversified, specialised and low-carbon: 7 to 9 Mt of imported soybean meal instead of 10.4 Mt; 4 to 8% of high-protein feed protein from microbes; 15 to 50% of aquafeed omega-3 from non-marine oils; and a review at every five-year plan ([[ch24-vision-2050]]).

## What to do

**Ten plays; three lead.** We describe ten concrete plays with what must be true, kill tests, first customers and horizons, and score them with weights each audience can change ([[ch26-plays]]). Three lead under almost every weighting:

- **T1. Domestic textured plant protein** for *chay* brands and frozen-food makers, replacing imports from China (0 to 2 years); best owned by a chay maker, co-packer or distributor and financed with debt, not venture equity.
- **T2. Functional microbial feed ingredients** for shrimp and pangasius, sold on survival and feed efficiency (1 to 3 years).
- **T4. A cassava-starch-to-fermentation-sugar hub** next to starch factories, to keep a value-adding step in Vietnam (1 to 3 years; needs a committed fermenter).

**Shared pilot and tolling fermentation (T5)** is what most fermentation plays need first.

**Cheap public steps.** The four highest-ranked policy options cost little and need no new agency ([[ch27-policy-options]]):

1. A one-line clause in the new Food Safety Law letting the Government regulate "new foods and new food ingredients" (first comments October 2026; vote considered May 2027).
2. A joint note from the agriculture and health ministries on the GMO status of purified precision-fermented proteins.
3. A raw-material section in the aquafeed list.
4. Generic entries for microbial biomass in the livestock feed list.

The larger outlays are a shared pilot fermentation line, public feed trials and a protein-quality laboratory.

**Product targets and demand moves.** Eleven target product profiles, a demand check that strengthens domestic textured protein (T1) and plant-drink proteins (T10), and twenty-one cheap moves, each with who would pay; most of the label rules first proposed are already law and need enforcement ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).

**Nine no-regret moves pay off in all four worlds,** most of them public, cheap and able to start in 2026 to 2028: a national protein and feed balance; a feed-efficiency programme; new-food and new-feed routes with deadlines; shared pilot and trial capacity; a quality laboratory and price series; residue-carbon rules; an omega-3 plan; climate-proof siting; and process-engineering skills ([[ch28-robust-moves]]).

## Checked against the actors

We named 80 real actors who could move alternative protein in Vietnam, turned their stated priorities into 118 decision questions and checked Part III against them: it answered one in full and 71 in part. Sixteen follow-up research lines changed these points ([[ch29-actor-check]], [[app-m4-actor-check-waves]]):

- **Schools:** national guidance already asks for plant protein at least twice a week, and schools fill it with tofu; the menu software still prints an animal-protein floor of 48%.
- **Domestic textured protein:** equipment is cheap (Chinese lines from USD 11,000 to 100,000), there is no tariff shelter, and soy-flour price decides the margin; the benchmark path supports about one line by 2035.
- **Blends:** soy extension paid at every annual hog price from 2019 to 2024; buyers punish hidden filler, not a declared ingredient.
- **Rules:** a high-protein claim needs 10 g per 100 g; vegetarian claims, percentage labels and the GMO threshold are already set; a sugar tax from 2027 may reach sweetened plant milks while dairy is exempt.
- **Funders:** in our assessment, only 4 of the original 19 demand moves have a funder whose rules fit and that accepts Vietnam; the benchmark path spares about 2.4 to 2.7 million animals a year by 2035, nearly all poultry, and import substitution spares none.
- **Children:** stunting among children under five is 18% (32% among ethnic minority children), a problem of the youngest children; school lunches, including in upland boarding schools, already carry enough protein.
- **Firms:** of 24 alternative-protein firms in Vietnam and the region since 2019 in our register, 7 are active, 4 pivoted (three of them towards private label, co-manufacturing or non-food), 2 exited and 11 are dormant (no news found); none of the 4 Vietnam-based meat-analogue makers is active.

## For each reader

- **Investors:** back B2B feed and ingredient revenue with a named strategic buyer, on the Entobel template; underwrite capital intensity, not consumer growth ([[brief-investors]]). On demand, size ingredient businesses on the imports they can replace, not on consumer forecasts; treat domestic textured protein as debt-financed import substitution with a soy-flour price test, and price extenders against soy.
- **Policy makers:** act on the Food Safety Law window (first comments October 2026, vote considered May 2027) and the two feed-list fixes; favour capital sharing over tax holidays ([[brief-policy]]). Put protein quality, zinc and iron, not an animal share, into school menu tools, and rule on plant milks under the sugar tax before 2027.
- **Startups:** start where the route is already clear (textured protein, listed feed materials, koji) and sell function or import substitution ([[brief-startups]]). Sell a familiar food with little capital and aim first for ingredient, private-label or co-packing supply to incumbents; a menu listing for your own analogue brand is not a business customer.
- **Manufacturers:** you are the first customer for textured protein, functional feed additives and local drink proteins, and the natural owner of a textured-protein line; a joint trial is the cheapest start ([[brief-manufacturers]]). Higher-protein chay through private label, extenders tested against your own soy recipe, and plant milks at or below 5 g sugar per 100 ml meet the demand that exists.
- **Research bodies:** protein-quality data (including phytate and zinc in tofu, textured soy and blends), duckweed safety, soy-wastewater protein in shrimp, thermotolerant strains on cassava and characterised *tương* strains are the most useful gaps; an equal-saving blind test of a fungal extender against soy would settle its job ([[brief-research]]).
- **International bodies:** support the novel-food drafting, public feed trials, a pilot line and development-finance structures for first plants ([[brief-international]]). Match demand-side funding to your unit and rules: few diet-change funds offer a Vietnam route (the 2026 regional fund published no Vietnam application guide, and the leading animal charity evaluator's grants now exclude diet change), and climate is a co-benefit, not the case.

## What would change our view

- The Food Safety Law passes without a new-food route, or with one that has no timelines.
- Fishmeal falls back below about USD 1,800 a tonne for a sustained period.
- Chinese microbial protein or textured soy lands in Vietnam below the cost of Vietnamese pilot-scale production.
- An incumbent fermenter offers tolling, which would sharply improve the economics of functional feed ingredients, bulk microbial protein and shared pilot fermentation (plays T2, T3 and T5).
- A tested Vietnamese study showing repeat purchase of a modern analogue or blend at a premium over meat, or a probability survey showing chay keeping rising among young urban people.
- Delivered food-grade soy flour quoted below about USD 700 per tonne, or three chay makers signing conditional orders for a domestic textured protein.
- A written ruling that sweetened plant milks fall outside the sugar tax, or menu software that drops its animal-protein floor.

The twenty unknowns that matter most, and who can answer each, are in [[ch30-unknowns]].

It rests on ten rounds of desk research (2,384 sources), a retail audit in Nha Trang and Ho Chi Minh City, a reproducible protein and feed balance model, a reproducible demand model and a check against 80 named actors, with every headline number cross-checked against its source.
