# Executive summary

> **Draft for review.** Version 0.8. Not for citation.

*New to alternative protein? Read the prologue first: [[front-prologue]]. Short on time? Read [[front-two-minute]].*

**Protein for people first; feed only as context.** This study asks what Vietnam could make at home for people to eat, at what cost and under which rules, and who would buy it, now and to 2050. It treats demand as a design input, not a marketing plan. Feed appears only as context: it is the hidden import behind the meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish that people eat. Earlier drafts also assessed feed ingredients as products to make; that record stays in Appendices S6 and F6 and in the changelog ([[app-s6-feed-market]], [[app-f6-aquafeed-feedstock-futures]], [[app-m5-changelog]]). Insect protein is a benchmark, not a recommendation.

**Why Vietnam.** Vietnamese people eat more meat each year. Meat intake was 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, against a recommendation of 50 to 80 g, and animal foods supply 42% of protein. Most of that meat (about 90% in 2023), and the eggs and farmed fish, is produced in Vietnam on imported feed. About 99% of the soy protein in feed and about 74% of the maize supply were imported in 2025, for a feed-ingredient import bill of about USD 10 billion.

Disease shocks such as African swine fever hit pork supply and prices. So the case for making more protein for people at home is the security of that supply, industrial value and trade; environment and animal welfare are motivations for some actors ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]). The security case is real but narrow, as the next section shows.

## Food security: what the evidence supports, and what it does not

We tested the main food-security claims made for alternative protein in Vietnam ([[ch01-why-vietnam]], [[ch11-protein-diet]], [[ch20-drivers-2050]]).

- **Two thirds of animal protein rests on imports.** In 2023, 19% to 23% of the protein supply was imported food. Counting the feed behind domestic meat, eggs and farmed fish, 61% to 71% of animal protein rested on imports (our calculation). Soybeans for tofu and soy milk are 97.5% imported, and milk is about 30% self-sufficient on the measure of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO).
- **Imported meat is growing, but not subsidised.** Meat imports reached 978,000 t, worth USD 2.0 billion, in 2025. The evidence does not support the claim that Vietnam relies on subsidised imported meat: for most of what Vietnam buys, the origin gives no price support, and farm costs abroad are lower. New protein would displace at most about 1.6% of imported meat protein by 2035 (our calculation).
- **Disease is the shock consumers feel.** Consumer pork prices rose 57.23% in 2020 after African swine fever, and people switched to chicken and imported pork, not tofu. The agriculture ministry says the licensed vaccines do not protect against the recombinant strains now dominant in the north.
- **We found no evidence that alternative protein lowers disease risk.** No study we found measures lower zoonotic (animal-to-human), pandemic or antimicrobial-resistance risk from replacing meat. The risk depends on how animals are farmed and traded. At the scale demand supports, new protein would not change the exposure: swine fever culls in 2025 were 8.4 times the pigs it would spare in 2035 (our calculation).
- **Only domestic inputs reduce dependence.** Alternative protein made from imported soy, pea or wheat gluten moves the dependence: about 84% of the food plant-protein ingredients reaching Vietnam in 2025 came from China (our calculation). Fermentation on cassava or sugar, rice and peanut protein, and soy foods from Vietnamese beans reduce it.
- **Wild fish is under most pressure.** The marine fish stock in 2016 to 2020 was 22.1% below 2000 to 2005. Alternative protein does not touch this at the scale demand supports.
- **Official food security counts volumes, not imports.** The food-security resolution to 2030 (Resolution 34/NQ-CP) has no import or protein indicator. A national protein balance, by food, source and origin, is the first move; chapter 1 builds a first version from FAO data ([[ch28-robust-moves]]).

## Context and supply: ten findings

1. **The real non-animal protein economy is traditional.** Soy milk, tofu and industrial *đồ chay* (traditional vegetarian food) dominate; modern plant-based meat is nearly absent. In our audit of 186 products, the median label protein of the 43 foods with a label value was 4.7 g per 100 g. Every locally made product with 10 g or more ran on imported textured soy, soy protein or gluten, much of it from China ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]).

2. **Cheap, but not unique, and not ready on rules.** A regional study scores Vietnam 100 on labour and 0 on regulatory readiness, and labour and power are a small share of fermentation cost. Vietnam exported more than 2 Mt of cassava starch in 2023 and again in 2025, mostly to China. Yet China imported only USD 0.16 million of glucose syrup from Vietnam in 2024 ([[ch03-regional]]).

3. **Carbon-rich, protein-poor.** Vietnam grows about 10 Mt of cassava roots, harvests 43.5 Mt of paddy rice and has a sugar surplus. Its protein-rich side streams, such as okara (soy pulp), are small. The largest residue, cassava pulp (about 6 Mt wet a year), is low in protein. Cassava roots rose about 50 to 90% in price in the year to April 2026, and a fuel-ethanol mandate from June 2026 adds a new buyer ([[ch04-asset-map]]).

4. **Fermentation skills exist but are captive.** Three amino-acid makers (Vedan, Ajinomoto and Daesang) run almost all large aerobic (air-fed) fermentation: 11,000 to 34,000 m³ of tanks (our model). We found no published offer of contract manufacturing, no open pilot fermenter, no protein-isolate plant and no domestic textured-protein extruder. Frozen-food makers are ready co-packers (firms that make products for other brands) ([[ch05-industrial-base]]).

5. **Research sits next to alternative protein, not inside it.** Vietnam is strong in yeast protein expression, stem-cell culture, the genetics of koji (a mould used to ferment soy and rice) and microalgae. It is near zero in texturisation, mycoprotein (fungal protein), precision fermentation (microbes engineered to make one specific protein) and food cell lines. The main gap is shared infrastructure ([[ch06-knowledge-talent]]).

6. **Food rules have an open door but no gate.** New protein foods are sold through self-declaration. But the law also treats unpermitted "new-technology food" as recallable, and there is no procedure to get permission. Precision-fermented proteins probably count as products of a genetically modified organism (GMO) under a January 2026 decree. The new Food Safety Law, with first comments in October 2026 and a vote considered in May 2027, is a one-off window ([[ch07-rules]]).

7. **Capital is thin.** Only one alternative-protein deal has exceeded USD 5 million: Entobel's USD 32.5 million insect-meal plant. It is an insect-feed deal, outside this study's scope, but it shows a deal structure: a buyer's offtake (an agreement to buy the output), local private equity and a development-finance ticket. Agritech venture capital in Vietnam was about USD 1 million in 2025 ([[ch08-capital]]).

8. **Capital, not operating cost, decides fermentation economics.** On indicative numbers, fungal biomass protein made in Vietnam costs USD 4,050 to 14,700 per tonne of protein, 54 to 67% of it capital charges and maintenance. It costs more than the commodity plant proteins food makers import, so it fits as a functional food ingredient, not a bulk one. Textured plant protein is cheap to make and to equip. But Chinese product enters duty-free at about USD 1.29 to 1.38 per kg, so the delivered price of food-grade soy flour decides whether a Vietnamese line pays ([[ch09-economics]]).

9. **One technology family fits now: textured plant protein for food.** Local legume and rice proteins and fungal foods such as koji are worth proving. Precision fermentation fits only as high-value, low-dose products made under contract. Cultivated meat and seafood are research topics for this decade ([[ch10-technology-fit]]).

10. **The calendar is front-loaded.** Almost every relevant rule change lands in 2025 to 2027, so the direction of the next decade will be visible within about 18 months ([[ch07-rules]], [[ch19-outlook-2035]]).

## Demand: who would buy it

Part III ([[ch11-protein-diet]] to [[ch18-demand-sizing]]) asks who would buy new protein, in what form and how much. Each demand claim carries a badge (stated, revealed, tested or inferred); stated evidence from surveys usually overstates behaviour.

1. **A high-meat diet that is still rising.** Meat per person is projected to rise 25% by 2035, led by poultry, and adults already eat enough protein ([[ch11-protein-diet]]).
2. **Cheap protein sets the bar.** Eggs and chicken cost VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein; imported plant-based analogues cost 12 to 18 times as much per gram of protein. When pig producer prices jumped 70% after African swine fever, households switched to chicken, not tofu.
3. **Chay is the incumbent.** Existing *chay* (vegetarian) days already avoid an estimated 2.5% of meat protein, as much as Part IV's alternative-protein scenario assumes for 2030 to 2035. But that saving is already in the meat baseline ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]).
4. **People say yes and buy little.** In an urban online survey, 74% would choose plant-based meat at the same price as meat. But only 15% intend to eat less meat, and 3% eat plant-based meat weekly. Plant milk from local incumbents is the one modern plant category at scale ([[ch13-consumers]]).
5. **Blends are the entry route, and soy is the price to beat.** Soy extension (mixing soy into meat products) saved money at every annual hog price from 2019 to 2024. So a new extender has to beat hydrated soy, not meat, and be named as an ingredient, never as *độn* (filler). A GMO label carries a large price penalty in Vietnam ([[ch14-frontier-demand]]).
6. **Kitchens and factories before shelves.** Traditional markets take 83% of food retail. Factory canteens serve up to 1.2 billion meals a year, where only an ingredient fits the protein budget. Schools already reserve at least two plant-protein days a week, but tofu fills them beside meat. Retailers' own labels are a route to shelf for upgraded chay ([[ch15-channels]]).
7. **The clearest revealed demand is business to business.** Plant proteins shipped from China to Vietnam nearly tripled from 2019 to 2025, to about 54,000 t, much of it priced like feed. Chay makers are the most switchable buyers. These imports pay no duty under the ASEAN-China and Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership trade agreements, so a domestic maker has no tariff shelter ([[ch16-business-buyers]]).
8. **Export: a tariff edge, but falling demand for analogues.** Vietnam enters the EU, the UK and Japan duty-free on lines where Thai and Chinese goods pay 6 to 13%. But demand for finished plant-based meat is falling in the US and flat in Europe, and the export base is wrapped foods such as dumplings ([[ch17-export-demand]]).
9. **How much.** On a benchmark path, these routes provide about 19,000 t of protein a year by 2035. About 11,000 to 17,000 t of it would be made in Vietnam, mostly replacing imported ingredients, and it would displace about 0.2% of meat protein. Even a stretch path displaces 0.7% in 2035, against 3% in the alternative-protein scenario of the 2050 balance model (S-ALT). The food side of S-ALT is exploratory ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]).

## Futures: 2035 and 2050

Part IV ([[ch19-outlook-2035]] to [[ch24-vision-2050]]) never presents a scenario as a forecast.

1. **The pressures point to more imported protein.** The working-age population peaks around 2035 to 2040, and African swine fever stays endemic. Agriculture has to cut its emissions to 56 MtCO2e (million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent) by 2050, and the Mekong Delta keeps sinking by about 1.1 cm a year. No official meat, feed or protein number reaches beyond 2030 ([[ch20-drivers-2050]]).
2. **People will eat more meat, and the imports behind it will grow.** On trend, the meat protein Vietnamese people eat rises from about 1.01 Mt in 2025 to about 1.45 Mt in 2050 (our model). The soybean meal behind it, nearly all imported, rises from 7.2 Mt to about 10.4 Mt; across four scenarios the 2050 range is 7.0 to 11.7 Mt ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]).
3. **Protein for people saves the most imports per tonne.** Replacing 1 t of meat protein in diets avoids about 4.5 t of soybean-meal and 9.5 t of maize imports (our calculation). But at the scale demand supports (benchmark path), new protein displaces about 0.4% of meat protein in 2050: it adds a second source rather than replacing meat. Better feed conversion and formulation would cut 2050 soybean-meal need by about 3.4 Mt, about twice what S-ALT would; that lever sits outside this study's scope.
4. **Frontier technology works, but costs fall slowly.** Fermentation industries have cut costs by 13 to 20% per doubling of output, far slower than solar power. On our estimates, protein from renewable power made in Vietnam would still cost USD 3.3 to 6.5 per kg of protein in 2050. For food, an earlier and narrower window is formulating imported, already-approved ingredients of this kind from about 2028, if a new-food route exists ([[ch21-frontier-technology]]).
5. **Two external questions decide most.** Will imported protein (the feed behind domestic meat, and imported protein foods) come under chronic stress? Will Asia's fermentation market stay open or fall under Chinese price dominance? Crossing them gives four worlds for 2050: Regional workshop, Comfortable price-taker, Security build-out and Squeezed importer ([[ch23-scenarios-2050]]).

> **Vision, not forecast.** Our proposed vision is that by 2050 Vietnam feeds a larger, richer and older population from a protein supply that is measured, more diverse and more secure. New routes include upgraded chay, declared blends, canteen dishes, plant drinks and fermented foods. They provide about 67,000 to 172,000 t of protein a year, of which about 59,000 to 147,000 t is made in Vietnam (the benchmark and stretch paths of the demand model). Meat stays the main protein; new protein adds a second source. The vision is reviewed at every five-year plan ([[ch24-vision-2050]]).

## What to do

**Seven plays; three lead under almost every weighting.** A play is a business or programme that someone could start, with a named product, buyer and partner; readers can change the scoring weights ([[ch26-plays]]). The three that lead:

- **T1. Domestic textured plant protein** for chay brands and frozen-food makers, replacing imports from China (0 to 2 years). It is best owned by a chay maker, co-packer or distributor and financed with debt, not venture equity.
- **T4. A cassava-starch-to-fermentation-sugar hub** (a cluster of plants on one site) next to starch factories, so a food-fermentation step stays in Vietnam (1 to 3 years; needs a committed fermenter).
- **T5. Shared pilot and tolling fermentation** for food, which most fermentation plays need first (2 to 4 years). Tolling means renting time on someone else's fermenter.

Koji and fungal foods (T9) and mung bean and rice protein for plant drinks (T10) are worth proving. Precision fermentation fits only as contract-made, high-value ingredients (T7), and cultivated seafood is research for this decade (T8). Earlier drafts also assessed three feed plays; they are retired. Four public goods would lower the cost of every play: a proportionate new-food procedure, a protein-quality laboratory, a by-product atlas, and an open cost model and price series.

**First public steps.** The three highest-ranked policy options need no new agency. The first two cost little; the third takes more drafting ([[ch27-policy-options]]):

1. A one-line clause in the new Food Safety Law letting the Government regulate "new foods and new food ingredients" (first comments October 2026; vote considered May 2027).
2. A joint note from the agriculture and health ministries on the GMO status of purified precision-fermented proteins.
3. A proportionate novel-food procedure in the law's implementing decree.

The larger outlays are a shared food-grade pilot fermentation line and a protein-quality laboratory.

**Product targets and demand moves.** Ten target product profiles say what a product has to be for the demand that exists, and twenty cheap demand moves each name who would pay. Most of the label rules first proposed are already law and need enforcement ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).

**Seven no-regret moves pay off in all four worlds.** Most are public, cheap and able to start in 2026 to 2028 ([[ch28-robust-moves]]):

- a national protein balance;
- a new-food route with deadlines;
- shared food-grade pilot capacity;
- a protein-quality laboratory and price series;
- residue-carbon rules and a by-product atlas;
- climate-proof siting;
- process-engineering skills.

## Checked against the actors

We turned the stated priorities of 80 named actors into 118 decision questions. Part III answered one in full and 71 in part, and sixteen follow-up research lines then changed these points ([[ch29-actor-check]], [[app-m4-actor-check-waves]]):

- **Schools** fill their plant-protein days with tofu, and the menu software still prints an animal-protein floor of 48%.
- **Domestic textured protein:** equipment is cheap (Chinese lines from USD 11,000 to 100,000), and the soy-flour price decides the margin. The benchmark path supports about one line by 2035.
- **Blends and rules:** buyers punish hidden filler, not a declared ingredient. A sugar tax from 2027 may reach sweetened plant milks while dairy is exempt.
- **Funders:** in our assessment, only 4 of the original 19 demand moves have a funder whose rules fit and that accepts Vietnam.
- **Children:** stunting among children under five is 18%, but school lunches already carry enough protein.
- **Firms:** none of the 4 Vietnam-based meat-analogue makers in our register is active.

## For each reader

- **Investors:** size ingredient businesses on the imports they can replace, not on consumer forecasts. Treat domestic textured protein as debt-financed import substitution with a soy-flour price test. If a thesis rests on food security, check that the inputs are domestic ([[brief-investors]]).
- **Policy makers:** act on the Food Safety Law window and favour capital sharing over tax holidays. Put protein quality, zinc and iron, not an animal share, into school menu tools. Publish a national protein balance that shows where the protein people eat comes from ([[brief-policy]]).
- **Startups:** start where the route is already clear (textured protein, koji), and aim first for ingredient, private-label or co-packing supply to incumbents ([[brief-startups]]).
- **Manufacturers:** you are the first customer for textured protein and local drink proteins, and the natural owner of a textured-protein line. Higher-protein chay and extenders tested against your own soy recipe meet the demand that exists ([[brief-manufacturers]]).
- **Research bodies:** the most useful gaps are protein-quality data (including phytate and zinc in tofu, textured soy and blends), duckweed safety, heat-tolerant strains on cassava and characterised strains of *tương* (fermented soybean paste). For food security, the gaps are the domestic share of soybeans in tofu and soy milk, and a Vietnamese food balance ([[brief-research]]).
- **International bodies:** support the novel-food drafting, a food-grade pilot line and development-finance structures for first plants. Climate is a co-benefit, not the case. Do not fund alternative protein as pandemic prevention: the levers for that are surveillance, biosecurity and control of the wildlife trade ([[brief-international]]).

## What would change our view

- The Food Safety Law passes without a new-food route, or with one that has no timelines.
- Chinese textured soy or microbial food protein lands in Vietnam below the cost of Vietnamese pilot-scale production.
- An incumbent fermenter offers tolling. That would sharply improve the economics of shared pilot fermentation (T5) and of the food-fermentation plays that need it.
- A tested Vietnamese study shows repeat buying of a modern analogue or blend at a premium over meat, or a survey with a random sample shows chay keeping rising among young urban people.
- Delivered food-grade soy flour is quoted below about USD 700 per tonne, or three chay makers sign conditional orders for a domestic textured protein.
- A written ruling puts sweetened plant milks outside the sugar tax, or menu software drops its animal-protein floor.
- A study measures lower zoonotic or antimicrobial-resistance risk from replacing meat with alternative protein. We found none.

The eighteen unknowns that matter most, four of them on food security, and who can answer each, are in [[ch30-unknowns]].
