# S11. Capital: deals, investors and funding programmes

This appendix sets out who has paid for alternative protein in Vietnam, and what money is open to it now. It covers:

- the deals that have happened, and the investors with a relevant track record;
- the size of the local venture capital (VC) and private equity (PE) markets;
- development finance institutions (DFIs), the public banks and funds that invest in developing countries;
- the state venture framework created in 2025 and 2026, state credit and guarantees, grants and philanthropy, and accelerators;
- exit routes, meaning the ways investors sell their stake;
- bank instruments that can pay for a small processing line: finance leasing, factoring and supply-chain finance (section S11.14).

It is about money for supply: food-ingredient and processing plants, pilot facilities and research. The study concentrates on protein for people. Feed and agribusiness deals are kept as context.

> **Method note.** Our capital research reached its web-search limit early. After that we could read only pages we already knew. So the coverage is incomplete for DFIs other than IFC (the International Finance Corporation), Proparco and ADB Ventures (of the Asian Development Bank); regional corporate venture arms; Vietnamese food mergers and acquisitions (M&A); aquaculture-tech and feed-tech deals; and Vietnam venture totals for the first half (H1) of 2026. If a deal is missing here, we did not find it; that does not mean it did not happen.

Currency. Amounts are given as reported. Where we convert VND to USD, we use VND 26,000 per USD (our calculation). The capital research used about VND 25,380 per USD, implied by a March 2026 press report [@CAP-32] {VN-direct|Medium}, so some source USD figures differ slightly from ours.

## S11.1 Main findings

1. **Disclosed money into Vietnam-based alternative protein is small.** No disclosed deal in Vietnam that we found from 2015 to September 2026 in the protein families this study covers has exceeded USD 5 million (our reading of the deal log in section S11.2). The disclosed deals are small or undisclosed (findings 2 and 3), so there is no proven local financing template.
2. **We found no disclosed venture round for any Vietnam-based plant-based meat, plant-based dairy, precision fermentation, microalgae or cultivated-meat company.** The only plant-protein fundraising found is Emmay's: about USD 250,000 from three angel rounds, plus two US grants of unstated size, by 2021 (company claim) [@CAP-15] {VN-direct|Low}.
3. **Vietnamese strategic money has gone outward.** Vinh Hoan invested in Shiok Meats and Avant Meats (cultivated seafood, 2021); VinaCapital Ventures invested in Korea's INNOHAS (plant-based meat, 2024) [@CAP-16; @CAP-17; @CAP-18] {VN-direct|Low}.
4. **Vietnamese venture capital was USD 509 million in 103 deals in 2025; agriculture got USD 1 million, about 0.2%** (our calculation: 1 / 509) [@CAP-21] {VN-direct|Medium}. Private equity reached a record USD 3.96 billion in 46 deals [@CAP-21; @CAP-22] {VN-direct|Medium}.
5. **There were no VC- or PE-backed technology initial public offerings (IPOs) in 2025 or the four years before.** So a realistic exit for a food or ingredient company is a trade sale or a PE buyout [@CAP-21; @CAP-22] {VN-direct|Medium}.
6. **A state venture framework now exists.** Decree 264/2025/ND-CP (in force 14 October 2025) allows national and provincial venture funds to accept up to 50% portfolio loss per investment cycle. Ho Chi Minh City's fund (VND 500 bn) plans its first investments in Q4 2026; Hanoi's (VND 230 bn) caps each investment at 5% of the fund, about USD 0.44 million (our calculation at VND 26,000 per USD) [@CAP-29; @CAP-33; @CAP-34] {VN-direct|Medium}.
7. **Global alternative-protein investment is at a cyclical low and has shifted towards fermentation:** USD 881 million in 2025 and USD 359 million in H1 2026 [@CAP-51] {general|High}.
8. **We found no development finance institution with a direct ticket in Vietnam in the protein families this study covers.** A ticket is the amount one investor puts in. IFC's Vietnamese tickets found run from USD 20 million (GS25, retail) to USD 90.6 million (Mavin, proposed) [@CAP-41; @CAP-44] {VN-direct|Medium}.
9. **The largest alternative-protein research and development (R&D) call we found open to Vietnamese applicants in 2026 has closed.** Coefficient Giving's request for proposals (RFP, up to USD 30 million) closed its two rounds on 10 August and 15 September 2026 [@CAP-57] {general|High}.
10. **The region offers no proven financing template either.** Across East and Southeast Asia we found about USD 355 million to 365 million of disclosed private alternative-protein money since 2020, three quarters of it raised in 2020 to 2022, and no direct DFI ticket. Plants were built by corporate joint ventures and state-scheme loans (section S11.6) {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
11. **A small processing line can use bank instruments that already exist.** Finance leasing, factoring (including factoring for the purchaser), supply-chain finance around a large buyer and guarantees for loans to small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) all work under Vietnamese rules today [@SLK-01; @SLK-05; @SLK-19; @SLK-20] {VN-direct|High}. They fit a textured-protein line of up to about USD 1.4 million, not a fermentation plant (sections S11.9 and S11.14).

## S11.4 A possible structure for a first plant

No disclosed deal in Vietnam in the protein families this study covers has exceeded USD 5 million. The disclosed deals are small or undisclosed: Emmay's about USD 250,000 in angel rounds and grants (2017 to 2021) [@CAP-15] {VN-direct|Low}, and undisclosed strategic minority stakes (Vinh Hoan in Shiok Meats and Avant Meats; VinaCapital Ventures in INNOHAS) [@CAP-16; @CAP-17; @CAP-18] {VN-direct|Low}. So there is no proven local financing template.

For a Vietnamese food-ingredient plant, we suggest a structure that pairs a private-equity lead, a development-finance ticket and a named buyer's offtake (our suggestion):

- a local PE lead, with a local co-investor;
- a small DFI "upstream" ticket, meaning early project-development money, that lowers the risk of the first plant;
- an offtake agreement (a commitment to buy the output) from a named food manufacturer, signed before or alongside the plant.

## S11.6 The regional deal record: East and Southeast Asia

To see whether the Vietnamese gap is local or regional, research wave 12 logged alternative-protein deals in East Asia (China, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong) and Southeast Asia from 2019 to October 2026 (`regional_deals.csv`, 43 rows; method in `working-papers/wave12/`). Feed-only deals are kept as context, and public programmes are kept apart from private deals.

**What the record shows.**

- **Disclosed private money is about USD 355 million to 365 million across the region since 2020** (our sum of the main rows with a disclosed amount; the range comes from two disagreeing figures, see below). About three quarters of it was raised in 2020 to 2022 (our calculation) {VN-adjacent|Medium}. Undisclosed rounds are not counted, and coverage of China after 2023 is thin, so this understates the total.
- **The biggest rounds came before 2023.** Next Gen Foods (TiNDLE, Singapore) raised a USD 100 million Series A in February 2022, led by Temasek's Asia Sustainable Foods Platform and GGV Capital [@RDL-28; @RDL-29] {VN-adjacent|High}. Green Monday (Hong Kong) raised USD 70 million in 2020 from TPG's Rise Fund and Swire Pacific [@RDL-17] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Since 2023, three deals stand out.** DAIZ (Japan) financed a plant-based ingredient factory in Kumamoto with JPY 7.1 billion (about USD 47 million): JPY 3.78 billion of equity from 11 companies and JPY 3.4 billion of low-cost long-term loans from nine financial institutions under schemes of Japan's industry and agriculture ministries (METI and MAFF) [@RDL-20; @RDL-47] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. Changing Bio (China, precision-fermented milk proteins) raised a USD 22 million Series A [@RDL-15] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. Green Rebel Foods (Indonesia) raised USD 10.5 million or 12.5 million in January 2026 from its existing impact investors [@RDL-05; @RDL-43] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Plants were built by corporate joint ventures and state-backed loans, not by venture rounds.** In Thailand, Innobic (the life-science arm of the state energy group PTT) and NR Instant Produce formed a 50:50 venture, which in turn took 51% of Plant & Bean (Thailand), a contract plant-based factory with 3,000 t a year of first-phase capacity [@RDL-30; @RDL-31; @RDL-32; @RDL-33] {VN-adjacent|High}. In Japan, ministry-scheme loans paid for part of the DAIZ factory. In China, an equipment maker (Tofflon) co-financed CellX's cultivated-meat pilot plant [@RDL-13] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Vietnamese money appears only at the edges.** Besides the stakes in section S11.5, we found one new Vietnamese deal: an on-air offer on Shark Tank Vietnam in August 2024 of VND 15 billion (about USD 0.6 million) for 15% of YERA, a Nghe An maker of yeast-based protein, from an executive of Thai Huong Group, the parent of TH. We found no confirmation that it closed [@RDL-01] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **We found no direct DFI ticket anywhere in the region in these families.** ADB Ventures' portfolio lists no plant-based, mycoprotein, precision-fermentation or cultivated-meat company [@RDL-03] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. One database lists ADB Ventures among the investors in DAIZ's 2023 round; we could not verify it (OQ-474) [@RDL-47] {VN-adjacent|Low}. The only DFI link to a Vietnamese alternative-protein firm we found is technical assistance: the Asian Development Bank's #SheExports support for Emmay in December 2025, with no investment [@RDL-02] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Public money has grown while private money shrank.** Korea's government funded Simple Planet's cultivated meat and seafood work (KRW 11 billion, about USD 8.1 million, in 2024) and a shared cultivated-meat research centre in Uiseong (KRW 14.5 billion, about USD 10 million, for 2025 to 2027) [@RDL-25; @RDL-26] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. China funds a cultivated-meat laboratory in Beijing (about USD 11 million) [@RDL-16] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.

**Exits, write-downs and pivots** (see also `firm_outcomes.csv`):

- **Umami Bioworks took over Shiok Meats' assets for stock in March 2024.** No cash changed hands, and the merged company was valued at USD 68 million [@RDL-06; @RDL-38; @RDL-45] {VN-adjacent|High}. A listed investor in Umami Bioworks cut the fair value of its holding by about half between the end of 2024 and the end of 2025 (our calculation from its filing) [@RDL-39] {VN-adjacent|High}.
- **GOOD Meat paused cultivated-chicken production in Singapore by early 2024 and did not open its Bedok plant** [@RDL-36; @RDL-37] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Avant's Singapore unit went into liquidation in February 2026**; the Hong Kong parent turned to skincare ingredients made from fish cells [@RDL-07] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Monde Nissin wrote down Quorn by PHP 20.5 billion (2022), PHP 10.1 billion (2023) and PHP 6.8 billion (2024)**, and its founding family pledged financing to cover further impairments [@RDL-40; @RDL-41] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
- **Next Gen Foods paid for marketing with equity and bought a plant-based dairy brand** instead of raising a priced Series B; we found no later round [@RDL-27] {VN-adjacent|High}.

**What this means for a first plant in Vietnam** (our reading):

1. The region offers no precedent for a private-equity lead with a DFI ticket and a buyer's offtake in these families. The structures that did build capacity were a corporate joint venture with a state-linked partner (Thailand), equity plus state-scheme loans (Japan) and equipment-maker co-financing (China).
2. A domestic strategic buyer is the likeliest lead. Regional food companies take minority stakes (Maruha Nichiro in Umami Bioworks, Nichirei Foods in DAIZ, NH Foods in IntegriCulture) but rarely lead rounds [@RDL-18; @RDL-19; @RDL-45] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
3. Since 2023, equity rounds in the region outside Japan and China have rarely exceeded about USD 12.5 million. A first Vietnamese plant probably has to be fundable at about USD 5 million to 15 million, mostly from a sponsor's balance sheet and debt.
4. DFI money reaches the field through funds and technical assistance, not direct tickets. The routes already logged in section S11.8 (fund stakes, credit lines to an agribusiness sponsor) remain the practical ones.

## S11.9 State venture framework and state finance

**Decree 264/2025/ND-CP on national and local venture funds** (signed and in force 14 October 2025) [@CAP-29; @CAP-30] {VN-direct|High}:

- National fund: the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) represents the state's capital; at least VND 500 bn from the development budget at the start (about USD 19 million); at least VND 2,000 bn within five years including mobilised capital (about USD 77 million) (USD figures: our calculation).
- Investment forms: direct equity, fund-of-funds, co-investment.
- Loss threshold: 50% of charter capital across the portfolio per investment cycle (Art. 10.4.a).
- Cycle: 10 years, or 15 for strategic or dual-use technology (Art. 10.1.c).
- Managers are exempt from liability for losses from "objective risks" (Art. 14).
- Priority sectors include the green and circular economy (Art. 5.3).
- Local funds invest only in Vietnam; the provincial People's Committee sets the state share.
- Status: in June 2026 MOST was still preparing the national fund proposal; experts and international fund representatives quoted in the press favoured a fund-of-funds model [@CAP-31] {VN-direct|Medium}.

It suits established manufacturers adding a line, not startups that have no sales yet.

**Credit programmes.**

- A bank-funded preferential programme of VND 500 trillion (2025 to 2030; VND 100 trillion in 2025 to 2026) covers strategic infrastructure, high technology and innovation through 21 banks; no agriculture-specific window was found [@CAP-40] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- Decree 156/2025 (in force 1 July 2025) raises unsecured lending limits for agriculture (for example up to VND 5 bn for cooperatives) and gives organic and circular agriculture the same credit treatment as high-tech agriculture [@CAP-39] {VN-direct|Medium}. A side-stream protein plant framed as circular agriculture might qualify; we found no case that tests this.
- **The SME Development Fund (SMEDF)** lends to innovative SMEs and to SMEs in industry clusters and value chains that have feasible plans. Decree 45/2024/ND-CP (26 April 2024, in force 10 June 2024) amended its rules. It lends through commercial banks, which appraise each loan and bear the risk [@SLK-10] {VN-direct|High}. In 2021 it lent at 80% of the lowest commercial lending rate through five partner banks [@SLK-11] {VN-direct|Low}. We could not find its current rates, loan caps or terms (OQ-502). An SME that owns a textured-protein line and supplies a *chay* (vegetarian food) value chain could plausibly apply as a value-chain borrower (our reading).
- **Provincial SME credit guarantee funds** work under Decree 34/2018/ND-CP. Ho Chi Minh City's fund got a new charter on 29 May 2026 (Decision 3204/QĐ-UBND); the city now includes the former Ba Ria-Vung Tau and Binh Duong [@SLK-20] {VN-direct|High}:
  - charter capital of about VND 227.9 billion (about USD 8.8 million, our calculation);
  - guarantees of up to 100% of a loan, for working capital or for medium and long-term loans;
  - a cap per customer of 15% of actual charter capital for investment loans, about VND 34 billion or USD 1.3 million (our calculation), and total guarantees of up to three times charter capital;
  - conditions: a feasible plan, owner's equity of at least 20% of the project, no bad debt and collateral. The city sets the fees, which we could not see (OQ-503).

  The cap per customer covers almost all of the study's range for the installed cost of a textured-protein line, USD 0.28 to 1.38 million (our calculation; [[ch26-plays]]) [@SLK-20] {VN-direct|Medium}. It cannot carry a fermentation first plant of tens of millions of USD. We read only the Ho Chi Minh City fund.

**Other state instruments** covered elsewhere: the Investment Support Fund under Decree 182/2024 (high thresholds; see [[app-s9-regulation]]) [@REG-46] {VN-direct|High}; specialised science funds with state capital under Law 93/2025/QH15 ([[app-s7-research]]) [@REG-48] {VN-direct|High}; and interest subsidies of 70% of the loan interest rate, capped at 8% a year, in hi-tech agriculture zones under Decree 263/2026 [@INF-23] {VN-direct|Medium} ([[app-s8-labs-talent]]).

**Support for processing plants (Decree 57/2018/ND-CP).** Article 11 of Decree 57/2018/ND-CP (17 April 2018) pays 60% of the investment in a processing facility, up to VND 15 billion (about USD 0.58 million, our calculation) per project. The condition is that the processed products are worth at least 1.5 times the raw agricultural products [@SLK-21] {VN-direct|High}. In July 2026 MAE (the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment) still listed the decree as current policy [@SLK-22] {VN-direct|High}. A replacement draft discussed on 31 March 2026 would cap support at 30% of investment, and a Deputy Minister of Finance called the current decree's effect "limited" [@SLK-23] {VN-direct|Medium}.

For a textured-protein line, landed Chinese textured protein (USD 1.29 to 1.38 per kg) over the flour price at which a line breaks even (about USD 0.70 to 0.94 per kg) gives a ratio of 1.37 to 1.97 before yield losses (our calculation; [[ch26-plays]]) [@TIC-12; @BUY-02] {VN-direct|Medium}. So a line passes the 1.5 times test only when flour is cheap. We could not confirm that processing imported soy flour counts (OQ-501). Treat this support as a possible bonus, not a financing plan.

## S11.12 Exit landscape

Investors in Vietnam can exit mainly by selling to a larger company (a trade sale) or to a PE fund. The stock market is not yet a route for technology firms.

- **IPO route: closed for technology.** Vietnam had three IPOs in 2025, all in financial services (USD 1.35 billion), and no VC- or PE-backed technology IPOs in 2025 or the four years before; the IPO pipeline is expected to reach USD 3 billion to 5 billion in 2026 to 2027 [@CAP-21; @CAP-22] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Revealed strategic interest (verified):** Vinh Hoan (cultivated seafood stakes), CJ CheilJedang (co-investor in Shiok; large Vietnamese food footprint), VinaCapital (market-entry partner for INNOHAS) [@CAP-16; @CAP-17; @CAP-18] {VN-direct|Low}.
- **No corporate venture arm in food.** We found no venture arm of a Vietnamese food or dairy group, so there is no corporate fund to approach; Vingroup's VinVentures is for technology, not food (section S11.5) [@SLK-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. A domestic acquirer would buy or partner from its own balance sheet.
- **Plausible acquirers by fit (not verified as interested):** Masan (consumer foods, meat), Vinamilk, TH, Nutifood and Vinasoy/QNS (plant milks), KIDO (edible oils and fats), Thai Union, Minh Phu and Vinh Hoan (seafood; also aquafeed), Ajinomoto and Vedan (fermentation). For feed ingredients, which the study now treats as context, the buyers would be C.P. Vietnam, De Heus and other feed majors. This list comes from sector logic, not deal evidence.
- **Multiples:** M&A multiples for Vietnamese food deals were not established. No Vietnamese alternative-protein company has had a disclosed exit.
- **Consolidation among feed buyers (context):** De Heus Vietnam lists 21 plants (September 2026) and completed buying CJ Feed & Care in March 2026. In our reading, this concentrates feed buying power in one strategic buyer. We do not know whether the 21 plants include the former CJ mills [@VCO-09; @VCO-08] {VN-direct|Medium}.

> **For investors.** A thesis in Vietnamese alternative protein has to rest on business-to-business sales of ingredients to food makers and a strategic buyer, not on a consumer-brand exit. Agriculture VC was about USD 1 million in 2025 [@CAP-21] {VN-direct|Medium}, and there is no track record of venture exits.

## S11.14 Leasing, factoring and supply-chain finance

Three kinds of bank finance can pay for a small food-processing line without venture money: finance leasing, factoring and supply-chain finance arranged around a large buyer. They matter most for the domestic textured-protein line (play T1 in [[ch26-plays]]). Its equipment costs USD 0.28 to 1.38 million installed, and its larger need is USD 0.6 to 3.5 million of working capital (our calculation; [[app-s12-costs]]).

This section describes instruments under Vietnamese rules. It does not recommend providers and is not legal or investment advice. The rules are also in `instruments.csv` (INS-079 and INS-080).

**Finance leasing.** Circular 26/2024/TT-NHNN of the State Bank of Vietnam (SBV), in force from 1 July 2024, governs leasing by finance companies and finance-leasing companies [@SLK-01] {VN-direct|High}:

- Leasable assets are machinery, equipment, tools and means of transport that are not on the banned lists (Art 3(2)). An extruder, dryer and packing line fit.
- The lessee needs a feasible plan for using the asset and the capacity to pay (Art 5(1)). Leases of up to VND 100 million (about USD 3,800, our calculation) count as small and skip the plan test.
- Sale and leaseback is allowed (Arts 26 to 29). A firm sells equipment it owns to the lessor and leases it back. The asset must be owned, undisputed, not pledged elsewhere and in normal use.
- Leases are in VND. Foreign currency is allowed only for imported assets, and only if the lessee earns enough foreign currency to repay (Art 9(2)). So a maker selling in VND would lease an imported line in VND.
- A January 2026 SBV draft would raise the small-lease threshold to VND 400 million and allow intangible assets. We found no sign that it has been issued [@SLK-02] {VN-direct|Medium}.

The leasing sector is small. Reported leasing balances were VND 41,700 billion (about USD 1.6 billion, our calculation) on 30 June 2025, from six of nine companies, about 0.25% of total credit. The leasing association estimated about VND 47,000 billion for the whole sector; we give both (DG-386) [@SLK-04] {VN-direct|Medium}. Production lines made up over VND 6,900 billion (about USD 265 million, our calculation) [@SLK-04] {VN-direct|Medium}.

Eight leasing companies operate; the association's total count is internally inconsistent (DG-385) [@SLK-04; @SLK-24] {VN-direct|Medium}. BIDV-SuMi Trust Leasing (BSL), a 2017 joint venture of BIDV and Japan's Sumitomo Mitsui Trust Bank, says over 40% of its leases are production machinery and equipment, mainly for SMEs (company statement) [@SLK-24] {VN-direct|Medium}. Leasing fits the equipment of a textured-protein line, including sale and leaseback for a maker that already owns equipment. It does not fund working capital. We could not read any lessor's terms, such as the financing ratio, term or rate (OQ-498).

**Factoring.** Circular 20/2024/TT-NHNN, in force from 1 July 2024, replaced Circular 02/2017/TT-NHNN [@SLK-05] {VN-direct|High}:

- Providers are commercial banks, finance companies, factoring companies and foreign bank branches.
- It allows factoring with recourse to the seller, without recourse, and factoring for the purchaser. In the last form, the factor pays the seller on the buyer's behalf and the buyer repays. It is the nearest Vietnamese form to reverse factoring, where a large buyer arranges finance for its suppliers.
- Receivables must come from contracts for goods or services and have less than one year to run. Pledged, overdue and disputed receivables are excluded, and factoring terms are under one year.
- The SBV circulated a draft amendment on 13 March 2026. We found no issued text by 15 October 2026 [@SLK-07] {VN-direct|Medium}.

Factoring balances were about VND 16,000 billion in August 2025, about 0.1% of total credit (our calculation; DG-387) [@SLK-06] {VN-direct|Medium}. At a roundtable of the Vietnam Banks Association and IFC, bankers named the obstacles: receivables pledged twice, appraisal of buyers, accounting treatment and four legal gaps [@SLK-06] {VN-direct|Medium}. Factoring fits a working-capital gap better than leasing. But it works only when the buyer is large and creditworthy, because the factor prices the buyer's risk (our reading).

**Supply-chain finance around an anchor buyer.**

- BIDV's SCFast 2026 package (up to VND 15,000 billion, about USD 577 million, our calculation) offers supplier finance, buyer-side and seller-side factoring and overdrafts for distributors. SMEs referred by an anchor company can borrow up to 85% of order value with inventory as collateral (bank's own release) [@SLK-19] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- BIDV says it serves nearly 100 supply chains. Animal feed is the nearest named sector to food; we found no programme that names food or *chay* makers [@SLK-19] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- The IFC and the Swiss State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) run the Viet Nam Supply Chain Finance Program. Its second phase runs to 2029 with a SECO grant of CHF 5 million (Swiss francs). IFC says Vietnamese suppliers are typically paid 30 to 60 days after delivery [@SLK-18] {VN-direct|High}.

**What this means for a textured-protein line** (our reading):

- Leasing can pay for the equipment, and the guarantee fund and the SME Development Fund in section S11.9 can back a bank loan for it.
- The working capital depends on an anchor buyer. A line that supplies a large buyer, such as CJ Foods Vietnam or Vissan (both named T1 customers), could finance its receivables through supplier finance or factoring priced on the anchor's credit. The long tail of small *chay* makers is not an anchor.
- So the financing route supports the advice that T1 should be owned by, or contracted to, a large *chay* maker, co-packer or distributor ([[ch26-plays]]). Before building, ask the anchor buyer's bank whether it will finance the line owner's receivables (OQ-499).
- These instruments fit lines of up to a few million USD. They cannot carry a fermentation first plant of tens of millions of USD (plays T4 and T5).
