# Brief for policy makers

**The short version.** Protein for people first; feed only as context. This study asks what Vietnam could make at home for people to eat, from what, at what cost, under which rules, and who would buy it. Vietnam has the carbohydrate, the fermentation skills and the buyers. But it ranks joint last in the region (with Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines) on regulatory readiness [@RGN-01]. Most of the fix is cheap, and the most valuable step has a deadline. The National Assembly takes first comments on the revised Food Safety Law at its October 2026 session and considers passage at its May 2027 session. So a new-food clause needs to be in the draft revised between the two sessions, in practice by early 2027 [@APR-07] {VN-direct|Medium}.

## Why it matters: the security of the protein people eat

Vietnamese people eat more meat each year. Meat intake was 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, against a recommendation of 50 to 80 g [@DIE-01; @DIE-02; @DIE-03] {VN-direct|Medium}. Animal foods supply 42% of protein [@DIE-07; @DIE-08] {VN-direct|High}. Most of that meat, and the eggs and farmed fish, is produced in Vietnam on imported feed; about 90% of the meat supply was domestic in 2023 [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High}.

About 99% of the soy protein (our calculation from USDA supply data) and three-quarters of the maize in that feed are imported [@MAC-04; @MAC-08; @MAC-01]. That is roughly USD 10 billion of feed-ingredient imports in 2025 (our calculation) [@MAC-07; @MAC-08; @MAC-09; @MAC-10]. The 2030 livestock strategy targets 30 to 32 Mt of industrial feed, 8.5 to 10.5 Mt more in 2030 than in 2024 [@REG-54; @MAC-28]. Disease shocks also hit supply: after African swine fever reached Vietnam in 2019, the pig producer price rose 70% from 2019 to 2020 [@DIE-25] {VN-direct|Medium}.

Protein made in Vietnam for people, from domestic inputs where possible, adds a second source where it meets a price or function. The strongest case is supply security, industrial value and trade; environment and animal welfare are narrower motivations ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]). There is a limit. At the scale the demand evidence supports, about 0.2% of meat protein displaced by 2035 on the benchmark path, it does not change national exposure on its own ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]).

## Food security: measure first, and be precise about what new protein does

- **Most of the animal protein people eat rests on imports, but no target measures it.** In 2023, 19% to 23% of the protein supply was imported food (our calculation) [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|Medium}. Counting the feed behind domestic meat, eggs and farmed fish, 61% to 71% of animal protein rested on imports (our calculation) [@DIE-07; @MAC-01; @FS-23] {VN-direct|Low}. The food-security resolution to 2030 (Resolution 34/NQ-CP, 2021) sets production volumes and has no import, feed or protein indicator [@SEC-01] {VN-direct|High}.
- **So the first move is a national protein balance for people.** It would show what people eat by food, source and origin, with the imported feed behind domestic meat as a memo line. Chapter 1 builds a first version from data of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO); we found no food balance published by a Vietnamese agency. The 2026 plan of the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE) asks to "step by step reduce import dependence by developing domestic raw-material areas", the closest official hook [@SEC-02] {VN-direct|High}. Define the indicator by where the inputs come from: about 84% of the food plant-protein ingredients reaching Vietnam in 2025 came from China (our calculation), so protein made from them moves the dependence rather than reducing it [@BUY-02] {VN-direct|Medium} ([[ch28-robust-moves]]).
- **Disease is the shock consumers feel, and the vaccine gap is open.** Consumer pork prices rose 57.23% in 2020 after African swine fever (ASF) [@SEC-08] {VN-direct|High}. In 2025, 79% of the ASF samples typed in the north were recombinant strains, and MAE says the three licensed vaccines "have no protective effect" against them [@PAN-33] {VN-direct|High}. New protein is a second source, not disease control. We found no study that measures lower zoonotic, pandemic or resistance risk from replacing meat [@PAN-27; @PAN-30; @PAN-31] {general|Medium}. At the scale demand supports, it would spare about 142,000 pigs a year in 2035, against 2025 culls 8.4 times that (our calculation from [@MAC-01; @PAN-03] and [[app-d8-demand-model]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}.
- **Imported meat is not a subsidy problem.** Meat imports reached 978,000 t, worth USD 2.0 billion, in 2025 [@MIM-04; @MIM-05] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. For most of what Vietnam buys, the origin gives no price support [@MIM-15] {general|High}, and no trade-defence case on meat or dairy has been filed [@MIM-25] {VN-direct|Medium}.

## What could be made first

The study keeps seven plays. Three lead under almost every weighting:

- domestic textured plant protein to replace imports from China (T1);
- a cassava-starch-to-fermentation-sugar hub, a cluster of plants on one site, so a food-fermentation step stays in Vietnam (T4);
- shared pilot and tolling fermentation, where firms rent time on someone else's fermenter (T5). Most fermentation plays need it first.

Koji and fungal foods (T9) and mung bean and rice protein for plant drinks (T10) are worth proving. Precision fermentation fits only as contract-made, high-value ingredients (T7). Cultivated seafood is research for this decade (T8) ([[ch26-plays]]).

## What you need to know

1. **Food rules: an open door but no gate.** New protein foods enter through self-declaration. Yet "new-technology food not yet permitted for circulation" can be recalled, and no procedure grants permission. Firms face the risk of enforcement discretion, not a ban ([[ch07-rules]]).
2. **Precision-fermented proteins probably count as genetically modified (GMO) products** under Decree 43/2026. Making them with a GM strain needs a closed-production certificate, which has no published timeline or fee ([[ch07-rules]]).
3. **The region is moving.** Singapore has approved 18 novel foods. Thailand has a novel-food route with recognised assessment units. Its 2026 to 2035 biotechnology framework cites the same regional study that scores Vietnam 0 on rules. Korea amended an existing rule in about nine months ([[ch03-regional]]).
4. **Tax holidays are the weakest incentive for this industry.** Capital is most of the cost. A regional incentive study ranks capital grants and loan guarantees far above tax holidays ([[ch09-economics]]).

## Options, ranked

Three options lead. They are cheap and time-critical (2026 to 2027): the new-food clause, the joint note on precision-fermented proteins, and the novel-food procedure in the law's implementing decree. Owners are the Ministry of Health (MOH), the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE), the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST), the Ministry of Finance (MOF) and the cities.

| Rank | Option | Owner | Effort | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Delegation clause in the Food Safety Law: "The Government shall regulate the management of new foods and new food ingredients" | MOH; National Assembly committee | Low | First comments October 2026; passage considered May 2027 [@APR-07] |
| 2 | Joint MAE and MOH note: purified precision-fermented proteins labelled GM above 5%; no GMO food-use certificate if no viable GMO or recombinant DNA remains | MAE; MOH | Low | 3 to 6 months |
| 3 | Novel-food decree: "not novel" determination, traditional-food lane, reliance lane, recognised assessment units, public positive list | MOH; Government | Medium | Drafting Q4 2026 |
| 4 to 9 | Hanoi sandbox pilot; closed-production certificate guidance; high-tech tax guidance; shared food-grade pilot fermentation line; capital grants over tax holidays; fund priorities | MOST, MAE, MOF, cities | Low to High | 2027 to 2028 |

Ranks 10 to 19 include protein-quality testing, a by-product atlas, allergen and naming rules, and transition rules. All 33 options in the register, with instruments and precedents: [[ch27-policy-options]].

## Alignment with national strategy

The options serve three existing policies:

- Resolution 36-NQ/TW on biotechnology (biotech 7% of GDP by 2030; replace imported biotech products).
- Resolution 57 on science and innovation (research and development at 2% of GDP).
- The livestock strategy (Decision 1520: by-product use).

None of these names alternative protein. Naming it in biotechnology programmes and state venture-fund priorities costs little ([[ch07-rules]], [[ch08-capital]]).

## Trade-offs to weigh

- Speed against caution in a reliance lane.
- The risk that a new gate slows products that are legal today (hence transition rules).
- Tariffs that help food makers but weaken domestic ingredient makers.
- Public spending on a pilot line that needs an anchor host so it does not stand idle ([[ch27-policy-options]]).

## For provinces

Tay Ninh and the Central Highlands hold the cassava-starch industry. It could host fermentation-sugar hubs (play T4). Ho Chi Minh City, Dong Nai and Hanoi hold the industrial parks, research institutes, city venture funds and the sandbox. The provincial map changed on 1 July 2025 ([[app-s10-admin-map]]).

## The demand side

- **Most label rules already exist: enforce them and settle two questions.** TCVN 13625:2023 (a Vietnamese national standard) already defines vegetarian and vegan claims. But its vegetarian class allows egg, milk and honey. So ask the Vietnam Food Administration (VFA) to state that an unqualified *chay* or *thuần chay* (strict vegetarian) claim means the vegan criteria. Any meat named in a product name must already be quantified (Decree 37/2026 carries the rule of Decree 43/2017). So blends need only a voluntary industry code for generic names such as *giò lụa* and *xúc xích*. We found no rule that reserves *thịt* (meat), *sữa* (milk) or *phô mai* (cheese); defend that permissive regime in the new law. The GMO label applies only above 5% of ingredients. So one question remains for MAE and VFA: is a recombinant protein identical to the native one a detectable product of the modified gene? [@LBL-15; @LBL-06; @LBL-07; @LBL-10; @LBL-16] {VN-direct|High} ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]])
- **School meals: the animal-share target has expired, but the menu software still prints one.** Decision 1340 (2016) set targets that ended in 2020 [@CHN-33]. The Ministry of Health's Decision 3958/QĐ-BYT (December 2025) sets no animal share and asks for plant protein (legumes, tofu) at least twice a week. Our audit of published menus (`school_menu_weeks.csv`) finds that only 9 of 48 school-weeks meet that. Menus serve tofu beside meat. The menu software of the Ministry of Education and Training (MOET), Ajinomoto and the National Institute of Nutrition (NIN) prints a standard of "animal protein at least 48%" [@APR-04; @PMR-10; @PMR-26] {VN-direct|High}. Use three tools to replace that default with protein-quality, zinc, iron and sodium targets per lunch, and to add textured soy and legume products to the software's food list:
  - the School Health Programme 2026 to 2035 (Decision 973/QĐ-TTg);
  - the MOET *bán trú* (school lunch and midday care) circular due in 2026;
  - the digital menu tools due in 2027 to 2028 [@PMR-01] {VN-direct|High} ([[ch15-channels]]).
- **Children's nutrition: aim at the gaps that exist.** Stunting affects 18% of children under five (2023) and 32% of ethnic minority children, so it is a problem of the youngest children [@APR-27] {VN-direct|Medium}. For school-age children protein is not the gap. Published Hanoi primary-school lunches carry about 31.8 g (our calculation). Upland boarding lunches and dinners carried meat, fish or egg in 240 of 242 published meals (our menu coding), and weighed boarding diets met protein and zinc. The documented shortfalls are calcium and vitamin A, and our menu coding shows breakfast as the thin meal [@PMR-26; @UPL-01; @UPL-04; @UPL-09; @UPL-18] {VN-direct|Medium}. A plant dish that removes all meat needs added zinc; blends keep protein quality high ([[ch11-protein-diet]]).
- **Canteens and procurement.** Factory canteens serve up to 1.2 billion meals a year (our calculation from about 4.15 million industrial-park workers) [@CHN-37]. They are a large protein channel where price decides. A procurement standard for innovative protein in public and state-enterprise canteens is the form of this move a market-shaping funder can back. Corporate targets are a weak lever here. Accor's target of 50% vegetarian or plant-based dishes by 2030 is the only one we found that binds Vietnamese units. We found no Vietnamese protein buyer with a published target [@AFN-05; @AFN-40; @AFN-18] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Rule on plant milks before the sugary-drink excise starts.** From 1 January 2027, drinks above 5 g of total sugar per 100 ml pay 8% (10% from 2028). Dairy is exempt. Whether sweetened soy, nut and cereal milks count as *nước giải khát* (soft drinks) is unresolved. A written Ministry of Finance ruling before the tax starts would let makers plan recipes [@FTR-01; @FTR-02; @FTR-05] {VN-direct|High} ([[ch13-consumers]]).
- **Trade: tariffs give no shelter; origin rules and data matter more.** Chinese, ASEAN and Korean protein ingredients enter duty-free. They do so under ASEAN's agreements with China and Korea, the ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership. So changing most-favoured-nation (standard) rates cannot protect a domestic maker [@FTR-32; @FTR-33] {VN-direct|Medium}. Instead:
  - protect the EU, UK, Japanese and CPTPP (Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership) preferences on wrapped foods; Japan's edge on Vietnamese dumplings is 17.4 points;
  - publish an origin note on the EU cap of 20% non-originating flour and starch in wrappers;
  - create eight-digit customs subheadings for protein ingredients, so that demand is visible [@ORG-01; @ORG-05] {VN-direct|High} ([[ch17-export-demand]]).
- **Measure before targeting.** No probability survey asks how many people keep chay days. Yet existing chay days probably avoid as much meat protein as the Part IV alternative-protein scenario assumes for 2030 to 2035 ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]). National survey budgets, not philanthropy, should carry that question. Start with a lunar-date tabulation of the 2021 diet survey, in which 7.9% of adults ate no meat, poultry or fish on the previous day [@APR-36] {VN-direct|Medium}. Do not set national targets from consultancy forecasts ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]).

## Looking to 2050

- **Vietnam's 2050 plans cover inputs, not protein.** Power, hydrogen and emission caps have 2050 numbers; meat, feed and aquaculture targets stop at 2030. The successor livestock and fisheries strategies and the national biotechnology strategy are the natural homes for protein targets ([[ch20-drivers-2050]], [[app-f5-targets-hubs]]).
- **Protein for people saves the most imports per tonne.** On trend, the meat protein Vietnamese people eat rises from about 1.01 Mt in 2025 to about 1.45 Mt in 2050. The imported soybean meal behind it rises from 7.2 Mt to about 10.4 Mt (our model) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. Replacing 1 t of meat protein in diets avoids about 4.5 t of soybean-meal and 9.5 t of maize imports (our calculation) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. At the scale demand supports (benchmark path), new protein displaces about 0.4% of meat protein in 2050. It adds a second source rather than replacing meat ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]).
- **Feed efficiency is a larger lever, outside this study's focus.** In the balance model, better feed conversion and formulation would cut 2050 soybean-meal need by about 3.4 Mt. That is about twice what the study's large alternative-protein scenario (S-ALT) would do {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]).
- **Seven no-regret moves pay off in all four 2050 worlds.** Most can start by 2028 with existing institutions:
  - a national protein balance;
  - a new-food route with deadlines;
  - shared food-grade pilot capacity;
  - a protein-quality laboratory and price series;
  - residue-carbon rules and a by-product atlas;
  - climate-proof siting;
  - process-engineering skills.

  One cheap option belongs alongside them: naming food-fermentation sites in the MAE biotechnology-cluster plan (December 2026) ([[ch28-robust-moves]]).
- **A vision to debate, not a forecast.** By 2050 Vietnam feeds a larger, richer and older population from a protein supply that is measured, more diverse and more secure. It publishes a yearly protein balance of what people eat and where it comes from. Domestic makers supply most of the plant-protein ingredients that food makers now import. New routes provide about 67,000 to 172,000 t of protein a year. About 59,000 to 147,000 t of it is made in Vietnam (benchmark and stretch paths of our demand model) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. Meat stays the main protein. The vision is reviewed at every five-year plan ([[ch24-vision-2050]]).
