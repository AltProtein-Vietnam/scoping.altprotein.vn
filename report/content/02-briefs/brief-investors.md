# Brief for investors

**The short version.** Protein for people first; feed only as context. This study asks what Vietnam could make at home for people to eat. Vietnam runs a large animal-protein economy on imported plant protein, so the case for making protein at home is real. But there is almost no deal history. The near-term theses that pass our tests are business-to-business, and none is a classic venture deal:

- **Domestic textured plant protein (T1)** is an import-substitution plant. It is best owned by a *chay* (vegetarian food) maker, co-packer or distributor, and financed with debt. It has no tariff shelter, and the delivered price of soy flour decides its margin.
- **A cassava-starch-to-fermentation-sugar hub (T4)** and **shared pilot and tolling fermentation (T5)**, where firms rent time on someone else's fermenter, need strategic partners: starch groups, owners of fermentation tanks, and public or development-finance co-funding.
- **Fermentation is decided by capital cost**, not by Vietnam's cheap labour and power. Price every deal against Chinese imports, and expect exits through trade sales.

## What you need to know

1. **One precedent, and it is outside the focus.** Entobel's USD 32.5 million insect-meal plant is the only alternative-protein deal in Vietnam above USD 5 million. It is an insect-feed deal, outside this study's focus, but its structure is a template. Mekong Capital, Dragon Capital and a USD 2.5 million ticket from the International Finance Corporation (IFC) financed it, with Vinh Hoan (a seafood exporter) as anchor buyer. Local private equity in the lead, development finance alongside and a named buyer: that is the structure to copy ([[ch08-capital]]).
2. **Thin venture market for this sector.** Vietnam's venture capital was about USD 509 million in 2025, and agriculture took about USD 1 million of it. There have been no technology stock-market listings in five years. Realistic exits are trade sales to strategic buyers or private-equity buyouts ([[ch08-capital]]).
3. **Capital decides fermentation economics.** On our indicative cost stack, capital charges and maintenance are 54 to 67% of the cost of fungal biomass protein made in Vietnam. That protein costs USD 4,050 to 14,700 per tonne of protein ([[ch09-economics]]). Cheap labour and power are a small part of the cost.
4. **The competitor is Chinese.** Chinese microbial protein enters duty-free [@FTR-32] {VN-direct|Medium}. A Chinese yeast-protein plant runs at 11,000 t a year, and a 20,000 t a year mycoprotein plant began commercial runs in June 2026 ([[ch03-regional]]). Chinese textured protein, isolates and gluten also enter duty-free (see "The demand side" below).
5. **Food rules have no gate yet.** Familiar plant foods reach the market by self-declaration. But there is no route to positive clearance of a new food ingredient, and precision-fermented proteins probably count as genetically modified (GMO) products ([[ch07-rules]]).
6. **Watch October 2026 to May 2027.** The Food Safety Law revision is the event that most changes risk for food. The National Assembly takes first comments at its October 2026 session and considers passage at the May 2027 session. So a new-food clause needs to be in the draft revised between the two sessions, in practice by early 2027 [@APR-07] {VN-direct|Medium} ([[ch19-outlook-2035]]).
7. **The security case is real but narrow, and it favours domestic inputs.** About two thirds of the animal protein Vietnamese people eat (61% to 71% in 2023) rests on imports, directly or through feed (our calculation) [@DIE-07; @MAC-01; @FS-23] {VN-direct|Low}. A plant that runs on imported soy, pea or gluten moves that dependence rather than reducing it: about 84% of the food plant-protein ingredients reaching Vietnam in 2025 came from China (our calculation) [@BUY-02] {VN-direct|Medium}. Only routes on domestic inputs reduce it: fermentation on cassava or sugar (play T4), rice and peanut protein, and soy foods from Vietnamese beans [@SEC-05; @SEC-14; @FS-24; @FS-01] {VN-direct|Medium}. If a thesis leans on food security, check where the inputs come from. We found no study that measures lower disease or pandemic risk from alternative protein, so do not pitch it that way ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]).

## Theses and how to test them

Seven plays are active, and three lead under almost every weighting: domestic textured plant protein (T1), a cassava-starch-to-fermentation-sugar hub (T4) and shared pilot and tolling fermentation (T5). Koji and fungal foods (T9) and mung bean and rice protein for plant drinks (T10) are worth proving. Precision fermentation fits only as contract-made, high-value ingredients (T7). Cultivated seafood is research for this decade (T8).

| Play | Thesis | Diligence question (kill test) |
|---|---|---|
| T1 Domestic textured plant protein | An import-substitution ingredient plant. It replaces Chinese textured soy for *chay* and frozen-food makers. It is best owned by a chay maker, co-packer or distributor, and financed with bank debt and trade finance, not venture equity. Installed capex about USD 0.3 to 1.4 million; working capital USD 0.6 to 3.5 million (our calculations). No intellectual property; it displaces almost no meat | Is food-grade defatted soy flour delivered below about USD 700 to 940 per t (1 t/h line at 70% utilisation)? Have at least three buyers placed conditional orders totalling 2,000 t a year? Does ex-factory cost beat Chinese textured protein (customs code HS 2106.10)? It left China at USD 1.24 per kg free on board (FOB) in 2025. It lands at about USD 1.29 to 1.38 per kg with freight, port and inland costs (our calculation; duty 0% under the ASEAN-China Free Trade Area, ACFTA) [@TIC-02; @TIC-12; @FTR-32] {VN-direct\|Low} |
| T4 Cassava-starch-to-sugar hub | Keep the starch-to-glucose step in Vietnam and put food fermentation on the same site (a hub is a cluster of plants on one site). Build it with a starch group and a domestic or foreign food fermenter as strategic partners | Has a fermenter agreed to co-locate? Can sugar be contracted despite volatile cassava prices? |
| T5 Shared pilot and tolling fermentation | A food-grade pilot line plus tolling (renting time on someone else's fermenter) on existing industrial tanks. It needs an anchor host, an operator and public or development-finance co-funding. Owners of large tanks (Vedan, Ajinomoto, Daesang) are the natural partners | Does a host accept tolling terms? Is use above about a third of capacity in the first two years? |
| T9 Koji and fungal foods | Fermented foods and ingredients from a characterised food mould on food-grade side streams (okara, broken rice, spent grain), with low capital | Can strain safety data be obtained? Do sensory trials pass with target buyers? |
| T10 Mung bean and rice protein for plant drinks | Concentrates or isolates that replace imported pea protein in high-protein plant drinks | Does a buyer accept it on taste and stability? Is its cost close to imported pea protein? |
| T7 Precision fermentation (niche) | High-value, low-dose ingredients made under contract abroad | Is the product economic at titers (product per litre of broth) of 1 to 10 g/L? Is freedom to operate clear? |

## Who else is here

- **Local private equity and venture capital:** Mekong Capital, Dragon Capital and VinaCapital Ventures.
- **Development finance:** the IFC, and ADB Ventures, the Asian Development Bank's venture arm (no Vietnamese agrifood deal yet).
- **Regional seed specialists:** Better Bite Ventures, Big Idea Ventures and ProVeg Incubator.
- **New state venture funds:** Ho Chi Minh City (VND 500 billion) and Hanoi (VND 230 billion).
- **Strategic buyers, by fit:** dairy, seafood and amino-acid groups.

## What would make this more investable

- The three highest-ranked policy options ([[ch27-policy-options]], options 1 to 3):
  - a new-food clause in the Food Safety Law;
  - a joint note on the GMO status of purified precision-fermented proteins;
  - a novel-food procedure with timelines in the law's decree.
- Tolling access to existing fermentation tanks, or a shared food-grade pilot line (T5).
- A development-finance first-loss tranche for a first food-ingredient or fermentation plant. Make the case on imports and jobs, not on a climate label.
- For T1: a delivered soy-flour price sheet and conditional orders from chay makers. These cost tens of hours and decide the kill test ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).

## The demand side

- **The revealed demand is business-to-business, and a soy line can serve only part of it.** Plant proteins shipped from China to Vietnam nearly tripled from 2019 to 2025, to about 54,000 t. But the rise is mostly HS 3504 protein substances at about USD 1 per kg, priced like feed or technical protein rather than food isolate. Textured-type imports, the pool a soy extrusion line can serve, were at most about 12,800 t in 2025 [@BUY-02; @TIC-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. Chay makers are the most switchable buyers ([[ch16-business-buyers]]).
- **Size on the import-substitution pool, not on consumer forecasts.** On the benchmark path the routes create demand for about 19,000 t of protein a year by 2035. About 11,000 to 17,000 t of that would be made in Vietnam for the home market. For soy extrusion that is about one 1 t/h line or two 0.5 t/h lines, not three to four. The rest is gluten and isolates, which need other plants (our calculation) {VN-direct|Low}. Published Vietnamese market sizes have no traceable method, and global forecasts from 2019 to 2021 overshot by 10 to 100 times ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]).
- **T1 has no tariff shelter, cheap equipment and one decisive input.** Chinese, ASEAN and Korean textured protein, isolates and gluten enter duty-free. Chinese extrusion lines are listed at USD 11,000 to 100,000, and working capital is the larger need. The delivered price of food-grade soy flour (63 to 85% of cost, our calculation, [[ch09-economics]]) decides whether the margin is positive. Export origin rules give domestic textured soy no customer: a dumpling made with Chinese textured soy keeps its origin [@FTR-32; @TIC-02; @TIC-13; @ORG-01] {VN-direct|Low}.
- **Price blends and plant milks against what incumbents already use.** Soy extension already pays at every annual hog price from 2019 to 2024. So a new extender has to beat hydrated soy (about USD 2.6 to 2.8 per kg of protein, our calculation), not pork [@HXE-01; @DIE-25; @TIC-12] {VN-direct|Low}. From 2027, drinks above 5 g sugar per 100 ml pay an 8% excise (10% from 2028) if plant milks count. The upper bound for the Vinasoy soy-milk business is about VND 384 to 480 billion a year, a strong reason for incumbents to reformulate (our calculation) [@FTR-01; @CON-26; @AIB-05] {VN-direct|Low}. Pea protein enters duty-free from China, France and Canada, so a domestic isolate has no tariff shelter either [@FTR-32] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Impact screens.** T1 spares about no animals and avoids about no carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e), because soy replaces soy. So it will not count towards climate-screened targets such as ADB Ventures' 80% strong climate impact [@AIS-22] {VN-adjacent|Medium}. Pitch it to development finance on the import bill, jobs and value added. It would replace about USD 16 to 23 million of imports a year by 2035 on the benchmark path, gross (our model, [[ch18-demand-sizing]]). Per tonne of protein supplied, a hybrid extender avoids about 57 t CO2e and spares about 540 animals. A canteen ingredient avoids about 45 t and spares 750 to 1,100 animals (our calculation) [@FUF-03; @FUF-04] {general|Low}. Climate or welfare money fits blends and canteen ingredients, not T1.
- **Look for offtake, not surveys.** Lenders and investors increasingly weigh offtake agreements and operating economics [@AIS-01; @AIS-15] {general|Medium}. T1 and plant-drink proteins (T10) can show these; consumer analogues cannot (our assessment). 74% of urban online respondents say they would buy plant-based meat at parity, but only 15% intend to eat less meat ([[ch13-consumers]]).

## Looking to 2050

- **Price two external questions.** First, whether imported protein comes under chronic stress: the feed behind domestic meat, and imported protein foods. Second, whether Asia's fermentation market stays open or falls under Chinese price dominance. T4 (cassava sugar hub), T5 (shared pilot fermentation) and T9 (koji and fungal foods) hold in all four 2050 worlds and thrive in two. High-value precision fermentation (T7) is a bet: scale it only when its signposts trip ([[ch23-scenarios-2050]]).
- **Cost curves are engineering estimates, not observed trends.** Fermentation industries have cut costs 13 to 20% per doubling of output ([[ch21-frontier-technology]]).
- **Cheaper capital is a first-order lever.** Cutting the capital charge from 12% to 9% does as much for fermentation protein as the cost learning expected by 2030 (our calculation). Concessional debt and guarantees matter more than grants ([[ch28-robust-moves]]).
- **Timing.** Second-hand fermentation tanks are a 2027 to 2030 window.
- **Seven no-regret moves pay off in any 2050:** a national protein balance; a new-food route with deadlines; shared food-grade pilot capacity; a protein-quality laboratory and price series; residue-carbon rules and a by-product atlas; climate-proof siting; and process-engineering skills ([[ch28-robust-moves]]).
- **A vision to debate, not a forecast.** By 2050, domestic makers supply most of the plant-protein ingredients that food makers now import. New routes provide about 67,000 to 172,000 t of protein a year. About 59,000 to 147,000 t of it is made in Vietnam (benchmark and stretch paths of our demand model) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. Meat stays the main protein; new protein adds a second source ([[ch24-vision-2050]]).
