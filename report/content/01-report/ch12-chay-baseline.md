# 12. The incumbent: chay, tofu and soy milk

**In one paragraph.** In Vietnamese usage *chay* is three things at once: a practice (not eating animal flesh on set days of the lunar month and at festivals), a cuisine (*cơm chay*, *bún chay*) and a product category (the frozen *thực phẩm chay* aisle). It is Vietnam's largest plant-protein eating occasion, and it is served by tofu, soy milk, mushrooms and cheap industrial *đồ chay* (traditional vegetarian food), not by modern analogues. No survey asks about chay days, but national diet surveillance caps them: on an ordinary day in late 2021, 7.9% of adults ate no meat, poultry or fish. Revealed signals are strong and regular: specialist sellers report two to three times normal sales in the seventh lunar month, and mainstream retail 20 to 40% more. On a stated assumption that about 30% of people keep 2 to 3 chay days a month, chay days are about 2.5% of all person-days (about a third of that ceiling; about 5% is the practical upper bound) and already avoid about 25,000 t of meat protein a year, as much as the Part IV alternative-protein scenario assumes for 2030 to 2035. Chay foods are cheaper than meat per kilogram but protein-poor, so they are expensive per gram of protein. Their trust problems are borax, microbes and unlabelled goods rather than hidden meat, and the national vegetarian standard is voluntary and broader than Buddhist chay. For frontier actors chay is a ready shelf, cold chain and set of buyers for a higher-protein ingredient, and the wrong place to count meat displacement.

## 12.1 How many people eat chay: unmeasured, but capped

- **The religious base.** Pew Research Center's face-to-face probability survey (n = 2,255, June to September 2023) found that 38% of Vietnamese adults identify as Buddhist, 48% report no religion and 86% offered food or drink to ancestors in the past year. It asked nothing about diet [@CHY-06] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}. Official counts of Buddhists differ threefold: 4.6 million in the 2019 census against more than 14 million registered followers in the 2023 government White Paper [@CHY-08; @CHY-07] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. Lay people who keep lunar chay days include many who say they have no religion.
- **The calendar.** The schedules described in academic and press sources are consistent: two days a month (the 1st and 15th of the lunar month, *mùng một* and *ngày rằm*), four days (adding the 14th and the last day), eight or ten days, and whole months, above all the 1st lunar month and the 7th, which holds the *Vu Lan* festival [@CHY-09; @CHY-10; @CHY-25; @CHY-26] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Every Vietnamese academic survey we found recruited at chay restaurants, pagodas or universities, so none gives a prevalence [@CHY-09; @CHY-10; @CHY-11] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.
- **A ceiling from national diet data.** No probability survey asks about chay, but one asks about diet. The Diet Quality Questionnaire (Gallup, 1,007 adults by mobile telephone, 13 November to 12 December 2021, an ordinary month with two principal chay days) found that 92.1% of adults had eaten meat, poultry or fish on the previous day, so 7.9% (95% interval 5.3 to 11.0%) had a flesh-free day (our calculation from the published share) [@APR-36; @BRD-01; @BRD-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. A chay day is flesh-free, so this caps chay-day person-days. Vietnam sits mid-region on this measure, below Thailand (9.8%, surveyed in 2023) and China (14.9%) and above the Philippines (5.8%) and Cambodia (3.3%); food lists differ, so the comparison is indicative [@APR-36; @BRD-03] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Lunar chay days do not make Vietnam an outlier in meat-free days. {{kn:kn-flesh-free-days}}
- **Online panels overstate it.** Rakuten Insight's online panel (n = 4,018, November 2021) found 86% had eaten "plant-based alternatives", the highest of 13 markets, and 10% said they eat only plant-based food [@CHY-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Vietnamese respondents count tofu, soy milk and *đồ chay* as plant-based, so these numbers measure familiarity with the incumbent, not demand for new products.
- **Rejected.** The widely repeated "about 10% of Vietnamese are vegetarian" has no survey behind it and we do not use it [@CHY-15; @CHY-18] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. National diet data contradict it: only 7.9% of adults (at most 11.0%) had a flesh-free day, and that share must hold chay keepers as well as any full-time vegetarians [@APR-36] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

The single cheapest piece of missing evidence in Part III is still a chay-day question in a probability survey ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]). A cheaper first step may exist: if Gallup holds the interview dates of the 2021 survey (not confirmed), the flesh-free share on recall days that fell on the lunar 1st or 15th can be compared with other days. About 65 recall days fall on the two principal chay days, so power is low: even a doubling would be only borderline detectable (our calculation) [@BRD-03] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 12.4 What chay buyers value, tolerate and fear

**Imitation meat is mainstream and religiously tolerated.** Monks quoted in Buddhist media call *giả mặn* (chay food that imitates meat) a skilful means that reduces killing; a minority of Buddhist writers say it keeps the mind attached to meat; we found no binding Sangha ruling against it [@CHY-53; @CHY-54; @CHY-55] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. One Ho Chi Minh City chay shop named *chả chay* (mock pork roll) and mushrooms as its best sellers in Vu Lan 2024 [@CHY-26] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. The authors of a 2019 southern study observe that periodic eaters prefer processed foods in their chay meals while strict vegetarians mostly eat unprocessed plant foods [@CHY-09] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. That is the key split: the periodic majority buys mock meat; the strict minority buys whole foods [@CHY-09; @CHY-26] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Two Hanoi sellers reported in 2026 that buyers are moving from "industrial" chay to mushrooms, lotus, beans and vegetables [@CHY-20; @CHY-21] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}; we log this as a possible regional difference or trend ([[app-r2-disagreements]]).

**The allium rule binds a minority.** Strict Buddhist cooking avoids the five pungent plants (*ngũ vị tân*: onion or scallion, chives, garlic and two small alliums). The Sangha newspaper's advisory panel says this binds people who have taken the Bodhisattva precepts, not lay people keeping lunar days [@CHY-52] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Mainstream industrial chay ignores it: in our re-read of the retail audit, at least 36% of chay-named products with an ingredient list name onion, garlic, shallot or leek, and about 5% (3 of 64) name egg or dairy [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed} ([[app-d3-retail-demand-signals]]). Allium-free and egg-free variants are a requirement for pagoda kitchens, offerings and strict buyers, not for the mass market.

**Trust is the weakest point, and the problem is safety, not hidden meat.** Unlabelled mock meat at VND 60,000 to 250,000 per kg is sold openly in Ho Chi Minh City markets; inspectors seized a tonne of untraceable *chả chay* in 2025 and 500 kg of Chinese textured soy without Vietnamese labels, transported as "chay spare ribs", in 2022 [@CHY-23; @CHY-59; @CHY-60] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. The recurring cases are about safety: borax in *chả đòn chay* (chay pork roll; 6,573 kg seized in An Giang in 2022) and in tofu skin, a botulism case in 2020, and expert warnings about additives, dyes and reused oil in the main 2026 exposé [@TRU-16; @TRU-14; @TRU-04; @LBL-23; @CHY-23] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Meat in chay is a latent rumour, not a live scandal. A Can Tho University team built a DNA test for meat in vegetarian food, citing "many cases" of meat found in chay foods, but reported no prevalence data [@CHY-61] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. We found no press or official report confirming meat in a Vietnamese chay product from 2015 to 2026. The only official case is an April 2021 seizure of more than 3 t of *chả đòn chay* in An Giang on suspicion of meat and fish, with no published result; meat-in-chay stories circulate as a relayed 2015 Taiwanese scandal and as social-media videos [@TRU-18; @TRU-01; @TRU-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-meat-in-chay-cases}}

**Inspections check papers, not meat.** After the 2020 botulism case, Hanoi inspected 93 chay facilities and fined 14 a total of VND 55 million; the checks covered hygiene, origin papers and storage, and no meat content was reported. We found no official report of meat-DNA testing of chay [@LBL-23; @LBL-24] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. A test is close: in February 2025 the state testing centre QUATEST 3 announced a validated real-time PCR screen for vertebrate DNA (tested on 86 species, detection limit 0.001%), to be offered once its accreditation registration is approved [@TRU-24] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

> **Correction.** In v0.6 this said QUATEST 3 had sold a PCR screen for 86 animal species since February 2025; the source says the method was validated then and will be offered only once registration is approved, and the 86 species are its test panel.

**Certification is voluntary and broader than Buddhist chay.** The national standard TCVN 13625:2023 (equivalent to ISO 23662:2021) defines vegetarian and vegan claims, but its "vegetarian" allows egg, milk and honey and it has no allium rule, so its chay is broader than the chay that pagoda kitchens buy. V-Label is offered through SGS; we found no national or Buddhist chay certification in use [@LBL-15; @CHY-57; @CHY-58] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. A false chay claim is already unlawful, because labels must be truthful and names must not mislead about composition [@LBL-06; @LBL-09] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

## 12.5 Price: cheap per kilogram, dear per gram of protein

Street chay costs VND 15,000 (2026) to 30,000 (2024) a plate against VND 30,000 to 35,000 for a meat rice plate in Ho Chi Minh City in 2026 [@CHY-38; @CHY-42; @CHY-40] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. In modern retail, Vissan's frozen chay spring roll (*chả giò*) sold 38% below its meat version [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}; the other same-brand pairs we found are low confidence and range from 52% below to 19% above the meat version [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Premium chay dining costs VND 239,000 to 379,000 a head at a premium buffet (2026) and VND 350,000 to 400,000 a bill at a well-known restaurant (2022) [@CHY-33; @CHY-37] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Per gram of protein the picture reverses. With a median of about 4.3 g of protein per 100 g, filled chay wrappers cost a median VND 369,000 per 100 g of protein and market mock meat roughly VND 120,000 to 200,000 (assuming 4.5 to 5 g of protein), against VND 66,000 for tofu on label values [@FORM-01; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. The buyer does not see this, because chay is bought per kilogram and per plate. It defines the headroom for a better ingredient: at today's shelf price, a *chả* (sausage or loaf) with 12 g of protein per 100 g would deliver protein at roughly VND 50,000 to 75,000 per 100 g, in tofu's range (our calculation) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 12.6 A channel, but not a displacement route

**Where chay is a ready channel.** Signed frozen chay sections with several brands exist in the modern chains we audited, and markets carry fresh and loose chay [@FORM-01; @CHY-26; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Buyers already pay for chay chicken, fish, shrimp, ham, sausages and spare ribs [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}, so a higher-protein analogue asks no one to accept a new idea. Retailers already commission own-label chay from Vietnamese makers, so a better ingredient can reach the shelf through a co-packer [@CPC-15; @AIB-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Demand is predictable, with fortnightly peaks and two big months a year. And the high-protein chay products already rely on imported Chinese textured soy or gluten ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]), so a domestic replacement is a purchasing decision by manufacturers, not a behaviour change by consumers {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

**Where it resists.** Price ceilings are low, and in 2026 one Hanoi restaurant reported offering-tray buying power falling as costs rose [@CHY-21; @CHY-38] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Some sellers report a shift away from processed ("industrial") chay [@CHY-20; @CHY-21] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. The strict segment wants whole plants [@CHY-09; @CHY-20; @CHY-21] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. Offering trays are bought for presentation and ingredients (our reading: not protein) [@CHY-24] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. Chay manufacturers and shop owners, in the interviews we found, talk about price, convenience, freshness, safety and natural ingredients; none asked for more protein.

**The displacement arithmetic.** On a stated assumption that 30% of people keep 2.5 chay days a month, chay days are about 2.5% of all person-days (our assumption and calculation; no survey measures either input) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. That uses about a third of the 7.9% flesh-free ceiling from national diet data. The illustrative range we gave earlier (20 to 50% keeping 2 to 4 days, 1.3 to 6.7%) overreaches at the top: 6.7% would need 85% of all flesh-free days to be chay days and exceeds the survey's lower confidence bound of 5.3%, so about 5% of person-days is the practical upper bound (our calculation) [@APR-36; @BRD-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. Multiplied by average meat protein per person-day, existing chay days avoid about 25,000 t of meat protein a year in 2025 (our calculation, [[app-d8-demand-model]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}. That is the same order as the Part IV S-ALT scenario, which assumes 1% of meat protein (about 11,800 t) replaced by plant-based and fermented foods in 2030 and 3% (about 39,000 t) in 2035 ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]). {{kn:kn-chay-days-share}}

Three consequences follow {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}:

1. Chay days are already in the meat baseline. A novel protein that wins chay-day protein replaces tofu, imported textured soy, gluten and mushrooms. It improves nutrition and substitutes imports; it displaces no meat. Retailer ratio methods that count protein-rich foods by weight, as WWF's 2025 method does, would score protein-poor chay foods at face value, so impact should be counted in protein, not kilograms [@AFN-34] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
2. Meat displacement needs either more chay occasions (a third day a month, chay breakfasts, canteen chay days) or entry into ordinary meals. [[ch18-demand-sizing]] shows that added chay days are, on paper, the cheapest route to the S-ALT volumes. Organised promotion of plant-based eating is small: Humane World for Animals included Viet Nam in its 2025 plant-based menu training (370 or more chefs and 570 or more nutritionists across seven countries), and Dharma Voices for Animals runs a pagoda-based programme at Phap Van Pagoda in Hanoi [@AFN-23; @AFN-36] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
3. We found no evidence on compensation, that is, whether people who eat chay on the 1st and 15th eat more meat on other days.

## 12.7 What this means for the frontier

- **Sell an ingredient first.** The winning first product is a protein base (textured, fungal or blended) that existing chay makers and retailers' own-label co-packers put into *chả*, *giò*, *nem*, dumplings and spring rolls.
- **Design target.** Lift product protein from 4 to 5 g to 10 to 12 g per 100 g while keeping the shelf price inside today's range (supermarket about VND 56,000 to 190,000 per kg for filled wrappers; market VND 60,000 to 90,000 per kg for *giò* and *nem*) [@FORM-01; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. At 10 g per 100 g a food meets the "high protein" (*cao đạm*) condition of the voluntary standard TCVN 14429:2025; of 43 audited plant-protein foods, the 7 that already meet it are all frozen analogues, and only 6 of 18 filled wrappers reach even the 5 g "source" level [@LBL-03; @FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed} ([[ch13-consumers]]).
- **Build for the calendar.** At retail, plan capacity and frozen stock for two to three times normal demand in the 1st and 7th lunar months [@CHY-16] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. An ingredient maker should plan for a smaller swing: China-reported shipments run about a third higher in the two months before the Vu Lan month and fall deeply in the *Tết* month, though Vietnam's own import records show no pre-Vu Lan rise [@BRD-05; @BRD-06; @BRD-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Lead with safety and a full label.** A registered maker, a full label, few additives and a short ingredient list meet the category's live trust problems (borax, microbes, unlabelled goods). "Verified meat-free" is a secondary reassurance, which a PCR screen could supply once QUATEST 3 offers it. Avoid "lab", "future of meat" and environmental claims as the main message.
- **Offer allium-free and egg-free variants** for pagoda kitchens and strict buyers, clearly labelled.

**For each reader.**
- **Startups:** your first customers are chay makers and the co-packers of retailers' own-label chay; price per kilogram of finished product, not per gram of protein, is what their buyers see.
- **Investors:** underwrite on per-kilogram price parity with existing *đồ chay* and repeat orders from manufacturers; demand is seasonal and price sensitive.
- **Manufacturers:** a declared protein content would be new on the chay shelf. The claim conditions are set by TCVN 14429:2025 and need no registration; print the number ("10 g đạm/100 g"), because *giàu đạm* (rich in protein) is not a defined term. Test reformulations at the Vu Lan and first-month peaks, when trial is highest.
- **Research bodies:** publish a composition table for chay foods and develop allium-free flavour systems. Test borax, preservatives, microbes and protein first, and add animal DNA by asking QUATEST 3 about its new animal-DNA screen. Publish with the Vietnam Food Administration (VFA), publish the methods and do not name small makers: a 2016 consumer-association survey of fish sauce (101 of more than 150 samples flagged) led to delistings, a Ministry of Health rebuttal and a backlash against the testers [@TRU-24; @TRU-19] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Policy makers:** TCVN 13625:2023 already defines vegetarian and vegan claims, and false chay claims are already unlawful. Ask VFA to state that an unqualified *chay* or *thuần chay* (vegan) claim means the vegan criteria, with egg or milk named in a qualifier and allium-free left as a voluntary claim. The new Food Safety Law, which the National Assembly takes first comments on in October 2026 and considers for passage in May 2027, and its decree are the window [@APR-07; @LBL-15] {VN-direct|Medium}.
