# 12. The incumbent: chay, tofu and soy milk

**In brief.**

- **Chay is Vietnam's largest plant-protein eating occasion.** *Chay* is first a practice, not eating animal flesh on set days of the lunar month. It is also a cuisine and a shop category. Tofu, soy milk, mushrooms and cheap factory-made *đồ chay* (vegetarian food) serve it, not modern meat copies.
- **No survey counts chay days, but national diet data cap them.** On an ordinary day in 2021, 7.9% of adults ate no meat, poultry or fish.
- **Sales follow the lunar calendar,** doubling in specialist shops in the seventh lunar month.
- **Chay days already avoid about 2.5% of meat protein,** as much as Part IV's alternative-protein scenario assumes for 2030 to 2035. That saving is already in the meat baseline.
- **Chay foods are cheap per kilogram but low in protein.** Their trust problems are borax, microbes and unlabelled goods, not hidden meat.
- **For new protein makers, chay is a ready shelf and set of buyers** for a higher-protein ingredient. It is the wrong place to count less meat.

---

## 12.1 How many people eat chay: not measured, but capped

- **The religious base.** About 38% of Vietnamese adults identify as Buddhist, and 48% report no religion [@CHY-06] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}. Many people with no religion also keep lunar chay days.
- **The calendar.** People keep two chay days a month (the 1st and 15th of the lunar month), four, or more. Some keep whole months, above all the 1st and the 7th, which holds the *Vu Lan* festival for parents and ancestors [@CHY-09; @CHY-10; @CHY-25; @CHY-26] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Every survey we found recruited at chay restaurants, pagodas or universities, so none measures how many people keep chay [@CHY-11] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.
- **A ceiling from national diet data.** In a 2021 random-sample survey, 92.1% of adults had eaten meat, poultry or fish the previous day. So 7.9% had a flesh-free day (95% range 5.3% to 11.0%; our calculation) [@APR-36; @BRD-01; @BRD-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. A chay day is flesh-free, so this caps chay days as a share of all days. {{kn:kn-flesh-free-days}}
- **Vietnam is mid-region.** It sits below Thailand (9.8%) and China (14.9%) on meat-free days, and above the Philippines and Cambodia [@APR-36; @BRD-03] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Online panels overstate it.** In one online panel, 86% of Vietnamese said they had eaten "plant-based alternatives" [@CHY-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. They count tofu and soy milk, so this measures familiarity with the incumbent, not demand for new products.
- **Rejected: "about 10% are vegetarian".** No survey is behind this widely repeated claim, and national diet data contradict it [@CHY-15; @CHY-18; @APR-36] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}.

The cheapest gap to fill in Part III is a chay-day question in a random-sample survey ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).

## 12.2 Chay sales follow the lunar calendar

Chay demand roughly doubles in specialist shops in the 7th lunar month, and rises 20% to 40% in supermarkets. It moves the prices of mushrooms, whose supply cannot grow quickly, but not of tofu. **Two lessons for design.** Tofu stays the price anchor even at the peak. And mushroom prices jump, which opens room for a shelf-stable protein that holds its price [@CHY-21; @CHY-19; @CHY-22] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.

**The calendar reaches ingredient orders too.** Chinese shipments of textured protein to Vietnam rise by about a third before the Vu Lan month, while shipments to other countries stay flat [@BRD-05; @BRD-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. So part of the imported textured protein goes into chay. Vietnam's own import records do not show the rise [@BRD-06] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

## 12.4 Chay buyers accept mock meat; their worry is safety

**Mock meat is mainstream and religiously tolerated.** Monks quoted in Buddhist media call chay that imitates meat a "skilful means" that reduces killing. We found no ruling against it from the national Buddhist organisation [@CHY-53; @CHY-54; @CHY-55; @OIC-17] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. The majority, who eat chay on set days, buy mock meat; the strict minority buys whole plant foods [@CHY-09; @CHY-26] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Some Hanoi sellers report a shift towards mushrooms and vegetables [@CHY-20; @CHY-21] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}.

**The onion rule binds a minority.** Strict Buddhist cooking avoids onion, garlic and related plants. This binds people who have taken certain vows, not lay people keeping lunar days [@CHY-52; @OIC-18] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. At least 36% of factory chay products list onion, garlic, shallot or leek, and about 5% list egg or dairy [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Pagoda kitchens and strict buyers need variants without them; the mass market does not.

**Trust is the weakest point, and the problem is safety, not hidden meat.** Unlabelled mock meat is sold openly in Ho Chi Minh City markets, and inspectors have seized tonnes of untraceable chay [@CHY-23; @CHY-59; @CHY-60] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. The recurring cases involve borax, a banned preservative, in chay pork roll and tofu skin; one case of severe food poisoning; and warnings about dyes and reused oil [@TRU-16; @TRU-14; @TRU-04; @LBL-23; @CHY-23] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Meat in chay is a rumour, not a live scandal.** We found no confirmed report of meat in a Vietnamese chay product from 2015 to 2026 [@CHY-61; @TRU-18; @TRU-01; @TRU-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Inspections check hygiene and papers, not meat content [@LBL-23; @LBL-24] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. A state laboratory has validated a DNA screen for animal content, to be offered once it is accredited [@TRU-24] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-meat-in-chay-cases}}

**Certification is voluntary and broader than Buddhist chay.** The national vegetarian standard allows egg, milk and honey, and has no onion rule. We found no national or Buddhist chay certification in use [@LBL-15; @CHY-57; @CHY-58] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. A false chay claim is already unlawful, because names must not mislead [@LBL-06; @LBL-09] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

## 12.5 Chay is cheap per kilogram but expensive per gram of protein

**Chay is cheap per plate.** Street chay costs VND 15,000 to 30,000 a plate, against VND 29,000 to 35,000 for a meat rice plate [@CHY-38; @CHY-42; @CHY-40] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Vissan's frozen chay spring rolls sell 38% below its meat version [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

**But it is dear per gram of protein.** Filled chay wrappers hold about 4.3 g of protein per 100 g, so their protein costs a median VND 369,000 per 100 g. Tofu costs VND 66,000 [@FORM-01; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

Buyers do not see this, because they buy per kilogram and per plate. But it shows the room for a better ingredient. At today's shelf price, a chay loaf with 12 g of protein per 100 g would supply protein at tofu's price (our calculation) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 12.6 Chay is a ready channel, but not a route to less meat

**Where chay is a ready channel.**

- Supermarkets have frozen chay sections with several brands, and markets sell fresh chay [@FORM-01; @CHY-26; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- Buyers already pay for chay chicken, fish, shrimp, ham and sausages [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. So a higher-protein version asks no one to accept a new idea.
- Retailers already commission store-brand chay from Vietnamese co-packers [@CPC-15; @AIB-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- Demand is predictable, with peaks every two weeks and two big months a year.
- High-protein chay already uses imported Chinese textured soy or gluten ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]). So a domestic replacement is a buying decision by makers, not a change in what consumers do {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

**Where it resists.** Price ceilings are low, some sellers report a shift away from processed chay, and strict buyers want whole plants [@CHY-21; @CHY-38; @CHY-20; @CHY-09] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. None of the chay makers or shop owners we found asked for more protein.

**The meat arithmetic.** Existing chay days avoid about 25,000 t of meat protein a year (our calculation) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}. It rests on an assumption that 30% of people keep 2.5 chay days a month, so chay days are about 2.5% of all days. No survey measures either input {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. The national diet data put a practical upper bound at about 5% [@APR-36; @BRD-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. The 25,000 t is the same order as Part IV's alternative-protein scenario for 2030 to 2035 ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]). {{kn:kn-chay-days-share}}

Three consequences follow {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}:

1. **Chay days are already in the meat baseline.** A new protein that wins chay-day meals replaces tofu, imported soy and mushrooms. It improves nutrition and replaces imports, but it displaces no meat. Count any effect in protein, not kilograms [@AFN-34] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
2. **Displacing meat needs more chay occasions,** such as a third chay day a month or canteen chay days. On paper, this is the cheapest route to less meat ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]). Campaigns for plant-based eating in Vietnam are small [@AFN-23; @AFN-36] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
3. **We found no evidence on whether chay keepers eat more meat on other days.**

## 12.7 What this means for new protein

- **Sell an ingredient first.** The likeliest first winner is a protein base, textured, fungal or blended, that chay makers and co-packers put into rolls, dumplings and spring rolls.
- **Design target.** Raise protein from 4 to 5 g to 10 to 12 g per 100 g, and keep the shelf price inside today's range [@FORM-01; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. At 10 g a food can claim "high protein" (*cao đạm*). Today only frozen meat copies reach that, and only a third of filled wrappers reach even 5 g [@LBL-03; @FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed} ([[ch13-consumers]]).
- **Build for the calendar.** Plan stock for two to three times normal demand in the 1st and 7th lunar months [@CHY-16] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.
- **Lead with safety and a full label.** A registered maker, a full label and a short ingredient list answer the category's trust problems. Do not lead with "lab", "future of meat" or environmental claims.
- **Offer onion-free and egg-free variants** for pagoda kitchens and strict buyers.

**For each reader.**

- **Startups:** your first customers are chay makers and store-brand co-packers. They see the price per kilogram, not per gram of protein.
- **Investors:** underwrite on price per kilogram against existing chay, and on repeat orders from makers. Demand is seasonal and price sensitive.
- **Manufacturers:** print the protein number, for example "10 g đạm/100 g" (10 g of protein per 100 g). Test new recipes at the lunar peaks, when most buyers try products.
- **Researchers:** publish a composition table for chay foods, and test for borax, preservatives and microbes. Work with the food safety authority and do not name small makers; a 2016 fish sauce survey led to a backlash against the testers [@TRU-24; @TRU-19] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Policy makers:** ask the food safety authority to state that an unqualified *chay* or *thuần chay* (vegan) claim means vegan. Time safety inspections before the 1st and 7th lunar months [@APR-07; @LBL-15] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **International organisations and funders:** fund the cheapest evidence first: a chay-day question in a national survey, and a chay safety and composition survey. Count effects only for added chay occasions, in protein ([[ch18-demand-sizing]], [[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).
