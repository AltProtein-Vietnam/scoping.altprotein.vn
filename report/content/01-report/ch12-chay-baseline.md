# 12. The incumbent: chay, tofu and soy milk

**In brief.**

- **Chay is Vietnam's largest plant-protein eating occasion.** In Vietnamese usage *chay* is first a practice: not eating animal flesh on set days of the lunar month and at festivals. It is also a cuisine (*cơm chay*, chay rice dishes; *bún chay*, chay noodle dishes) and a product category (the frozen *thực phẩm chay*, or chay foods, aisle). Tofu, soy milk, mushrooms and cheap industrial *đồ chay* (traditional vegetarian food) serve it, not modern analogues.
- **No survey asks about chay days, but national diet data set a ceiling.** On an ordinary day in late 2021, 7.9% of adults ate no meat, poultry or fish.
- **Sales follow the lunar calendar.** Specialist sellers report two to three times normal sales in the seventh lunar month, and mainstream retail 20 to 40% more.
- **Chay days already avoid meat.** On a stated assumption that about 30% of people keep 2 to 3 chay days a month, chay days are about 2.5% of all person-days. That is about a third of the ceiling; about 5% is the practical upper bound. Existing chay days already avoid about 25,000 t of meat protein a year, as much as the Part IV alternative-protein scenario assumes for 2030 to 2035.
- **Chay foods are cheap per kilogram but low in protein,** so they are expensive per gram of protein. Their trust problems are borax, microbes and unlabelled goods, not hidden meat. The national vegetarian standard is voluntary and broader than Buddhist chay.
- **For makers of new protein, chay is a ready shelf, cold chain and set of buyers** for a higher-protein ingredient. It is the wrong place to count meat displacement.

## 12.1 How many people eat chay: not measured, but capped

- **The religious base.** A Pew Research Center survey found that 38% of Vietnamese adults identify as Buddhist and 48% report no religion. Most (86%) offered food or drink to ancestors in the past year. It was a face-to-face survey of a random sample (n = 2,255, June to September 2023), and it asked nothing about diet [@CHY-06] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}. Official counts of Buddhists differ threefold: 4.6 million in the 2019 census against more than 14 million registered followers in the 2023 government White Paper [@CHY-08; @CHY-07] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. Lay people who keep lunar chay days include many who say they have no religion.
- **The calendar.** Academic and press sources describe the same schedules. People keep two days a month (the 1st and 15th of the lunar month, *mùng một* and *ngày rằm*), four days (adding the 14th and the last day), or eight or ten days. Some keep whole months, above all the 1st lunar month and the 7th, which holds the *Vu Lan* festival for parents and ancestors [@CHY-09; @CHY-10; @CHY-25; @CHY-26] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Every Vietnamese academic survey we found recruited at chay restaurants, pagodas or universities, so none measures how many people keep chay [@CHY-09; @CHY-10; @CHY-11] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.
- **A ceiling from national diet data.** No random-sample survey asks about chay, but one asks about diet. In the Diet Quality Questionnaire, 92.1% of adults had eaten meat, poultry or fish on the previous day. So 7.9% (95% interval 5.3 to 11.0%) had a flesh-free day (our calculation from the published share). Gallup surveyed 1,007 adults by mobile telephone from 13 November to 12 December 2021, an ordinary month with two principal chay days [@APR-36; @BRD-01; @BRD-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. A chay day is flesh-free, so this share caps chay days as a share of all person-days (one person on one day). {{kn:kn-flesh-free-days}}
- **Vietnam is mid-region on meat-free days.** It sits below Thailand (9.8%, surveyed in 2023) and China (14.9%), and above the Philippines (5.8%) and Cambodia (3.3%). Food lists differ, so the comparison is indicative [@APR-36; @BRD-03] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Lunar chay days do not make Vietnam an outlier in meat-free days.
- **Online panels overstate it.** Rakuten Insight's online panel (n = 4,018, November 2021) found 86% had eaten "plant-based alternatives", the highest of 13 markets. In the same panel, 10% said they eat only plant-based food [@CHY-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Vietnamese respondents count tofu, soy milk and *đồ chay* as plant-based. So these numbers measure familiarity with the incumbent, not demand for new products.
- **Rejected: "about 10% are vegetarian".** We do not use this widely repeated claim, because no survey is behind it [@CHY-15; @CHY-18] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. National diet data contradict it. Only 7.9% of adults (at most 11.0%) had a flesh-free day, and that share has to include chay keepers as well as any full-time vegetarians [@APR-36] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

The cheapest gap to fill in Part III is still a chay-day question in a random-sample survey ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]). A cheaper first step may exist. Each person in the 2021 survey reported on the previous day (their recall day). If Gallup holds the interview dates (not confirmed), the flesh-free share on recall days that fell on the lunar 1st or 15th can be compared with other days. Only about 65 recall days fall on the two principal chay days, so the test is weak: even a doubling would be only borderline detectable, and not detectable once the survey's design effect is allowed for (our calculation; [[app-d1-diet-chay-prices]]) [@BRD-03] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 12.4 Chay buyers accept mock meat; their worry is safety

**Imitation meat is mainstream and religiously tolerated.** Monks quoted in Buddhist media call *giả mặn* (chay food that imitates meat) a "skilful means" that reduces killing. A minority of Buddhist writers say it keeps the mind attached to meat. We found no binding ruling against it from the Vietnam Buddhist Sangha (*Giáo hội Phật giáo Việt Nam*), the national Buddhist organisation [@CHY-53; @CHY-54; @CHY-55; @OIC-17] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. One Ho Chi Minh City chay shop named *chả chay* (mock pork roll) and mushrooms as its best sellers in Vu Lan 2024 [@CHY-26] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.

The authors of a 2019 southern study observe that periodic eaters prefer processed foods in their chay meals, while strict vegetarians mostly eat unprocessed plant foods [@CHY-09] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. That is the main split: the majority, who eat chay on set days, buy mock meat; the strict minority buys whole foods [@CHY-09; @CHY-26] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Two Hanoi sellers reported in 2026 that buyers are moving from "industrial" chay to mushrooms, lotus, beans and vegetables [@CHY-20; @CHY-21] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. We log this as a possible regional difference or trend ([[app-r2-disagreements]]).

**The allium rule binds a minority.** Strict Buddhist cooking avoids the five pungent plants of the onion family (*ngũ vị tân*: onion or scallion, chives, garlic and two small alliums). The advisory panel of Giac Ngo, the newspaper of the Vietnam Buddhist Sangha in Ho Chi Minh City, says this binds people who have taken the Bodhisattva precepts (a set of vows), not lay people keeping lunar days [@CHY-52; @OIC-18] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.

Mainstream industrial chay ignores the allium rule. In our re-read of the retail audit, at least 36% of chay-named products with an ingredient list name onion, garlic, shallot or leek. About 5% (3 of 64) name egg or dairy [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed} ([[app-d3-retail-demand-signals]]). Pagoda kitchens, offerings and strict buyers need allium-free and egg-free variants; the mass market does not.

**Trust is the weakest point, and the problem is safety, not hidden meat.** Unlabelled mock meat at VND 60,000 to 250,000 per kg is sold openly in Ho Chi Minh City markets. Inspectors seized a tonne of untraceable *chả chay* in 2025 and 500 kg of Chinese textured soy without Vietnamese labels, transported as "chay spare ribs", in 2022 [@CHY-23; @CHY-59; @CHY-60] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.

The recurring cases are about safety. Borax turned up in *chả đòn chay* (chay pork roll; 6,573 kg seized in An Giang in 2022) and in tofu skin. There was a botulism (severe food poisoning) case in 2020. And the main 2026 press exposé carried expert warnings about additives, dyes and reused oil [@TRU-16; @TRU-14; @TRU-04; @LBL-23; @CHY-23] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Meat in chay is a background rumour, not a live scandal.** A Can Tho University team built a DNA test for meat in vegetarian food. It cited "many cases" of meat found in chay foods but reported no data on how common it is [@CHY-61] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. We found no press or official report confirming meat in a Vietnamese chay product from 2015 to 2026. The only official case is an April 2021 seizure of more than 3 t of *chả đòn chay* in An Giang on suspicion of meat and fish, with no published result. Meat-in-chay stories circulate as a relayed 2015 Taiwanese scandal and as social-media videos [@TRU-18; @TRU-01; @TRU-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-meat-in-chay-cases}}

**Inspections check papers, not meat.** After the 2020 botulism case, Hanoi inspected 93 chay facilities and fined 14 of them a total of VND 55 million. The checks covered hygiene, origin papers and storage, and reported no meat content. We found no official report of meat-DNA testing of chay [@LBL-23; @LBL-24] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

A test is close. In February 2025 the state testing centre QUATEST 3 announced a validated screen for vertebrate DNA using real-time PCR (polymerase chain reaction, a standard DNA test). It was tested on 86 species, has a detection limit of 0.001%, and will be offered once its accreditation registration is approved [@TRU-24] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

> **Correction.** In v0.6 this said QUATEST 3 had sold a PCR screen for 86 animal species since February 2025; the source says the method was validated then and will be offered only once registration is approved, and the 86 species are its test panel.

**Certification is voluntary and broader than Buddhist chay.** The national standard TCVN 13625:2023 (a voluntary Vietnamese standard, equivalent to ISO 23662:2021) defines vegetarian and vegan claims. Its "vegetarian" allows egg, milk and honey, and it has no allium rule, so its chay is broader than the chay that pagoda kitchens buy. V-Label, an international vegetarian and vegan label, is offered through the testing firm SGS. We found no national or Buddhist chay certification in use [@LBL-15; @CHY-57; @CHY-58] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. A false chay claim is already unlawful, because labels must be truthful and names must not mislead about composition [@LBL-06; @LBL-09] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

## 12.5 Chay is cheap per kilogram but expensive per gram of protein

Street chay costs VND 15,000 (2026) to 30,000 (2024) a plate, against VND 29,000 to 35,000 for a meat rice plate in Ho Chi Minh City in 2026 [@CHY-38; @CHY-42; @CHY-40] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. In modern retail, Vissan's frozen chay spring roll (*chả giò*) sold 38% below its meat version [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. The other same-brand pairs we found are low confidence and range from 52% below to 19% above the meat version [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Premium chay dining costs VND 239,000 to 379,000 a head at a premium buffet (2026) and VND 350,000 to 400,000 a bill at a well-known restaurant (2022) [@CHY-33; @CHY-37] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Per gram of protein the picture reverses. Filled chay wrappers have a median of about 4.3 g of protein per 100 g, so they cost a median VND 369,000 per 100 g of protein. Market mock meat costs roughly VND 120,000 to 200,000 (assuming 4.5 to 5 g of protein), against VND 66,000 for tofu on label values [@FORM-01; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

Buyers do not see this, because they buy chay per kilogram and per plate. But it shows the room for a better ingredient. At today's shelf price, a *chả* (sausage or loaf) with 12 g of protein per 100 g would supply protein at roughly VND 50,000 to 75,000 per 100 g, in tofu's range (our calculation) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 12.6 Chay is a ready channel, but not a route to less meat

**Where chay is a ready channel.**

- The modern chains we audited have signed frozen chay sections with several brands, and markets carry fresh and loose chay [@FORM-01; @CHY-26; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- Buyers already pay for chay chicken, fish, shrimp, ham, sausages and spare ribs [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. So a higher-protein analogue asks no one to accept a new idea.
- Retailers already commission own-label chay from Vietnamese makers. So a better ingredient can reach the shelf through a co-packer, a maker that produces goods under another firm's brand [@CPC-15; @AIB-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- Demand is predictable, with peaks every two weeks and two big months a year.
- The high-protein chay products already use imported Chinese textured soy or gluten ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]). So a domestic replacement is a buying decision by manufacturers, not a change in what consumers do {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

**Where it resists.**

- Price ceilings are low. In 2026 one Hanoi restaurant reported that buying power for offering trays fell as costs rose [@CHY-21; @CHY-38] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.
- Some sellers report a shift away from processed ("industrial") chay [@CHY-20; @CHY-21] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}.
- The strict segment wants whole plants [@CHY-09; @CHY-20; @CHY-21] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}.
- People buy offering trays for presentation and ingredients (our reading: not protein) [@CHY-24] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}.
- In the interviews we found, chay manufacturers and shop owners talk about price, convenience, freshness, safety and natural ingredients. None asked for more protein.

**The meat-displacement arithmetic.** Existing chay days avoid about 25,000 t of meat protein a year in 2025 (our calculation, [[app-d8-demand-model]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}. The steps are:

- **Share of days.** On a stated assumption that 30% of people keep 2.5 chay days a month, chay days are about 2.5% of all person-days (our assumption and calculation; no survey measures either input) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. That uses about a third of the 7.9% flesh-free ceiling from national diet data.
- **Upper bound.** The illustrative range we gave earlier (20 to 50% keeping 2 to 4 days, 1.3 to 6.7%) overreaches at the top. A share of 6.7% would need 85% of all flesh-free days to be chay days, and it exceeds the lower end of the survey's 95% interval (5.3%). So about 5% of person-days is the practical upper bound (our calculation) [@APR-36; @BRD-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
- **Protein.** The share of chay days multiplied by average meat protein per person-day gives the 25,000 t.
- **Comparison.** That is the same order as the large alternative-protein scenario of Part IV (S-ALT). S-ALT assumes that plant-based and fermented foods replace 1% of meat protein (about 11,800 t) in 2030 and 3% (about 39,000 t) in 2035 ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]). {{kn:kn-chay-days-share}}

Three consequences follow {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}:

1. **Chay days are already in the meat baseline.** A new protein that wins chay-day protein replaces tofu, imported textured soy, gluten and mushrooms. It improves nutrition and replaces imports, but it displaces no meat. Retailer ratio methods that count protein-rich foods by weight, as WWF's 2025 method does, would score protein-poor chay foods at face value. So count the effect in protein, not kilograms [@AFN-34] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
2. **Displacing meat needs more chay occasions or entry into ordinary meals.** More occasions could mean a third chay day a month, chay breakfasts or canteen chay days. [[ch18-demand-sizing]] shows that added chay days are, on paper, the cheapest route to the S-ALT volumes. Organised campaigns for plant-based eating are small. Humane World for Animals included Viet Nam in its 2025 plant-based menu training, which reached 370 or more chefs and 570 or more nutritionists across seven countries. Dharma Voices for Animals runs a pagoda-based programme at Phap Van Pagoda in Hanoi [@AFN-23; @AFN-36] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
3. **We found no evidence on compensation:** whether people who eat chay on the 1st and 15th eat more meat on other days.

## 12.7 What this means for new protein

- **Sell an ingredient first.** The first product most likely to win is a protein base (textured, fungal or blended). Existing chay makers and the co-packers of retailers' own-label chay would put it into *chả*, *giò* (meat-paste rolls), *nem* (fried or fermented rolls), dumplings and spring rolls.
- **Design target.** Raise product protein from 4 to 5 g to 10 to 12 g per 100 g, and keep the shelf price inside today's range. That range is about VND 56,000 to 190,000 per kg for filled wrappers in supermarkets, and VND 60,000 to 90,000 per kg for *giò* and *nem* in markets [@FORM-01; @CHY-20] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. At 10 g per 100 g a food meets the "high protein" (*cao đạm*) condition of the voluntary standard TCVN 14429:2025. Of 43 audited plant-protein foods, the 7 that already meet it are all frozen analogues. Only 6 of 18 filled wrappers reach even the 5 g level for a "source" of protein claim [@LBL-03; @FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed} ([[ch13-consumers]]).
- **Build for the calendar.** At retail, plan capacity and frozen stock for two to three times normal demand in the 1st and 7th lunar months [@CHY-16] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. An ingredient maker should plan for a smaller swing. China-reported shipments run about a third higher in the two months before the Vu Lan month and fall deeply in the *Tết* month. Vietnam's own import records show no rise before Vu Lan [@BRD-05; @BRD-06; @BRD-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Lead with safety and a full label.** A registered maker, a full label, few additives and a short ingredient list answer the category's current trust problems: borax, microbes and unlabelled goods. "Verified meat-free" is a secondary reassurance, which a PCR screen could supply once QUATEST 3 offers it. Do not lead with "lab", "future of meat" or environmental claims.
- **Offer allium-free and egg-free variants** for pagoda kitchens and strict buyers, clearly labelled.

**For each reader.**

- **Startups:** your first customers are chay makers and the co-packers of retailers' own-label chay. Their buyers see the price per kilogram of finished product, not per gram of protein.
- **Investors:** underwrite on per-kilogram price parity with existing *đồ chay* and on repeat orders from manufacturers. Demand is seasonal and price sensitive.
- **Manufacturers:** a declared protein content would be new on the chay shelf. TCVN 14429:2025 sets the claim conditions, and the claims need no registration. Print the number ("10 g đạm/100 g", that is, 10 g of protein per 100 g), because *giàu đạm* (rich in protein) is not a defined term. Test new recipes at the Vu Lan and first-month peaks, when the most buyers try products.
- **Research bodies:** publish a composition table for chay foods and develop allium-free flavour systems. Test for borax, preservatives, microbes and protein first, then add animal DNA by asking QUATEST 3 about its new screen. Publish with the Vietnam Food Administration (VFA), publish the methods and do not name small makers. A 2016 consumer-association survey of fish sauce (101 of more than 150 samples flagged) led to delistings, a Ministry of Health rebuttal and a backlash against the testers [@TRU-24; @TRU-19] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Policy makers:** TCVN 13625:2023 already defines vegetarian and vegan claims, and false chay claims are already unlawful. Ask VFA to state that an unqualified *chay* or *thuần chay* (vegan) claim means the vegan criteria. Egg or milk would then be named in a qualifier, and allium-free would stay a voluntary claim. The window is the new Food Safety Law and its decree. The National Assembly takes first comments on the law in October 2026 and considers it for passage in May 2027 [@APR-07; @LBL-15] {VN-direct|Medium}. Time safety inspections (borax, microbes, labels) before the 1st and 7th lunar months.
- **International organisations and funders:** fund the cheapest chay evidence first: a tabulation of the 2021 Diet Quality Questionnaire by lunar date, then a chay-day question in a survey. Fund a chay safety and composition survey too. Count effects only for added chay occasions, not upgraded ones, and count them in protein, not kilograms. Match the route to the funder's rules: several diet-change funders exclude Vietnam or this kind of work ([[ch18-demand-sizing]], [[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).
