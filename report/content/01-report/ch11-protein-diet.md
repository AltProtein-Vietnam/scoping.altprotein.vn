# 11. What Vietnam eats and pays for protein

**In one paragraph.** Any new protein in Vietnam enters a diet that is already rich in meat and still moving towards more of it. The national nutrition survey measured meat intake of 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, against a recommendation of 50 to 80 g, and animal foods now supply 42% of protein, twice the 1990 share. Health guidance asks people to eat legumes daily and red meat in moderation, while the livestock strategy's 2030 meat target has already been reached. Adults eat enough protein on average, so "more protein" is not the pitch. Children are not a protein market either: stunting (18% in 2023, 32% among ethnic minority children) is set before age two, and school lunches, even in upland boarding schools, already carry meat, fish or egg with enough protein and zinc; the documented shortfalls are breakfast, calcium and vitamin A. Soy foods are already everyday food: 40% of adults ate tofu, soy milk or beans the day before in a 2021 national survey. Protein is cheap: eggs and chicken cost VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein, tofu and dry legumes less, and lean pork about VND 71,000. Imported plant-based analogues cost VND 542,000 to 622,000. Protein sits in Vietnamese meals as noodle toppings, fillings, rolls and braised side dishes. When pork prices jumped 70% after African swine fever, households switched to chicken and imports, not to tofu. Together these facts give the targets a new protein must meet on price, protein content and format, and they show where demand is growing: poultry and eating out.

## 11.1 A high-meat diet that is still rising

**Intake.** The General Nutrition Survey 2019 to 2020 (22,400 households in 25 provinces) measured meat intake of 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, up from 84 g in 2010; urban residents ate 155.3 g. The national recommendation is 50 to 80 g [@DIE-01; @DIE-02; @DIE-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. We found no newer national intake survey: a September 2026 article on the National Institute of Nutrition (NIN) still used the 2020 figures [@APR-42] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-meat-intake-2020}}

**Supply.** FAO food balance sheets put meat supply at 60.5 kg per person in 2023 (carcass weight), up from 41.0 kg in 2010, and total protein supply at 102.2 g per person per day, of which 43.2 g (42%) came from animal foods. The animal share was 21% in 1990 and 28% in 2000 [@DIE-07; @DIE-08] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Rice still supplies 27.0 g of protein a day and soy foods only 4.9 g [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-animal-protein-share}}

**Daily reach.** The grams understate how widely plant protein is eaten. The one national probability survey of what people ate, the Diet Quality Questionnaire (a Gallup telephone sample of 1,007 adults, 13 November to 12 December 2021), found that 40.0% of adults had eaten dried beans, soybeans, tofu, soy milk or bean flour on the previous day, with no urban and rural gap (40.5% and 39.2%). In the same survey 92.1% had eaten meat, poultry or fish and 76.1% unprocessed red meat [@APR-36; @BRD-01; @BRD-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Soy foods are an everyday side for two adults in five, eaten beside meat rather than instead of it (our inference) {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. Full indicators: `diet_quality_vietnam.csv` and [[app-d1-diet-chay-prices]].

**Direction.** The OECD-FAO outlook projects meat food use per person rising from 49.8 kg in 2025 to 62.2 kg in 2035 (retail weight), a rise of 25%. Poultry leads, from 17.0 to 25.8 kg; pork rises only from 28.6 to 31.5 kg [@DIE-11] {VN-direct|Medium} {fx:projection}. With population growth this adds about 1.57 Mt of meat a year by 2035 (our calculation) [@DIE-11; @DIE-36] {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. Population rises only about 5% over the same decade, so almost all of the growth is more meat per person [@DIE-36] {VN-direct|Medium} {fx:projection}. Official targets point the same way. The livestock strategy (Decision 1520/QĐ-TTg) aims for 58 to 62 kg of carcass meat per person by 2030, a level FAO supply had already reached in 2023, while the health ministry's guidance asks for red meat in moderation (section 11.6) [@APR-10; @DIE-07] {VN-direct|High} {fx:projection}. The balance model of Part IV uses the same outlook ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]). {{kn:kn-meat-growth-2035}}

**Who eats more.** At home, urban and rural households now use the same amount of meat (2.60 kg per person a month in 2024); urban meat growth has moved to eating out. The richest fifth uses 3.17 kg and the poorest 1.97 kg, but the poorest fifth's meat use nearly doubled from 2010 to 2024, so growth now comes from lower-income households catching up [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Home meat use has been flat since 2022, the first pause since 2010. With eating out recovering and FAO supply still rising in 2023, this is not yet evidence of a plateau in total meat eaten [@DIE-05; @DIE-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

## 11.2 Enough protein for adults, and for school-age children

Average protein intake is about 73 to 80 g per person per day: 72.8 g measured in 929 adults in Hanoi and Hung Yen (former Thai Binh) in 2022 [@DIE-32] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}, and about 80 g derived from the national survey's energy and protein shares [@DIE-01] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. The 2026 recommendation is about 0.93 g per kg of body weight, with a 50 g reference for labels, and it asks for "high-quality protein from diverse animal and plant sources" rather than a set animal share [@DIE-04] {VN-direct|High}. So for the average adult, protein is sufficient and meat is in excess of guidance.

Children's nutrition problems are real, but for school-age children they are not a protein gap (our inference from the school evidence below; an earlier draft described "too little high-quality protein at very low cost in poor upland districts"). Stunting among under-fives fell from 29.3% (2010) to 19.6% (2020) and 18% (2023), and it is 32% among ethnic minority children [@APR-27; @DIE-01; @DIE-03] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Low serum zinc affected 53.3% of children aged 6 to 59 months in 2020 [@APR-42] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Stunting is set largely before age two, the first 1,000 days that UNICEF and the World Bank target [@APR-44] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}, so it runs through complementary feeding and home diets, not school meals (our inference) [@NQR-28; @NQR-29] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}, and UNICEF's 2027 to 2031 programme for Viet Nam accordingly prioritises preventive nutrition through "school food and complementary food standards" and marketing restrictions [@APR-27] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}. What school plates carry:

- **Urban lunches.** One Hanoi primary school's published lunch sheets (25 days) carry about 31.8 g of protein (21.2 to 38.6 g), against a 2026 recommendation of 32 to 49 g for the whole day for boys aged 6 to 11 [@PMR-26; @NQR-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Upland boarding schools.** In 13 ethnic-minority boarding schools in five upland provinces that publish their menus (22 school-weeks, 353 meals, 2026), 240 of 242 lunches and dinners carried a meat, fish or egg dish, and pork appeared in 102 of 124 lunches, 82% (our coding, `upland_school_weeks.csv`) [@UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-04; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16; @UPL-17] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. The four kitchens that publish cost sheets bought about 110 to 270 g of raw pork per pupil a day, including a lunch paid for under the border-commune decree (Decree 339/2025) [@UPL-01; @UPL-04; @UPL-07; @UPL-09; @UPL-10] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Tofu appears beside or inside pork dishes, except in one egg-and-tofu lunch; no textured soy or mock meat appears in any of the 353 meals [@UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-04; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16; @UPL-17] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Weighed diets.** Kitchen-weighed boarding diets at two schools in Yen Bai (now Lao Cai) in 2019 met protein and zinc needs (zinc at 131 to 157%) but supplied only 36.5% of calcium and 41 to 45% of vitamin A [@UPL-18] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The thin meal is breakfast: 86% of boarding breakfasts carry little or no animal food [@UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-04; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Full data: `upland_school_weeks.csv`.

Two consequences follow for new protein. First, a plant dish that removes all the meat or fish from a lunch would need added zinc, because national school guidance doubles a child's zinc requirement (5.6 to 11.2 mg a day at age 6 to 7) when a diet has little animal protein [@APR-04] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}; every one of the 124 upland lunches coded carried meat, fish or egg [@UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-04; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16; @UPL-17] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. A blend keeps protein quality: a minced dish with 20 to 30% of its protein from textured soy scores a DIAAS (a protein-quality score) of about 97 or more on the older-child pattern (our calculation, using pork at 113 to 137 and, as a proxy for textured soy, extruded soybean at 65 to 97 scored on the young-child pattern) [@NQR-05; @NQR-07] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}. Second, the stunting agenda sits before school age. There a plant product must register as a food for children under 36 months [@NQR-04] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}, be scored against the stricter young-child protein-quality pattern [@PRA-02; @NQR-31] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}, and compete with milk, meat and fortified cereal blends, which is where the trial evidence sits [@NQR-28; @NQR-29; @NQR-30; @NQR-31] {general|Medium} {dx:tested}. School routes are assessed in [[ch15-channels]].

Overweight is rising at the same time: among 5 to 19 year olds it rose from 8.5% to 19.0% between 2010 and 2020 (26.8% in cities), and adult diabetes from 5.3% to 10.9% (2010 to 2022, modelled) [@DIE-01; @DIE-28] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Vietnam's protein-related problems are too much meat in cities and poor feeding before age two in upland and ethnic minority communities; neither is a gap that a cheap school protein dish would fill (our inference) {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. {{kn:kn-stunting}}

Three benchmarks follow {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}:

- **The plant floor** is tofu and dry legumes at about VND 15,000 to 40,000 per 100 g of protein.
- **The mass animal floor** is eggs and chicken at VND 34,000 to 45,000. This is the price a mass-market protein food competes with. {{kn:kn-protein-price-floor}}
- **The mainstream meat reference** is lean pork at about VND 71,000. Beef (about VND 119,000 to 143,000) and shrimp (about VND 200,000) are premium, and shrimp is the one mass protein where a seafood analogue has wide price headroom.

Imported analogues sit at 12 to 18 times the price of eggs and chicken breast per gram of protein, and about 8 to 9 times lean pork [@FORM-01; @COST-39; @COST-40; @COST-41; @DIE-12] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The premiums that coincided with stalling demand in the United States and Europe were 35 to 77% per kilogram ([[ch13-consumers]]; [@GLB-03; @GLB-09; @GLB-10]) {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Vietnam's gap is an order of magnitude larger. {{kn:kn-analogue-price-gap}}

## 11.4 Where protein sits in the dish

Meat took 26.7% of household food spending in 2024 (VND 349,800 per person a month), fish 8.4%, eggs 1.3% and tofu 0.7%. Meals eaten away from home took 22.5% of food spending: 29.6% in cities and 16.1% in rural areas, with the highest spending in the South East [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Eating out has not recovered its 2018 peak share of 26.7% [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

We found no measured portion weights for the protein part of *phở* (rice noodle soup), *bún* (rice vermicelli dishes), *cơm tấm* (broken rice with grilled pork) or *bánh mì* (filled baguette), and log this as a high-priority open question [[app-r1-open-questions]]. What the evidence does show:

- The carbohydrate bases carry little protein: flat rice noodles 3.2 g, rice vermicelli 1.7 g and bread 7.9 g per 100 g [@DIE-12] {VN-direct|High}. In noodle dishes the protein is almost all topping.
- In noodle dishes the protein is sliced or minced meat, meatballs or fish cake on top; in rice meals it is a side dish such as braised pork or fish, egg or tofu in tomato sauce; in *bánh mì* it is a filling of pork roll (*chả lụa*), pâté, grilled meat or egg (our description, not measured) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.
- The traditional *đồ chay* (traditional vegetarian food) industry already makes analogues of exactly these components: vegetarian pork roll, meatballs, fish cake and beef slices [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

The formats that fit Vietnamese dishes are toppings, fillings, rolls, balls and mince, not whole-muscle steaks or burgers.

## 11.5 Price shocks and what people switch to

Pork prices swing hard; chicken prices barely move. After African swine fever reached Vietnam in 2019, the pig producer price rose 70% from 2019 to 2020 (VND 46,076 to 78,138 per kg) while the chicken price stayed flat [@DIE-25] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. USDA records pork consumption down 12.6% in 2019, chicken up 26.2% and chicken imports up 107% [@DIE-10] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Home tofu use did not move (0.44 kg per person a month in 2018 and in 2020), while home egg and fish use rose [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

This is the clearest revealed evidence of cross-protein substitution in Vietnam, and it ran to other animal proteins. It has two uses for frontier actors. First, it warns that a pork shock does not by itself send buyers to plant protein. Second, it sizes the cost motive processed-meat makers already have, which is steady rather than a hedge. Soy extension saves money at every hog price since 2019: replacing 20% of the lean in *chả lụa* saves about VND 9,100 to 16,600 per kg at 2019 and 2020 prices, so a pork shock changes the size of the saving, not its sign, and 20% extension trims only about a sixth of a recipe's exposure to the shock (our calculation) [@DIE-25; @HXE-01] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. The extender a new protein must beat is therefore hydrated soy, not pork ([[ch14-frontier-demand]], [[ch16-business-buyers]]). COVID-19 in 2020 confounds the home-use figures, and we found no published cross-price elasticities for Vietnam that we could read.

## 11.6 Health and food safety as demand drivers

Food-safety worry is near universal. More than 90% of respondents in a pork study in two provinces worried about contaminated pork [@DIE-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}, and pork shoppers in northern Vietnam feared chemical hazards more than microbes [@DIE-31] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. What people pay for safety is more modest than what they say. A hypothetical choice experiment in Hanoi and Hai Phong found premiums of 70% for pork from certified abattoirs and up to 102% for hormone- and drug-free pork [@DIE-29] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested}; an auction with real money found 20% [@DIE-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested}. Plan on the lower figure.

Rising overweight and diabetes [@DIE-01; @DIE-28] {VN-direct|Medium}, and official messaging that Vietnamese are "cutting rice and eating more meat" [@DIE-38] {VN-direct|Medium}, give a health reason to eat less meat, and current guidance says so. The Ministry of Health's Ten tips to 2030 (Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT, November 2024) ask people to combine animal and plant foods, to eat fish, poultry, and nuts and seeds daily and to eat red meat in moderation [@APR-17; @APR-18] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}. Its guidance under the new Law on Disease Prevention (Decision 1982/QĐ-BYT, 1 July 2026) tells people with diet-related chronic disease to use diverse animal and plant protein, prioritising fish, lean meat, eggs, milk and legumes [@APR-16; @APR-15] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}. Neither names an alternative or plant-based product; Decision 1982 names legumes and the Ten tips name nuts and seeds [@APR-16; @APR-17] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}. A sponsored article tied to a soy-products company already uses this health-policy hook [@APR-43] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Revealed behaviour does not yet follow the guidance: home meat use rose in every income group from 2010 to 2024 [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. We found no nationally representative survey of why Vietnamese eat less meat, when they do.

## 11.7 Vietnam against its neighbours

Vietnam eats far more meat than its income predicts. At about USD 13,500 per person (PPP, 2023) its meat supply of 60.5 kg already exceeds Japan's long-run plateau of 45 to 50 kg (from FAO's historic series, not like-for-like with the new series) and is more than twice Thailand's 24.5 kg, and it is close to China's at the same income in 2013 (59 kg) [@DIE-07; @DIE-08; @DIE-09] {VN-adjacent|High} {dx:revealed}.

{{chart:chart-animal-protein-ladder}}

Three readings follow {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:inferred}:

1. **No single income level marks a meat plateau.** Plateaus run from about 25 kg (Thailand) to about 80 kg (Taiwan) and are set by food culture and fish supply more than by income. No neighbour has shown a sustained fall in meat per person as income grew.
2. **The animal share of protein levels off at 55 to 60%** in Japan, Korea, Taiwan and Malaysia. At 42%, Vietnam has room to rise; Thailand shows that a fish-rich diet can hold near 40%.
3. **The demand that novel protein could take a share of is growing in tonnes.** Each 1% of meat protein is about 9,200 t of protein a year on 2023 supply data, worth roughly VND 3,600 to 6,500 billion (USD 140 to 250 million) at today's chicken-to-pork prices per gram of protein (our calculation; supply overstates intake) [@DIE-07; @COST-39; @COST-40; @DIE-12] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 11.8 What this means for the frontier

- **Price.** Mass-market protein foods compete with eggs and chicken at VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein; a meat analogue in mainstream retail must aim at lean pork (about VND 71,000) or below. Above beef it is a premium or novelty item. A mass product priced above pork is not supported by any revealed evidence we found.
- **Protein.** Do not sell "more protein" to adults. Do sell meat-like protein density inside meat-replacing formats: 12 to 20 g per 100 g, against 19 g for lean pork and a 4.7 g median for today's *chay* foods ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]). At 10 g per 100 g a food qualifies for a "high protein" claim ([[ch13-consumers]]).
- **Format.** Build for the dish: toppings, fillings, rolls, balls and mince for home cooking and street food.
- **Growth.** Poultry, not pork, carries most projected meat growth to 2035; poultry-style formats deserve as much attention as pork analogues. Urban growth is in eating out, which points to foodservice and B2B routes ([[ch15-channels]]).
- **Nutrition.** Do not pitch a new protein as the answer to child undernutrition. For school-age children, including upland boarders, published menus and weighed diets show protein and zinc met at lunch and dinner; the documented shortfalls are breakfast, calcium and vitamin A. A plant dish that removes all the meat needs added zinc and little sodium; a blend in minced dishes needs nothing extra. Stunting is set before age two, where a plant product must meet the young-child protein-quality pattern, register as a food for children under 36 months and beat fortified cereal and milk products {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

**For each reader.**
- **Startups:** set price targets per 100 g of protein against eggs, chicken and lean pork, and design formats for named dishes.
- **Investors:** demand is growing in tonnes, but the money is in low-price everyday protein; models that need consumer premiums above about 20% are not supported by revealed evidence.
- **Manufacturers:** a pork extender saves money at every pork price; as a hedge it removes only about a sixth of a recipe's exposure to a pork shock, so value it on the saving and test a new extender against your own soy recipe.
- **Research bodies:** measure protein per serving in common dishes and publish a current food composition table that includes dishes and *chay* foods; measure phytate, zinc and DIAAS (on both the young-child and older-child patterns) of tofu, textured soy and blended minced dishes, and add textured soy to the composition table that school menu software uses.
- **Policy makers:** the 2026 recommendations, the Ten tips and Decision 1982 already favour diverse protein including legumes, and school guidance (Decision 3958/QĐ-BYT) sets no animal share, although the menu software still prints an animal-protein standard of at least 48% ([[ch15-channels]]). State zinc and iron per lunch by absorption band and sodium per lunch in the menu software, and note that mandatory fortification (Decree 09/2016) reaches no soy protein food [@APR-04; @NQR-02; @APR-16; @APR-17] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **International organisations:** separate the urban "too much meat" problem from child undernutrition, and within child undernutrition separate school-age children (plates already carry protein and zinc; breakfast, calcium and vitamin A are the gaps) from children under two (complementary-food standards, where plant products face the young-child protein-quality pattern, registration and trial evidence favouring milk or fortified blends). An India roundtable is examining adding plant and fermented proteins to feeding schemes on cost and protein-quality grounds [@AFN-14] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:stated}; Vietnam's school plates point to micronutrients instead. A fortified plant item for boarding-school breakfasts or charity-funded lunches is a research option to test on calcium and vitamin A as well as zinc, not a product brief.
