# 11. What Vietnam eats and pays for protein

**In brief.**

- **Meat intake is high and still rising.** People ate 136.4 g of meat per person per day in 2020, against a national recommendation of 50 to 80 g. Animal foods now supply 42% of protein, twice the 1990 share. Health guidance asks for red meat in moderation, but the livestock strategy's 2030 meat target has already been reached.
- **There is no protein gap to fill.** Adults eat enough protein on average, so "more protein" is not the selling point. Stunting (18% in 2023, 32% among ethnic minority children) is set before age two, and only about half of children aged 6 to 23 months eat a diverse enough diet. School lunches, even in upland boarding schools, already carry meat, fish or egg with enough protein and zinc; the gaps are breakfast, calcium and vitamin A.
- **Soy foods are everyday food, made from imported beans.** In a 2021 national survey, 40% of adults had eaten tofu, soy milk or beans the day before. About 97.5% of Vietnam's soybean supply was imported in 2023.
- **Protein is cheap.** Eggs and chicken cost VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein, tofu and dry legumes less, and lean pork about VND 71,000. Imported plant-based analogues cost VND 542,000 to 622,000.
- **Protein is a topping, filling or side dish.** It sits in meals as noodle toppings, fillings, rolls and braised side dishes. When the pig producer price rose 70% after African swine fever in 2019, households switched to chicken and imports, not to tofu, and in 2025 they switched to chicken again.
- **These facts set the targets.** A new protein has to meet them on price, protein content and format. Demand is growing in poultry and in eating out.

> **How to read Part III.** Part III treats demand as an input to the supply choices in Parts II and IV. It does not plan consumer marketing. Every demand claim carries two tags. The evidence tag gives the type of evidence and our confidence in it. The demand evidence tag says how the evidence was gathered. {dx:stated} marks what people say in surveys. {dx:revealed} marks what people and firms actually do (sales, prices, trade, menus, consumption statistics). {dx:tested} marks tastings, auctions, choice experiments and trials. {dx:inferred} marks our inference from indirect evidence. Stated evidence usually overstates behaviour. Method and limits: [[app-m3-demand-method]].

## 11.1 A high-meat diet that is still rising

**Intake.** Vietnamese people ate 136.4 g of meat per person per day in 2020, up from 84 g in 2010, and urban residents ate 155.3 g. The General Nutrition Survey 2019 to 2020 measured this in 22,400 households in 25 provinces. The national recommendation is 50 to 80 g [@DIE-01; @DIE-02; @DIE-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. We found no newer national intake survey. A September 2026 article on the National Institute of Nutrition still used the 2020 figures [@APR-42] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-meat-intake-2020}}

**Supply.** The food balance sheets of the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) estimate the food available to eat in each country. They put meat supply at 60.5 kg per person in 2023 (carcass weight, which includes bone), up from 41.0 kg in 2010. Total protein supply was 102.2 g per person per day, of which 43.2 g (42%) came from animal foods. The animal share was 21% in 1990 and 28% in 2000 [@DIE-07; @DIE-08] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Rice still supplies 27.0 g of protein a day and soy foods only 4.9 g [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-animal-protein-share}}

**Origin.** The soy foods people eat rest almost wholly on imported beans, and nearly all wheat is imported. Vietnam's import dependency for soybeans was 97.5% in 2023 (imports over production plus imports minus exports), and its wheat self-sufficiency was 1.6% [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Soy foods carried about 181,000 t of protein in 2023. Vietnam's own soybean crop (48,000 t) could cover at most 9% of the 533,000 t of beans used as food (our calculation) [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

A second source, the OECD-FAO Agricultural Outlook described below, records far less soybean food use, and none from 2024. We use FAO ([[app-r2-disagreements]], DG-362). We found no figure for the share of Vietnamese beans in tofu and soy milk (open question OQ-463). Chapter 1 sets out where the rest of the protein supply comes from ([[ch01-why-vietnam]]). {{kn:kn-soybean-import-dependence}}

**Daily reach.** Plant protein is eaten more widely than the grams suggest. In 2021, 40.0% of adults had eaten dried beans, soybeans, tofu, soy milk or bean flour on the previous day, with no urban and rural gap (40.5% and 39.2%). In the same survey 92.1% had eaten meat, poultry or fish and 76.1% unprocessed red meat. The survey is the Diet Quality Questionnaire, the only national random-sample survey of what people ate (a Gallup telephone sample of 1,007 adults, 13 November to 12 December 2021) [@APR-36; @BRD-01; @BRD-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Soy foods are an everyday side for two adults in five, eaten beside meat rather than instead of it (our inference) {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. Full indicators: `diet_quality_vietnam.csv` and [[app-d1-diet-chay-prices]].

**Direction.** Meat eating is projected to keep rising. The OECD-FAO Agricultural Outlook is a joint projection by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and FAO. It projects meat food use per person rising from 49.8 kg in 2025 to 62.2 kg in 2035 (retail weight), a rise of 25%. Poultry leads, from 17.0 to 25.8 kg; pork rises only from 28.6 to 31.5 kg [@DIE-11] {VN-direct|Medium} {fx:projection}.

With population growth, this adds about 1.57 Mt (million tonnes) of meat a year by 2035 (our calculation) [@DIE-11; @DIE-36] {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. Population rises only about 5% over the same decade, so almost all of the growth is more meat per person [@DIE-36] {VN-direct|Medium} {fx:projection}.

Official targets point the same way. The livestock strategy (Decision 1520/QĐ-TTg) aims for 58 to 62 kg of carcass meat per person by 2030. FAO supply had already reached that level in 2023. The Ministry of Health's guidance, by contrast, asks for red meat in moderation (section 11.6) [@APR-10; @DIE-07] {VN-direct|High} {fx:projection}. The balance model of Part IV uses the same outlook ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]). {{kn:kn-meat-growth-2035}}

**Who eats more.** Meat growth now comes from lower-income households and from eating out. At home, urban and rural households now use the same amount of meat: 2.60 kg per person a month in 2024. Urban meat growth has moved to eating out. The richest fifth of households uses 3.17 kg and the poorest fifth 1.97 kg. But the poorest fifth's meat use nearly doubled from 2010 to 2024: lower-income households are catching up [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

Home meat use has been flat since 2022, the first pause since 2010. This is not yet evidence that total meat eating has levelled off, because eating out is recovering and FAO supply was still rising in 2023 [@DIE-05; @DIE-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

## 11.2 Enough protein for adults, and for school-age children

Adults eat enough protein on average: about 73 to 80 g per person per day. A 2022 study measured 72.8 g in 929 adults in Hanoi and Hung Yen (former Thai Binh) [@DIE-32] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. About 80 g is derived from the national survey's energy and protein shares [@DIE-01] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

The 2026 recommendation is about 0.93 g per kg of body weight, with a 50 g reference for labels. It asks for "high-quality protein from diverse animal and plant sources" rather than a set animal share [@DIE-04] {VN-direct|High}. So the average adult eats enough protein, and more meat than guidance advises.

Children's nutrition problems are real, but for school-age children they are not a protein gap. This is our inference from the school evidence below; an earlier draft described "too little high-quality protein at very low cost in poor upland districts".

Stunting means being too short for one's age, a sign of long-term undernutrition. Stunting among children under five fell from 29.3% (2010) to 19.6% (2020) and 18% (2023). It is 32% among ethnic minority children [@APR-27; @DIE-01; @DIE-03] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Low serum zinc (zinc in the blood) affected 53.3% of children aged 6 to 59 months in 2020 [@APR-42] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.

Stunting is set largely before age two, in the first 1,000 days that UNICEF and the World Bank target [@APR-44] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. So it runs through complementary feeding (the foods a baby eats alongside breast milk) and home diets, not school meals (our inference) [@NQR-28; @NQR-29] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}. In line with this, UNICEF's 2027 to 2031 programme for Viet Nam puts preventive nutrition first, through "school food and complementary food standards" and marketing restrictions [@APR-27] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}.

**The gap before age two is diet diversity, not protein supply.** In 2020 to 2021, only 55.2% of children aged 6 to 23 months ate from at least five of eight food groups, the minimum dietary diversity measure. The groups include flesh foods, eggs, dairy and legumes. The share was 37.3% in the poorest fifth of households and 67.9% in the richest. It was 23.0% in Khmer households and 8.7% in Mong households, a small sample of 34 children [@FBS-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. National protein supply (about 102 g per person per day in 2023) is not the constraint; access to diverse, animal-source and fortified foods for poor and ethnic minority infants is (our inference) [@FBS-01; @DIE-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

> **Open question.** A press report of August 2026 quotes the director of the National Institute of Nutrition as giving 14.8% stunting among children under five in 2025 [@FBS-02] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. It does not name the survey, and 14.8% is also the 2019 to 2020 survey's stunting rate for ages 5 to 19. We keep 18% (2023) until the institute publishes its 2025 figure (OQ-464 in [[app-r1-open-questions]]; DG-365 in [[app-r2-disagreements]]).

What school meals contain:

- **Urban lunches.** One Hanoi primary school's published lunch sheets (25 days) carry about 31.8 g of protein (21.2 to 38.6 g). The 2026 recommendation for the whole day is 32 to 49 g for boys aged 6 to 11 [@PMR-26; @NQR-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Upland boarding schools.** We coded the published menus of 13 ethnic-minority boarding schools in five upland provinces (22 school-weeks, 353 meals, 2026). In 240 of 242 lunches and dinners there was a meat, fish or egg dish, and pork appeared in 102 of 124 lunches, 82% (our coding, `upland_school_weeks.csv`) [@UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-04; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16; @UPL-17] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. The four kitchens that publish cost sheets bought about 110 to 270 g of raw pork per pupil a day. This includes a lunch paid for under the border-commune decree (Decree 339/2025) [@UPL-01; @UPL-04; @UPL-07; @UPL-09; @UPL-10] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Tofu appears beside or inside pork dishes, except in one egg-and-tofu lunch. No textured soy or mock meat appears in any of the 353 meals [@UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-04; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16; @UPL-17] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Weighed diets.** Kitchen-weighed boarding diets at two schools in Lao Cai (former Yen Bai) in 2019 met protein and zinc needs (zinc at 131 to 157%). But they supplied only 36.5% of calcium and 41 to 45% of vitamin A [@UPL-18] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The thin meal is breakfast: 86% of boarding breakfasts carry little or no animal food [@UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-04; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Full data: `upland_school_weeks.csv`.

Two consequences follow for new protein:

- **Replacing all the meat needs added zinc; a blend keeps protein quality.** National school guidance doubles a child's zinc need (5.6 to 11.2 mg a day at age 6 to 7) when a diet has little animal protein [@APR-04] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Every one of the 124 upland lunches we coded carried meat, fish or egg [@UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-04; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16; @UPL-17] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. DIAAS (digestible indispensable amino acid score) measures protein quality against a reference pattern of amino-acid needs, which is stricter for young children than for older ones. A minced dish with 20 to 30% of its protein from textured soy scores a DIAAS of about 97 or more on the older-child pattern. This is our calculation, using pork at 113 to 137 and, as a proxy for textured soy, extruded soybean at 65 to 97 scored on the young-child pattern [@NQR-05; @NQR-07] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
- **Stunting is set before school age.** There a plant product must register as a food for children under 36 months [@NQR-04] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. It would be scored against the stricter young-child protein-quality pattern [@PRA-02; @NQR-31] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}. And it would compete with milk, meat and fortified cereal blends, which is where the trial evidence sits [@NQR-28; @NQR-29; @NQR-30; @NQR-31] {general|Medium} {dx:tested}. School routes are assessed in [[ch15-channels]].

Overweight is rising at the same time. Among 5 to 19 year olds it rose from 8.5% to 19.0% between 2010 and 2020 (26.8% in cities). Adult diabetes rose from 5.3% to 10.9% (2010 to 2022, modelled) [@DIE-01; @DIE-28] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Vietnam's protein-related problems are too much meat in cities and poor feeding before age two in upland and ethnic minority communities. Neither is a gap that a cheap school protein dish would fill (our inference) {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. {{kn:kn-stunting}}

Three benchmarks follow {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}:

- **The plant floor** is tofu and dry legumes at about VND 15,000 to 40,000 per 100 g of protein.
- **The mass animal floor** is eggs and chicken at VND 34,000 to 45,000. This is the price a mass-market protein food competes with. {{kn:kn-protein-price-floor}}
- **The mainstream meat reference** is lean pork at about VND 71,000. Beef (about VND 119,000 to 143,000) and shrimp (about VND 200,000) are premium. Shrimp is the one mass protein where a seafood analogue has wide price headroom.

Imported analogues cost 12 to 18 times as much as eggs and chicken breast per gram of protein, and about 8 to 9 times as much as lean pork [@FORM-01; @COST-39; @COST-40; @COST-41; @DIE-12] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. In the United States and Europe, demand stalled at premiums of 35 to 77% per kilogram ([[ch13-consumers]]; [@GLB-03; @GLB-09; @GLB-10]) {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Vietnam's gap is an order of magnitude larger. {{kn:kn-analogue-price-gap}}

## 11.4 Protein is a topping, filling or side dish

The formats that fit Vietnamese dishes are toppings, fillings, rolls, balls and mince, not whole-muscle steaks or burgers. The evidence follows.

Meat took 26.7% of household food spending in 2024 (VND 349,800 per person a month), fish 8.4%, eggs 1.3% and tofu 0.7%. Meals eaten away from home took 22.5% of food spending: 29.6% in cities and 16.1% in rural areas. Spending was highest in the South East [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Eating out has not recovered its 2018 peak share of 26.7% [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

We found no measured portion weights for the protein part of *phở* (rice noodle soup), *bún* (rice vermicelli dishes), *cơm tấm* (broken rice with grilled pork) or *bánh mì* (filled baguette). We log this as a high-priority open question ([[app-r1-open-questions]]). What the evidence does show:

- **The carbohydrate bases carry little protein:** flat rice noodles 3.2 g, rice vermicelli 1.7 g and bread 7.9 g per 100 g [@DIE-12] {VN-direct|High}. In noodle dishes the protein is almost all topping.
- **Protein comes as a topping, side dish or filling.** In noodle dishes it is sliced or minced meat, meatballs or fish cake on top. In rice meals it is a side dish such as braised pork or fish, egg or tofu in tomato sauce. In *bánh mì* it is a filling of pork roll (*chả lụa*), pâté, grilled meat or egg (our description, not measured) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.
- **Chay makers already copy these parts.** The *đồ chay* (traditional vegetarian food) industry makes analogues of exactly these components: vegetarian pork roll, meatballs, fish cake and beef slices [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

## 11.5 When pork prices jump, people switch to other animal protein, not tofu

Pork prices swing hard; chicken prices barely move. After African swine fever reached Vietnam in 2019, the pig producer price rose 70% from 2019 to 2020 (VND 46,076 to 78,138 per kg). The chicken price stayed flat [@DIE-25] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Consumer pork prices, measured by the price-index item, rose 57.23% in 2020 [@SEC-08] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

The US Department of Agriculture records pork consumption down 12.6% in 2019, chicken up 26.2% and chicken imports up 107% [@DIE-10] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Home tofu use did not move (0.44 kg per person a month in 2018 and in 2020), while home egg and fish use rose [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

**Supply and policy moved to other animal protein too.** In 2019 pig output fell 13.8% and poultry output rose 16.5% [@PAN-03] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. In 2020 the government ordered about 100,000 t of pork imports for the first quarter. It also asked authorities to encourage "pork substitutes and frozen pork" (Notice 132/TB-VPCP) [@PAN-03] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

**A short panic, not a shift to plant protein.** In March 2019 Hanoi traders reported about a month of pork avoidance, with shoppers moving to vegetables and tofu [@PAN-34] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. That was a short food-safety panic: annual home tofu use did not change. We follow the revealed figure ([[app-r2-disagreements]], DG-355).

**The 2025 wave repeated the pattern.** Culling sales briefly pushed live pig prices down to about VND 50,000 per kg in November 2025. Consumers "increasingly substituted chicken for pork" [@MAC-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. We found no retail data showing that tofu, soy milk or *chay* products sold more during either wave (open question OQ-450).

This is the clearest revealed evidence of people switching between proteins in Vietnam, and in both waves they switched to other animal proteins, helped in 2020 by imports. It has two lessons for makers of new protein. First, a pork shock does not by itself send buyers to plant protein. Second, it sizes the cost reason that processed-meat makers already have to use soy, and that reason is steady rather than a hedge against shocks.

An extender is a plant or fungal protein that replaces part of the lean meat in a processed product. Extending meat with soy saves money at every hog price since 2019. Replacing 20% of the lean in *chả lụa* saves about VND 9,100 to 16,600 per kg at 2019 and 2020 prices. So a pork shock changes the size of the saving, not its sign. And 20% extension trims only about a sixth of a recipe's exposure to the shock (our calculation) [@DIE-25; @HXE-01] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

The extender a new protein has to beat is hydrated soy, not pork ([[ch14-frontier-demand]], [[ch16-business-buyers]]). COVID-19 in 2020 distorts the home-use figures. We found no published cross-price elasticities for Vietnam that we could read (measures of how demand for one food responds to the price of another).

## 11.6 Health and food safety: strong worries, smaller changes in behaviour

Food-safety worry is near universal. More than 90% of respondents in a pork study in two provinces worried about contaminated pork [@DIE-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Pork shoppers in northern Vietnam feared chemical hazards more than microbes [@DIE-31] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.

People pay less for safety than they say they would. A hypothetical choice experiment in Hanoi and Hai Phong found premiums of 70% for pork from certified abattoirs and up to 102% for hormone- and drug-free pork [@DIE-29] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested}. An auction with real money found 20% [@DIE-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested}. Plan on the lower figure.

There is a health reason to eat less meat, and current guidance says so. Overweight and diabetes are rising [@DIE-01; @DIE-28] {VN-direct|Medium}, and official messages say Vietnamese are "cutting rice and eating more meat" [@DIE-38] {VN-direct|Medium}. Two Ministry of Health documents set the guidance:

- **The Ten tips to 2030** (Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT, November 2024) ask people to combine animal and plant foods, to eat fish, poultry, and nuts and seeds daily and to eat red meat in moderation [@APR-17; @APR-18] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}.
- **Guidance under the new Law on Disease Prevention** (Decision 1982/QĐ-BYT, 1 July 2026) tells people with diet-related chronic disease to use diverse animal and plant protein, favouring fish, lean meat, eggs, milk and legumes [@APR-16; @APR-15] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}.

Neither names an alternative or plant-based product. Decision 1982 names legumes and the Ten tips name nuts and seeds [@APR-16; @APR-17] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}. A sponsored article tied to a soy-products company already uses this health guidance as a selling point [@APR-43] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.

Behaviour does not yet follow the guidance: home meat use rose in every income group from 2010 to 2024 [@DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. We found no nationally representative survey of why Vietnamese eat less meat, when they do.

## 11.7 Vietnam eats more meat than its income predicts

Vietnam eats far more meat than its income predicts. Its income was about USD 13,500 per person in 2023, measured at purchasing power parity (which adjusts for local prices). At that income its meat supply of 60.5 kg already exceeds Japan's long-run plateau of 45 to 50 kg. Japan's figure comes from FAO's historic data series, which is not like-for-like with the new series. Vietnam's supply is also more than twice Thailand's 24.5 kg, and close to China's at the same income in 2013 (59 kg) [@DIE-07; @DIE-08; @DIE-09] {VN-adjacent|High} {dx:revealed}.

Three readings follow {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:inferred}:

1. **No single income level marks a meat plateau.** Plateaus run from about 25 kg (Thailand) to about 80 kg (Taiwan). Food culture and fish supply set them more than income does. No neighbour has shown a sustained fall in meat per person as income grew.
2. **The animal share of protein levels off at 55 to 60%** in Japan, Korea, Taiwan and Malaysia. At 42%, Vietnam has room to rise. Thailand shows that a fish-rich diet can hold near 40%.
3. **The demand that a new protein could take a share of is growing in tonnes.** Each 1% of meat protein is about 9,200 t of protein a year on 2023 supply data. That is worth roughly VND 3,600 to 6,500 billion (USD 140 to 250 million) at today's chicken-to-pork prices per gram of protein (our calculation; supply overstates intake) [@DIE-07; @COST-39; @COST-40; @DIE-12] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 11.8 What this means for new protein

- **Price.** Mass-market protein foods compete with eggs and chicken at VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein. A meat analogue in mainstream retail needs to aim at lean pork (about VND 71,000) or below. Priced above beef, it is a premium or novelty item. We found no revealed evidence that supports a mass product priced above pork.
- **Protein.** Do not sell "more protein" to adults. Instead, match meat's protein density in foods that replace meat: 12 to 20 g per 100 g. That compares with 19 g for lean pork and a 4.7 g median for today's *chay* foods ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]). At 10 g per 100 g a food qualifies for a "high protein" claim ([[ch13-consumers]]).
- **Format.** Build for the dish: toppings, fillings, rolls, balls and mince for home cooking and street food.
- **Growth.** Poultry, not pork, carries most projected meat growth to 2035, so poultry-style formats deserve as much attention as pork analogues. Urban growth is in eating out, which points to selling through foodservice and other businesses ([[ch15-channels]]).
- **Nutrition.** Do not pitch a new protein as the answer to child undernutrition. For school-age children, including upland boarders, published menus and weighed diets show that lunch and dinner meet protein and zinc needs. The gaps are breakfast, calcium and vitamin A. A plant dish that removes all the meat needs added zinc and little sodium; a blend in minced dishes needs nothing extra. Stunting is set before age two. There a plant product has to meet the young-child protein-quality pattern and must register as a food for children under 36 months. It also has to beat fortified cereal and milk products {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

**For each reader.**

- **Startups:** set price targets per 100 g of protein against eggs, chicken and lean pork, and design formats for named dishes.
- **Investors:** demand is growing in tonnes, but the money is in low-price everyday protein. Revealed evidence does not support business models that need consumer premiums above about 20%.
- **Manufacturers:** a pork extender saves money at every pork price. As a hedge it removes only about a sixth of a recipe's exposure to a pork shock. So value it on the saving, and test a new extender against your own soy recipe.
- **Research bodies:** measure protein per serving in common dishes, and publish a current food composition table that includes dishes and *chay* foods. Measure phytate (a plant compound that lowers zinc and iron uptake), zinc and DIAAS of tofu, textured soy and blended minced dishes, on both the young-child and older-child patterns. Add textured soy to the composition table that school menu software uses.
- **Policy makers:** the 2026 recommendations, the Ten tips and Decision 1982 already favour diverse protein, including legumes. School guidance (Decision 3958/QĐ-BYT) sets no animal share, although the menu software still prints an animal-protein standard of at least 48% ([[ch15-channels]]). In the menu software, state zinc and iron per lunch by absorption band, and sodium per lunch. The absorption band shows how much of each mineral the body can take up from the meal. Note that mandatory fortification (Decree 09/2016) reaches no soy protein food [@APR-04; @NQR-02; @APR-16; @APR-17] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **International organisations:** treat the urban "too much meat" problem and child undernutrition as separate problems. Within child undernutrition, separate school-age children from children under two. School-age plates already carry protein and zinc; breakfast, calcium and vitamin A are the gaps. For children under two the issue is complementary-food standards, where plant products face the young-child protein-quality pattern, registration, and trial evidence that favours milk or fortified blends. An India roundtable is examining adding plant and fermented proteins to feeding schemes on cost and protein-quality grounds [@AFN-14] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:stated}. Vietnam's school plates point to micronutrients instead. A fortified plant item for boarding-school breakfasts or charity-funded lunches is a research option to test on calcium and vitamin A as well as zinc. It is not a product brief.
