# 27. Policy options

**In brief.**
- Vietnam does not need a new law or a new agency to give new protein foods a workable legal path.
- The most valuable step is also cheap and time-bound: a short clause in the new Food Safety Law. It would let the Government regulate "new foods and new food ingredients" and put the detail in a decree. First comments come at the October 2026 National Assembly session, and the vote is considered at the May 2027 session. So the clause needs to be in the revised draft by early 2027.
- Two more steps follow closely. One is a joint note from the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE) and the Ministry of Health (MOH). It would say how the new decree on genetically modified organisms (GMOs) treats proteins made by engineered microbes (precision fermentation). The other is a proportionate novel-food procedure in the decree that implements the law.
- The costly options are shared infrastructure: a food-grade pilot fermentation line and a protein-quality laboratory.
- We set out 33 options and rank the 19 current ones. For each, we name the owning agency, the instrument to amend and a precedent. This is policy analysis, not legal advice.

> **Demand-side reasons.** Part III (the demand chapters) gives demand-side reasons for several of these options. It also adds seven cheap rules on labels, names, public buying and statistics (demand moves DMV-13 to DMV-19 in [[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]). They can use the same Food Safety Law window.

> **Timing and labels.** The law's timing is now settled: first comments in October 2026 and a vote considered in May 2027 [@APR-07] {VN-direct|Medium}. Research on labels and tariffs narrows three options [@LBL-10; @LBL-06; @FTR-32] {VN-direct|Medium}:
> - The GMO label applies only above 5% of ingredients, so the labelling half of option 2 shrinks to one question on detectability.
> - No rule reserves meat or milk words, so option 12 keeps the allergen list and turns naming into guidance.
> - Protein ingredients from China and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) already enter duty-free, so option 19 touches only other origins.

> **Neutrality note.** AltProtein Vietnam presents these as options with their evidence, costs and trade-offs. They are framed around food security, industrial value and trade. Choosing among them is for Vietnamese policy makers.

## 27.1 Principles behind the ranking

1. **Proportionate, not permissive or prohibitive.** Most new proteins are ordinary foods with a new source. Rules should gate what is new, not everything that is different ([[ch07-rules]]).
2. **Rely on other regulators' work.** Under Decree 43/2026, Vietnam already fast-tracks GMOs approved in five developed countries of the OECD or the G20 [@REG-18; @REG-23] {VN-direct|Medium}. The same logic can apply to novel foods.
3. **Publish timelines and fees.** The regional study's most specific advice for Vietnam is to publish procedures and statutory timelines [@RGN-01] {VN-direct|High}. Korea published its fee and review period before any approval, and that gave applicants something to plan around [@RGN-34] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
4. **Share capital, not only tax.** Capital charges and maintenance are most of the cost of fermentation protein ([[ch09-economics]]). The regional study ranks capital grants and loan guarantees above tax holidays [@RGN-01] {general|High}.
5. **Use windows while they are open.** The Food Safety Law revision is a one-off opportunity; the next revision may be years away [@REG2-04; @REG2-05] {VN-direct|Medium}.

## 27.2 The ranked options

We ranked the options by their effect on whether firms can invest, by how feasible they are and by how urgent they are. Each option keeps its code (PO-001 to PO-033) from `policy_options.csv`, which holds the full table with sources and notes.

Effort means:
- **Low:** a letter, guidance or a list update.
- **Medium:** a circular or a decree amendment.
- **High:** a new chapter of law, a new regime or major spending.

Owners use these short forms: MAE and MOH (above); VFA (Vietnam Food Administration, part of MOH); MOST (Ministry of Science and Technology); MOF (Ministry of Finance); MOIT (Ministry of Industry and Trade); FIRI (Food Industries Research Institute, under MOIT). Terms such as reliance, sandbox and tolling are in the glossary ([[app-r3-glossary]]).

## 27.3 Trade-offs to weigh

- **Speed against caution.** A reliance lane (option 3) speeds access for foods approved elsewhere, but it imports other regulators' judgements. A common middle path limits it to foods approved by two or more reference regulators. It also keeps a veto for concerns specific to Vietnam, such as diet patterns and allergens.
- **Gate against open door.** Today, new protein foods enter through self-declaration, with legal uncertainty ([[ch07-rules]]). A new procedure adds a gate for genuinely new ingredients. Without timelines and enough assessors, it could slow products that are legal today. Transition rules (option 14) and a "not novel" decision reduce that risk.
- **Tariffs: a smaller trade-off than it looked.** Textured protein, isolates and gluten from China, ASEAN and Korea already enter at 0%. They come in under four trade agreements: ASEAN's agreements with China and with Korea, the ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership. Yeast and single-cell protein (protein from microbial cells) from China and ASEAN enter at 0% too. So the MFN duties shield a domestic textured-protein play (T1) or local yeast makers only from the United States and other origins with no trade agreement [@FTR-32; @FTR-33] {VN-direct|Medium}. Cutting them would change little for most buyers; raising them would not reach Chinese product.
- **GMO status of fermentation products.** A light-touch reading for purified proteins (option 2) follows the product-based logic that Decree 43/2026 already applies. But some consumers and trading partners may expect GMO labelling. The mandatory label already applies only above 5% of ingredients, and not where the gene and its product cannot be detected. So low-dose uses such as lactoferrin (about 0.08% of a formula) are outside it; the open question is uses at high inclusion [@LBL-10; @LBL-25] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Public money.** A pilot line (option 7) is the one large public outlay. The case rests on use by several firms and research groups. Without an anchor host and operator, it risks becoming an idle asset, as has happened elsewhere.

The 2025 mergers make coordination simpler than before. One ministry (MAE) now holds GMO biosafety and genetic resources, as well as feed. One (MOST) holds standards, the sandbox, high-tech status and intellectual property ([[ch07-rules]], [[app-s10-admin-map]]).

## 27.5 What this means

- **Policy makers:** the three highest-ranked options need no new agency and belong in 2026 and 2027. Options 1 and 2 cost little; option 3 takes more drafting. Option 1 closes once the law is passed. The vote is considered at the May 2027 session, so the clause needs to be in the draft revised between readings, by early 2027.
- **International bodies:** the most useful support is technical. It includes drafting help for the novel-food decree (through agreements between regulators), funding for the protein-quality laboratory and co-funding of a pilot line.
- **Research bodies:** options 10 and 11 (protein-quality testing and the by-product atlas) are public goods that research bodies can lead. Proposals ready before the next funding round will move faster.
- **Investors and companies:** track options 1 to 3 as signposts ([[ch19-outlook-2035]]). A written ministry opinion on your specific ingredient is still the cheapest way to reduce risk today.
