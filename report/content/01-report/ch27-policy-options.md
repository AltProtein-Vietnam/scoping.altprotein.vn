# 27. Policy options

**In brief.**
- Vietnam needs no new law or agency to give new protein foods a workable legal path.
- The most valuable step is cheap and time-bound: a short clause in the new Food Safety Law letting the Government regulate "new foods and new food ingredients". It has to be in the draft by early 2027, before the vote considered for May 2027.
- Two more steps follow closely: a joint ministry note on how the 2026 GMO decree treats proteins made by engineered microbes, and a proportionate novel-food procedure in the law's decree.
- The costly options are shared infrastructure: a food-grade pilot fermentation line and a protein-quality laboratory.
- We rank 19 options and name the agency, the legal instrument and a precedent for each. This is policy analysis, not legal advice.

> **Neutrality note.** AltProtein Vietnam presents these as options, with their evidence, costs and trade-offs, framed around food security, industrial value and trade. Choosing among them is for Vietnamese policy makers. Part III adds seven cheap rules on labels, names, public buying and statistics that can use the same Food Safety Law window ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).

## 27.1 Principles behind the ranking

1. **Proportionate, not permissive or prohibitive.** Most new proteins are ordinary foods from a new source. Rules should gate what is new, not everything that is different ([[ch07-rules]]).
2. **Rely on other regulators' work.** Vietnam already fast-tracks GMOs approved in five developed countries [@REG-18; @REG-23] {VN-direct|Medium}. The same logic can apply to novel foods.
3. **Publish timelines and fees.** The regional study's most specific advice for Vietnam is to publish procedures and deadlines [@RGN-01] {VN-direct|High}. Korea published its fee and review period before any approval [@RGN-34] {VN-adjacent|Medium}.
4. **Share capital, not only tax.** Capital is most of the cost of fermentation protein ([[ch09-economics]]). Capital grants and loan guarantees beat tax holidays [@RGN-01] {general|High}.
5. **Use windows while they are open.** The Food Safety Law revision is a one-off; the next may be years away [@REG2-04; @REG2-05] {VN-direct|Medium}.

## 27.2 The ranked options

We ranked options by their effect on whether firms can invest, by how feasible they are and by how urgent. Each keeps its code (PO-001 to PO-034).

Effort means: **low**, a letter, guidance or list update; **medium**, a circular or decree amendment; **high**, a new chapter of law, a new regime or major spending.

Owners: MAE (Ministry of Agriculture and Environment), MOH (Ministry of Health) and its Vietnam Food Administration (VFA), MOST (Ministry of Science and Technology), MOF (Ministry of Finance), MOIT (Ministry of Industry and Trade) and FIRI (the national food research institute, under MOIT).

### Tier 1: cheap and time-critical (2026 to 2027)

| Rank | Option | Owner | Instrument | Precedent | Effort | Timeline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (PO-015) | A short clause in the new Food Safety Law: "The Government shall regulate the management of new foods and new food ingredients", with a definition based on history of use | MOH (VFA) drafts; National Assembly reviews | Draft Food Safety Law | Singapore 2025; Thailand 2016 | Low | Before the May 2027 vote |
| 2 (PO-016) | A joint MAE and MOH note on two points. First, a purified protein made by an engineered microbe in a closed plant, with no living GMO or GM DNA in the product, needs no GMO food certificate. Second, whether such a protein counts as a detectable GMO product, which decides the label above 5% | MAE; MOH | Official letter | Decree 43/2026 already separates organisms from products; EU groups asked the same in 2026 | Low | 3 to 6 months |
| 3 (PO-019) | A novel-food procedure in the law's decree: a "not novel" ruling on request; a fast lane for traditional foods; reliance on two or more trusted regulators; full assessment by recognised assessors; a public approved list | MOH; Government | Decree replacing Decree 15/2018 | Thai assessors; EU traditional-food route; Vietnam's GMO fast-track | Medium | In force 2027 to 2028 |

Sources: [@REG2-04; @REG2-05; @REG2-11; @REG-18; @REG-23; @REG2-26; @APR-07; @LBL-10; @PPE-06] {VN-direct\|Medium}.

**Why these first.** Option 1 works only while the law is being drafted [@APR-07] {VN-direct|Medium}. Option 2 removes the biggest legal unknown for precision fermentation at the cost of a letter. It could ask firms to show no living production cells, no GM DNA and no antibiotic-resistance markers, as European groups proposed in 2026 [@PPE-06] {general|Medium}. Option 3 gives the clause its detail.

For cultivated meat and seafood, option 3's reliance lane has a ready basis: a 2023 safety framework from FAO and the World Health Organization, and Singapore's published approvals [@SLC-05; @SLC-06; @RGN-04] {general|Medium}.

**Why option 10 covers function.** Buyers specify how a protein behaves, such as gel strength in sausage or stability in long-life drinks [@BUY-05; @BUY-16] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. We found no laboratory in Vietnam that sells such tests [@SLP-17] {VN-direct|Low}. An open method guide exists [@SLP-10] {general|Medium}. This extends the protein-quality laboratory (public good P4, [[ch26-plays]]).

**Why option 16 is narrow.** A 2026 decree already speeded up access licences; benefit sharing of at least 1% of revenue is unchanged [@SLF-02; @REG-60] {VN-direct|High}. What is missing is standard terms for public collections, which would support candidate public good P7.

## 27.3 Trade-offs to weigh

- **Speed against caution.** A reliance lane speeds access, but borrows other regulators' judgements. A middle path limits it to foods approved by two or more trusted regulators, and keeps a veto for concerns specific to Vietnam, such as diet and allergens.
- **Gate against open door.** Today new protein foods enter by self-declaration, with legal uncertainty ([[ch07-rules]]). A new procedure adds a gate for genuinely new ingredients. Without deadlines and enough assessors, it could slow products that are legal today. Transition rules and a "not novel" ruling reduce that risk.
- **Tariffs: a smaller trade-off than it looks.** Protein ingredients from China, Southeast Asia and Korea already enter duty-free [@FTR-32; @FTR-33] {VN-direct|Medium}. Cutting other duties would change little; raising them would not reach Chinese product.
- **GMO status.** A light-touch reading for purified proteins follows the logic the 2026 decree already applies, but some consumers may expect a GMO label. Low-dose uses are already outside the label; the open question is high-dose uses [@LBL-10; @LBL-25] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Public money.** A pilot line is the one large public outlay. Without an anchor host and operator, it risks becoming an idle asset, as has happened elsewhere.

## 27.5 What this means

- **Policy makers:** the three top options need no new agency. Options 1 and 2 cost little; option 3 takes more drafting. Option 1 closes when the law passes, so the clause must be in the draft by early 2027.
- **International bodies:** the most useful support is technical: drafting help for the novel-food decree, funding for the protein-quality laboratory, and co-funding for a pilot line.
- **Researchers:** options 10 and 11 are public goods you can lead. A themed research call (PO-034) would give a first, small source of money.
- **Investors and companies:** track options 1 to 3 as signposts ([[ch19-outlook-2035]]). A written ministry opinion on your ingredient is still the cheapest way to cut risk today.
