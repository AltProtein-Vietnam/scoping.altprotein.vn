# 17. Export demand: who abroad would buy Vietnamese alternative protein

**In brief.**
- Vietnam exports almost no protein ingredients. In 2023 it sold USD 0.52 million of textured and concentrated protein abroad, against about USD 34 million of imports under the same code. China exported USD 380 million in 2024.
- Vietnam does export wrapped food at scale: USD 128.2 million of dumplings, wontons and other stuffed pasta in 2023, about 250 times its textured-protein exports. In 2025 it out-supplied China and Thailand in the UK, the US and Korea. An export plant-protein product would join this base, through food exporters rather than plant-based brands.
- Demand abroad for finished plant-based meat is falling in the United States and flat in Europe. European retailers meet their plant-protein targets with pulses, price cuts and hybrids (products that mix meat and plant protein) made in Europe. Japanese and Korean buyers take textured protein from China and from their own plants.
- Vietnam's clearest advantage is tariffs. It pays zero duty in the EU, the UK and Japan on lines where Thai and Chinese goods pay 6 to 13%. On dumplings in Japan, it has a 17.4-point edge over China and Thailand.
- The edge has conditions. EU-bound wrapped foods may contain at most 20% non-originating flour or starch by weight, and Vietnam cannot ship meat-filled foods to the EU. The United States now charges Vietnamese goods an extra 12.5%, the EU restricts 32 meat names, and any novel ingredient needs its own approval abroad.
- Seafood buyers' rules for aquaculture feed are context only (17.5). This study concentrates on protein for people.

Four points stand out {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

- **Textured protein is untested as an export, and China sets its price.** China exported USD 380 million under HS 2106.10 in 2024, at about USD 2.13 per kg on weights estimated by UN Comtrade. The code covers all protein concentrates as well as textured protein [@EXP-40] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Vietnam does not use its tariff edge here. EU27 imports of textured protein from Vietnam were USD 0.01 million (3 t) in 2025, against USD 42.6 million from China [@XBA-28] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Vietnam already sells wrapped foods at scale.** In 2025, partner-reported imports of stuffed pasta from Vietnam were USD 48.1 million in the US, 32.4 million in the EU27, 15.5 million in Japan, 14.8 million in the UK, 13.3 million in Korea and 10.6 million in Australia. Vietnam out-supplied China and Thailand in the UK, the US and Korea and was second to China in the EU, Japan and Australia [@XBA-28] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Most of these foods are filled with meat or seafood. The Vietnam-made plant items we found on Western shelves are led by vegetables, at 3.6 to 6.2 g of protein per 100 g. They are a supermarket own-brand jackfruit fillet and a vegetable bun in the UK, vegetable spring rolls in North America and a vegetable gyoza in Australia [@XBA-26; @ORG-12] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.
- **Seafood chains may already sell protein ingredients to Europe.** Vietnam exported USD 13.0 million of protein substances (HS 3504) in 2023, mainly to Spain, the UK, Korea and Denmark [@EXP-39] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Vinh Hoan, a pangasius exporter, sells collagen peptides and gelatin [@EXP-49]. Vietnam is the largest producer of pangasius certified by the Aquaculture Stewardship Council (ASC) [@EXP-32] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. In our reading, certified seafood chains such as Vinh Hoan's collagen line are the nearest model for selling a novel protein ingredient to businesses abroad. We found no source on what European buyers ask of these ingredients {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.
- **The firms that hold foreign buyers are food exporters, not plant-based brands.** Wrapped-food, cashew and seafood exporters already have the buyers, certifications and cold or dry export logistics. We found no Vietnamese exporter of cashew milk or cashew cheese with verifiable volumes, and no Vietnamese seafood exporter selling plant-based seafood abroad [@EXP-19; @EXP-49; @XBA-20] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. One small maker of mushroom and mycelium (fungal root-thread) foods reports exports of about 10% of its revenue to the UK, Australia, India and the Netherlands [@AIS-39] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.

Growth in rich markets is in plain, cheap, versatile formats. Tofu, tempeh and seitan grew by almost 30% in Germany and the Netherlands in 2025. US "chunks, shreds and strips" grew in units while the category fell, and private label leads European volume [@EXP-05; @EXP-01] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Brands are moving the same way. Singapore's TiNDLE switched to private label only from 2026 and plans to sell its US business. Its former chief executive and co-founder said there is "no real underlying consumer demand to shift away from meat" [@AIS-28; @AIS-29] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Retailer protein targets do not change this. Where they bite, retailers meet them with what is cheapest and closest: pulses, price parity and hybrids made in Europe. Examples are Albert Heijn's 15 hybrid products with field bean protein and Lidl's 60/40 beef and pea mince [@XBA-01; @XBA-02] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Their direct food suppliers are European. Lidl's list of about 750 food suppliers for 2025 deliveries has none in East, South or Southeast Asia; Asian foods reach it through European importers and traders [@XBA-07] {general|High} {dx:revealed}.

So the targets are not a pull for analogues made in Asia. A Vietnamese co-packer (a contract maker that packs products for another firm's brand) reaches a European discounter through an importer or brand owner that holds the contract (our reading) {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}. That is the segment a low-cost, tariff-free Asian contract manufacturer could serve. We found no verified case of a Western plant-based brand manufacturing in Vietnam, and no revealed sales data for China, Singapore, Australia or the Gulf [[app-r1-open-questions]].

## 17.3 Access: a tariff edge with conditions, and four barriers

**The tariff edge.** Vietnamese textured protein, food preparations, plant drinks, soy sauce, stuffed pasta and prepared nuts enter the EU at 0% under the EU and Vietnam free trade agreement. Thai and Chinese goods pay the EU's most-favoured-nation rates (the standard rates for countries without a trade deal). These are 12.8% on textured protein, 9% plus an agricultural component on food preparations, 9.6% on plant drinks (duty-free for Vietnam since 1 January 2025) and 7 to 10.2% on prepared nuts [@EXP-23] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. The UK gives the same preference [@EXP-24] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Japan admits Vietnamese textured vegetable protein free, against 10.6% for most countries and 6.6% for Chinese goods [@EXP-41] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The EU and Thailand are still negotiating; the EU and Indonesia agreement was finalised in September 2025 but is not in force [@EXP-47; @EXP-48] {general|High}. {{kn:kn-eu-tariff-edge}}

The edge is largest on wrapped foods, where Vietnam already sells. Vietnamese dumplings and other stuffed pasta enter the EU at 0%, against 8.3% plus EUR 6.10 (cooked) or EUR 17.10 (uncooked) per 100 kg, and Canada free, against 11%. Japan charges Vietnamese stuffed pasta 3.9% in fiscal 2026 under the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP). China and Thailand have no concession and pay 21.3%. That is a 17.4-point edge on a line where Vietnam sold USD 15.5 million in 2025 [@ORG-02; @ORG-05; @ORG-07; @XBA-28] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. The UK charges 0% under both its Vietnam agreement and CPTPP [@ORG-03] {VN-direct|High}.

{{chart:chart-tariff-edge}}

**Barrier 1: the United States.** Since 24 July 2026 the US has charged an extra 12.5% tariff on Vietnamese goods under Section 301 of its trade law. Malaysia, Indonesia and India pay 10% and Thailand 12.5%. Cashews and coconuts are exempt; prepared plant-based foods are not [@EXP-22] {VN-direct|High}. The US is also the largest buyer of Vietnamese stuffed pasta (USD 48.1 million in 2025, up 71% on 2024), and that growth is now exposed to the surcharge [@XBA-28] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. With US plant-based meat sales falling, this argues against sending plant-based exports to the US first. {{kn:kn-us-301-tariff}}

**Barrier 2: labels.** Since 19 August 2026 the EU restricts 32 meat terms on plant-based foods, including "meat", "chicken", "pork", "beef", "bacon" and "steak". "Burger", "sausage" and "nuggets" remain allowed for now, with a committee vote due in December 2026 [@EXP-25] {general|Medium}. Dairy names such as "milk" and "cheese" are reserved for milk products, and a 2017 court ruling confirmed that this applies to plant-based foods [@EXP-26] {general|Medium}. Export packs need descriptive names ("soy protein strips", "shiitake dumplings").

**Barrier 3: novel-food approvals abroad.** A Vietnamese product made from soy, gluten, cashew, coconut, rice, mushrooms or jackfruit needs no approval before sale in the EU or Singapore. A new fungal strain, a precision-fermented protein (made by microbes set up to produce one specific protein) or cultivated meat (grown from animal cells) does [@EXP-46; @EXP-34] {general|High}. Vietnam has no new-food route of its own ([[ch07-rules]]), so a Vietnamese exporter of a novel protein would file its first dossier abroad. On 10 August 2026 the US proposed tighter oversight of substances claimed to be "generally recognised as safe" (GRAS) [@EXP-45] {general|High}.

**Barrier 4: rules of origin.** The zero rate depends on the recipe. We read the origin protocol of the EU agreement. Its rules match the UK and Vietnam text on the chapter 19 rule (cereal, flour and starch foods) and the tolerance article [@ORG-01; @XBA-30] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

- **Wrapped foods to the EU.** For dumplings, buns and other chapter 19 foods, the wrapper is the binding limit. Non-originating flour, starch or rice may make up at most 20% of product weight, and the general tolerance cannot lift that cap. Flour milled in Vietnam from imported wheat is non-originating; Vietnamese rice flour and cassava starch are originating. Imported textured soy is not limited in dumplings [@ORG-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- ***Chay* food preparations to the EU.** Under heading 2106, imported textured soy may make up 10% of the weight of *chay* (vegetarian) food preparations. On our reading of the text, 10% of the ex-works (factory-gate) price is also allowed. Soy isolate and wheat gluten are not limited [@ORG-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **The UK, Japan and Canada.** CPTPP asks only for a change of chapter for stuffed pasta, so the recipe does not bind for these markets. The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) sets the same rule for Japan [@XBA-30; @ORG-06] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Meat.** Vietnam is not on the EU list of countries allowed to ship meat products (as adopted in 2021). Meat-filled stuffed pasta is not yet duty-free (EUR 6.787 per 100 kg in 2026, against EUR 54.30). So EU-bound Vietnamese wrapped foods are seafood-filled or plant-filled, and a plant-protein filling is the practical EU substitute for pork [@ORG-10; @ORG-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

The rules give domestic textured soy no export customer. A dumpling keeps its origin whatever soy it uses, and a *chay* preparation can meet the 2106 rule with isolate or gluten (our reading) {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

The EU deforestation regulation applies to soy from 30 December 2026 for large and medium operators. Our reading is that Vietnamese plant-based foods made from imported soy protein fall outside it unless exported as soybeans, soy flour, oil or cake; this should be confirmed [@EXP-27; @EXP-28] {general|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 17.4 The diaspora channel: real, small, *chay*

Vietnamese communities abroad are a real but small channel, and what they buy is *chay*. About 2.3 million people of Vietnamese origin lived in the US in 2023, and 326,630 Vietnam-born people in Australia in 2025 [@EXP-35; @EXP-36] {general|High}. On 24 September 2026 a US Asian online grocer listed Vietnam-made foods at USD 3.50 to 13 a pack. They included vegetarian net spring rolls, vegan lemongrass "chicken" jerky, vegetarian soups, bao buns, oyster sauce, noodles, fermented tofu and pho broth bases. None was marketed on protein, and US diaspora firms also make chay bases locally [@EXP-37] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

Protein-rich chay already travels through Asian grocers. Dry soy pieces at 48 to 61 g of protein per 100 g, made from imported soy isolate, are recorded under Vietnamese brands in France, Germany, Spain, Norway and Colombia. So are analogues at 40 to 42 g [@XBA-26; @XBA-27] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Noodle and pasta exports (HS 1902.30) going to the US, Germany and Poland are consistent with a diaspora channel [@EXP-39] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

This channel could take protein-upgraded chay products at similar prices, with frozen logistics in place. The gap is protein in frozen formats, not in dry pieces. The channel will not justify a novel-protein plant on its own {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

## 17.5 Feed rules for seafood: context, not a target

Seafood buyers set rules for the feed behind Vietnamese shrimp and pangasius. This study concentrates on protein for people, so these rules are context here, not a target. Version 0.7 read them as the demand test for two microbial feed plays (T2 and T3). Those plays were retired because feed is now context only ([[app-m5-changelog]]). The detail stays in [[app-d6-export]] (D6.7) and [[app-s6-feed-market]].

- **Standards.** The ASC Feed Standard v1.2 has been mandatory since 2 February 2026. Certified mills must commit to deforestation- and conversion-free soy and palm, improve marine-ingredient sourcing over time, and report greenhouse gas emissions per tonne of feed, with no target [@FBA-01; @EXP-30] {general|High}. From 1 May 2028 all ASC farm audits require compound feed from an ASC-certified mill [@FBA-02; @FBA-03] {general|Medium}. On 25 September 2026 the ASC register listed 15 certified Vietnamese feed mills, the most of any country (15 of 114), and 6 more in initial audit [@FBA-04] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **No reward for microbial protein.** None of the buyer rules we found requires or rewards yeast, fungal or bacterial biomass. The Best Aquaculture Practices standard's credit is for algae and other aquatic microorganisms, and we found no buyer that pays a documented premium for seafood raised on novel feed [@FBA-01; @FBA-05] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The buyer mix is also shifting: in January to August 2026, seafood exports to China and Hong Kong rose 33% while those to the EU fell 1.6% [@EXP-31] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Soy and deforestation.** UK supermarkets including Tesco, Sainsbury's, Aldi and Lidl, and seafood firms, committed to deforestation- and conversion-free soy, including soy embedded in products, by the end of 2025 [@EXP-33] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. Fish and crustaceans are not covered commodities under the EU deforestation regulation, so it does not reach Vietnamese shrimp and pangasius exports through their feed. It reaches them only through retailer and certification rules (our reading) [@EXP-27] {general|Low} {dx:inferred}.
- **Inactive yeast is traded for food and feed together.** Partner countries recorded 9,193 t of inactive yeast and other dead single-cell organisms (HS 2102.20) shipped to Vietnam in 2025, 2.4 times the 2019 tonnage. Of this, 95% came in at about USD 1.1 per kg, mainly from China and Brazil, and Vietnam also exported 8,166 t at USD 1.39 per kg [@FBA-17; @FBA-19] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The trade code does not separate feed from food.

## 17.6 Competitors and what would make Vietnam a preferred supplier

China dominates exports of protein concentrates and textured protein (HS 2106.10) and approved its first mycoprotein (protein from fungal biomass) in 2025. Thailand has private-label plant-based exporters and a future-food roadmap, but no EU agreement in force. Malaysia has a halal system and a lower US tariff [@EXP-40; @EXP-12; @EXP-13; @EXP-11; @EXP-52; @EXP-22; @EXP-47] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. In Japan, Fuji Oil supplies textured soy from its own plant in Japan [@XBA-18] {general|High} {dx:revealed}.

CJ, a foreign owner of Vietnamese wrapped-food export capacity, opened its Kizuna dumpling plant in Tay Ninh (former Long An) in 2022 to supply Japan, the EU and Australia. In 2024 it planned to make its dumplings for Europe in Hungary from the second half of 2026 [@XBA-20; @XBA-23] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.

Our synthesis of what would make Vietnam a preferred supplier {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}:

- **For EU and UK buyers, through importers and brand owners:** the offer is zero tariffs (6 to 13 points over Thailand and China), origin-compliant wrappers and frozen logistics already used by seafood exporters. It also needs seafood-grade food safety, certified to IFS Food or BRCGS (two food-safety standards European retailers ask for). The product should be a plant-protein version of a wrapped food these buyers already take from Vietnam. The first buyers are European importers of Asian frozen foods and brand owners already making wrapped foods in Vietnam, not the retailers themselves [@EXP-23; @EXP-24; @XBA-04; @XBA-11; @XBA-07; @XBA-20; @XBA-25] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
- **For Japanese buyers of wrapped foods:** the 17.4-point CPTPP edge on dumplings, with no recipe condition beyond a change of chapter. We do not know who in Japan buys Vietnamese dumplings, or whether they would take a plant-protein version.
- **For Japanese and Korean ingredient buyers (weakly evidenced):** Vietnam has a 6.6-point edge over China in Japan. Even so, textured protein from Vietnam sold under USD 0.35 million a year in each market in 2024 to 2025. The leading Japanese maker is expanding at home, and a leading finished brand dropped several products in 2025 [@XBA-28; @XBA-18; @XBA-17; @EXP-41] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Keep this route only for a specified fungal or fermented protein with a named buyer; drop plain textured soy.
- **Against:** the US tariff, no domestic protein-ingredient base, no novel-food approvals abroad, reliance on imported soy and gluten for every high-protein local product, European lists of direct suppliers, and CJ's move of EU supply to Hungary.

## 17.7 What this means for the frontier

**For each reader.**
- **Startups:** the best-evidenced first export product is a vegetable or plant-protein version of a dumpling, bun or rice-paper roll that a buyer already takes from Vietnam. Sell it through European importers of Asian foods or brand owners that already make wrapped foods in Vietnam, and name it without restricted meat or dairy terms. Protein-upgraded chay for the diaspora comes second; the gap there is protein in frozen formats. Functional plant or fungal protein for Japanese and Korean manufacturers is weakly evidenced and needs a named buyer and specification first. Plant-based seafood for export remains the weakest bet on current evidence.
- **Investors:** export demand justifies co-packing capacity with IFS Food or BRCGS certification more than a new brand. Price the US tariff and CJ's move of EU supply to Hungary explicitly, prefer buyers without EU plants, and budget for foreign approvals for any novel ingredient. Export origin rules give a domestic textured-soy plant (play T1, domestic textured plant protein, [[ch26-plays]]) no customer.
- **Manufacturers:** IFS Food or BRCGS is the entry ticket. Lidl requires IFS or BRC with annual audits, and 74.8% of Carrefour's supplier sites in France were certified to IFS, BRC or FSSC 22000 in 2025. FSSC 22000 alone is not what Lidl names [@XBA-04; @XBA-11] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. For EU-bound wrapped foods, check the flour share of the wrapper and use Vietnamese rice flour or cassava starch where needed; ship to the UK, Japan and Canada under CPTPP. Cashew and seafood processors already own the buyers and cold chain that an export plant-based or hybrid line needs.
- **Research bodies:** for wrapped foods, cost a compliant rice-flour or cassava-starch wrapper against wheat.
- **Policy makers:** protect and use the EU, UK and Japanese preferences (6 to 13 points on plant-based lines and 17.4 points on dumplings in Japan). Publish an origin note for wrapped-food exporters on the EU 20% flour and starch cap and on the 10% tolerance by weight or value; imported soy protein is not the main constraint. Build a new-food route that mirrors EU and Singapore evidence rules, so that one dossier serves several markets.
- **International organisations and funders:** replace a buyer survey with quote requests. Ask three EU importers of Asian frozen foods and two brand owners already sourcing wrapped foods from Vietnam to price one vegetable and one plant-protein wrapped product. Ask them also for their certification and origin rules. Retailer targets need no survey: they are published. Fund a first EU or Singapore dossier for a Vietnamese fungal or single-cell protein (protein from yeast, bacteria or other microbes grown as biomass).
