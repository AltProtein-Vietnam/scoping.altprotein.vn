# 1. Why Vietnam: the protein people eat, and the imports behind it

**In brief.**
- Vietnamese people eat more meat each year: 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, against a recommended 50 to 80 g. Animal foods supply 42% of protein.
- About one fifth of the protein supply is imported food. Meat imports are growing, because costs abroad are lower, not because of subsidies.
- Domestic farms run on imported feed. Counting that feed, about two thirds of the animal protein people eat rests on imports (our calculation).
- Feed-price shocks land mainly on farms. Disease is the shock consumers feel: pork prices rose 57% in 2020 after African swine fever.
- The case for making protein at home rests first on security, and it is real but narrow. Only protein made from domestic inputs reduces import dependence, and at the scale demand supports, it adds a second source rather than cutting exposure.

> **Method note.** This study is about protein for people. Feed appears only as context: it is the hidden import behind the meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish that people eat.

## 1.1 What Vietnamese people eat, and where it comes from

**People eat a lot of meat, and more each year.** The General Nutrition Survey 2019 to 2020 measured meat intake of 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, up from 84 g in 2010. The national recommendation is 50 to 80 g [@DIE-01; @DIE-02; @DIE-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Animal foods supply a growing share of protein.** In 2023 total protein supply was 102.2 g per person per day, and 42% of it came from animal foods. That share was 21% in 1990 [@DIE-07; @DIE-08] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Rice still supplies 27.0 g of protein a day, and soy foods 4.9 g [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Chapter 11 gives the full picture ([[ch11-protein-diet]]).

{{kn:kn-animal-protein-share}}

**Vietnam's own farms and ponds produce meat, eggs, milk and fish at scale, and output is still growing.**

- **Meat.** In 2025 Vietnam produced 5.39 Mt (million tonnes) of pork and 2.60 Mt of poultry meat, live weight [@MAC-14; @MAC-12] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Eggs and milk.** Farms produced 21.4 billion eggs and 1.3 Mt of fresh milk in 2025 [@MAC-14] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Fish and shrimp.** Fisheries output reached 9.95 Mt in 2025, of which 6.12 Mt was farmed. Much of the shrimp and pangasius is exported (section 1.5) [@MAC-15] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Still growing.** In the first half of 2026, pork output rose 4.8% and poultry 5.6% [@MAC-18] {VN-direct|Medium}.

{{chart:chart-animal-protein-output}}

**About one fifth of the protein people eat is imported food.** In 2023 about 19% to 23% of the protein supply was imported, up from 11% to 13% in 2010. The low figure counts net imports, the high figure gross imports (our calculation) [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|Medium}.

- **Mostly imported:** soybeans for tofu and soy milk, wheat and milk. Beef, offal and groundnuts were 40% to 47% imported, up from near zero in 2010 [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Produced at home:** pork, eggs, fish and rice. About 90% of the meat supply was domestic in 2023. Their exposure runs through feed (section 1.2) [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High}.

**Meat and dairy imports are a second, smaller exposure, and they are growing.** Vietnam imported 978,000 t of meat worth USD 2.0 billion in 2025, 12% more by value than in 2024. Most was poultry by-products, Indian buffalo meat, frozen pork and offal [@MIM-04; @MIM-05; @MIM-06] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Dairy imports were worth USD 1.44 billion, up 27% [@MIM-12] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

{{kn:kn-meat-imports-2025}} {{kn:kn-dairy-imports-2025}}

**The import share differs by food (2025).** Pork was about 4% imported and poultry 11% to 17% [@MIM-03; @MIM-05; @MIM-06] {VN-direct|Medium}. Beef and buffalo meat were 32% to 48% imported, depending on the data source [@MIM-03; @QNT-01] {VN-direct|Medium}. Domestic milk meets about 30% to 40% of demand [@DIE-07; @MIM-13] {VN-direct|Low}. Where sources disagree, we give both figures.

{{kn:kn-beef-import-share}} {{kn:kn-milk-self-sufficiency}}

**Counting feed, about two thirds of the animal protein people eat rests on imports.** Domestic pigs, poultry and farmed fish eat mostly imported feed (section 1.2). We added the protein these animals owe to imported feed to the animal foods imported directly. On that basis, 61% to 71% of animal protein rested on imports in 2023. For all protein the share is 40% to 46% (our calculation) [@DIE-07; @MAC-01; @FS-23] {VN-direct|Low}. This is an estimate, not a measured flow, and a lower bound. Chapter 22 follows it to 2050 ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]).

## 1.2 The imported feed behind it

**The farms run on compound feed, and most of its protein and grain is imported.** This is context for the study, not a target. Vietnamese mills made about 22 Mt of livestock and poultry feed in 2025, not counting feed for fish and shrimp [@MAC-01; @MAC-28] {VN-direct|Medium}.

| Ingredient (2025) | Imports | Share of supply imported | Main origins | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maize | 11.91 Mt, USD 2.95 bn | about 74% | Argentina, Brazil, United States | [@MAC-08; @MAC-01] {VN-direct\|High} |
| Soybeans | 2.61 Mt, USD 1.21 bn | 98.4% (our calculation) | Brazil, United States | [@MAC-04; @MAC-09] {VN-direct\|High} |
| Soybean meal | 5.70 Mt | about 99% of soy protein (our calculation) | Mostly Argentina | [@MAC-04; @MAC-09] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Wheat (all uses) | 5.45 Mt, USD 1.46 bn | 100% | Australia, Brazil, United States | [@MAC-10] {VN-direct\|Medium} |

{{kn:kn-corn-import-share}}

**Domestic crops cover very little.** Vietnam grew only about 42,000 t of soybeans in 2025, about 2% of what it crushes. Maize output was flat at about 4.1 Mt [@MAC-04; @MAC-01] {VN-direct|High}. New crushing plants process imported beans, so they shift imports from meal to beans without reducing dependence [@VCO-16] {VN-direct|Medium}.

**The import bill.** Feed grains, soybeans, wheat and feed ingredients cost about USD 10.2 billion in 2025 (our calculation from [@MAC-07; @MAC-08; @MAC-09; @MAC-10]). Weighted by protein content, about 93% of feed protein is imported (our calculation) [@FS-23; @MAC-01; @DIE-12; @FBS-04; @FBS-05] {VN-direct|Medium}.

> **Correction.** A widely repeated figure, "41,000 t of soybeans produced against 3.2 Mt imported", mixes 2026 forecasts with 2025 actuals. The 2025 actuals are 42,000 to 43,000 t produced and 2.61 Mt imported [@MAC-04].

## 1.3 Exposure: feed prices hit farms, disease hits consumers

Imported feed passes price shocks abroad straight into farm costs. Disease adds shocks at home, and these are the ones consumers feel.

- **Feed is most of the cost.** Feed is 60% to 70% of livestock production cost, and over 70% for pigs [@MAC-24; @MAC-43] {VN-direct|Low}.
- **2022.** After the war in Ukraine began, wheat import prices rose 37% in a year [@MAC-44] {VN-direct|Medium}. Consumers barely felt it: in 2022 food prices rose 1.6% and pork prices fell [@SEC-09] {VN-direct|High}.
- **2026.** Feed prices rose 10% to 14% from January to April 2026, and farm-gate egg prices fell below cost [@MAC-24] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Disease.** African swine fever killed or forced the culling of about 6 million pigs in 2019, over 20% of the herd. It returned in 2025, when 1.2 million to 1.3 million pigs were culled [@MAC-37; @MAC-01; @MAC-12] {VN-direct|Medium}.

**Disease is the protein shock consumers feel.** Consumer pork prices rose 57% in 2020, after the 2019 epidemic. That alone added nearly 2 points to inflation [@SEC-08] {VN-direct|High}. So imported feed is mainly a risk for farmers, processors and the trade balance. For consumers, the price risk in protein food is mainly disease (our reading) [@SEC-08; @SEC-09; @MAC-43] {VN-direct|Medium}.

{{kn:kn-pork-price-2020}}

**The 2019 shock was large for pork and small for protein.** Pig output fell 14% in 2019 while poultry output rose 17% [@PAN-03] {VN-direct|High}. People switched to chicken, not tofu: home tofu use did not change ([[ch11-protein-diet]]) [@DIE-10; @DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

**Swine fever is now a lasting risk.** In 2025, 79% of virus samples typed in the north were new mixed strains. The agriculture ministry says the three licensed vaccines "have no protective effect" against them [@PAN-33] {VN-direct|High}. Major waves stay at least as likely to 2030 (our reading) [@PAN-33] {VN-direct|Medium} {fx:trend}.

**The steady health cost is foodborne.** The Ministry of Health counted 123 human infections and 2 deaths in 2025 from a pig bacterium, *Streptococcus suis*, caught mainly from raw pig blood [@PAN-15] {VN-direct|Medium}. Bird flu in people is rare: 130 cases since 2003, none in 2026 to September [@PAN-01] {VN-direct|High}.

## 1.4 Growth ahead: more meat per person, and more imported feed

- **People will eat more meat.** The OECD-FAO outlook projects meat use per person rising 25% from 2025 to 2035, led by poultry [@DIE-11] {VN-direct|Medium} {fx:projection}.
- **Population adds little.** It grows only about 5% from 2025 to 2035, so demand growth comes mostly from income and diet [@GT-12] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Official targets point the same way.** The livestock strategy targets 30 to 32 Mt of industrial feed and 6.0 to 6.5 Mt of meat by 2030 [@MAC-23] {VN-direct|High}.
- **No target for local feed inputs.** We found no number for using more domestic feed ingredients in the published strategies [@MAC-23; @REG-54] {VN-direct|High}.

On current trends, every extra tonne of feed will be imported. Chapter 22 follows this to 2050 ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]).

## 1.5 Trade: an exporter of animal protein and an importer of the plant protein behind it

Vietnam exported USD 11.3 billion of seafood in 2025, including about USD 4.6 billion of shrimp and USD 2.2 billion of pangasius [@MAC-15; @MAC-01] {VN-direct|High}.

**Vietnam imports plant protein and energy, turns them into animal protein, and exports much of the result.** Its strength is processing. Its weak point is the input.

The same pattern holds for starch. In 2025 Vietnam became China's largest supplier of cassava starch, at about 2.3 Mt in 11 months. But it sold China almost no glucose [@RGN-51; @RGN-55; @FS-01; @FS-13] {VN-direct|High}. The step that turns starch into fermentation sugar mostly happens after the starch leaves Vietnam ([[ch04-asset-map]], [[ch03-regional]]).

## 1.6 What the case for alternative protein rests on, and what it does not

Four arguments carry different weight.

1. **Security of the protein people eat (strong, with limits).** About two thirds of the animal protein people eat rests on imports, directly or through feed (section 1.1). Protein made in Vietnam adds a second source. This argument has the most evidence, but the remedy is narrow.

   **Only protein made from domestic inputs reduces the dependence.** Fermentation on cassava or sugar, rice and peanut protein, and soy foods from Vietnamese beans use domestic inputs [@SEC-05; @SEC-14; @FS-24; @FS-01] {VN-direct|Medium}. Alternative protein made from imported soy, pea or wheat gluten only moves the dependence, often from the Americas to China. China supplied about 84% of the plant-protein food ingredients reaching Vietnam in 2025 (our calculation) [@BUY-02] {VN-direct|Medium}.

   {{kn:kn-china-share-plant-protein-ingredients}}

   Protein for people saves the most imports per tonne. Replacing 1 t of meat protein avoids about 4.5 t of soybean meal and 9.5 t of maize imports (our calculation; [[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}.

   The limit is scale. On the benchmark demand path, new protein displaces about 0.2% of meat protein by 2035 (our calculation; [[ch18-demand-sizing]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}. It would spare about 142,000 pigs a year in 2035, while swine fever culls in 2025 were 8.4 times that number (our calculation from [@MAC-01; @PAN-03]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. On its own, it does not change national exposure.

   {{kn:kn-asf-culls-vs-spared}}

   **The evidence does not show that it reduces disease risk.** We found no study that measures lower pandemic or drug-resistance risk from replacing meat. Claims that it does come from reviews and opinion pieces [@PAN-27; @PAN-30; @PAN-31] {general|Medium}. Research ties these risks to the wildlife trade, farm biosecurity and antibiotic use [@PAN-28; @PAN-29] {general|Medium}. Vietnamese studies point the same way [@PAN-25; @PAN-26; @PAN-21; @PAN-33] {VN-direct|Medium}.

   **Subsidised imported meat is not the problem.** For most of the meat and milk Vietnam buys, the exporting country gives no price support [@MIM-15] {general|High}. India taxes its buffalo meat rather than supporting it, and agricultural export subsidies ended under World Trade Organization rules [@MIM-15; @MIM-16; @MIM-17] {general|High}. Imported meat is cheaper because costs differ: Vietnamese pig farm prices in 2024 were 1.6 to 2.8 times those in Brazil, Russia, the US and the EU (our calculation) [@MIM-15] {VN-direct|Medium}. New protein would displace at most about 1.6% of imported meat protein by 2035 (our calculation) [@MIM-04; @QNT-01] {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}.

   {{kn:kn-origin-price-support}} {{kn:kn-imported-meat-displaceable-2035}}

2. **Industrial value (strong, but conditional).** Two steps could stay at home: textured plant protein to replace imports from China ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]), and turning exported cassava starch into fermentation sugar (section 1.5). This fits national biotechnology policy ([[ch07-rules]]). It depends on costs ([[ch09-economics]]) and on a legal route to market.
3. **Environment (real, but narrower than often claimed).** Livestock caused 5.8% of national emissions in 2016, against far more from rice [@MAC-33] {VN-direct|Medium}. In our judgement, the environmental case rests more on the land behind imported feed, manure pollution and antibiotic use. About 72% of antimicrobials used in Vietnam in 2015 went to animals [@MAC-36] {VN-direct|Medium}.
4. **Animal welfare (a motivation for some actors, including the publisher of this report).** Tens of millions of pigs and hundreds of millions of poultry are on farms at any time. This report does not model welfare effects ([[ch26-plays]]).

Consumer demand is not an argument this study makes. Part III sizes what people and firms would buy, as an input to design. It does not tell people what to eat.

## 1.7 What this means for each reader

- **Investors:** the buyers are businesses: food makers, *chay* (vegetarian food) makers and canteens ([[ch16-business-buyers]]). Imports set the price: textured protein, isolates and gluten from China and Southeast Asia enter duty-free [@FTR-32] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Size each business on named buyers, not on total protein tonnage.
- **Policy makers:** the food-security resolution to 2030 sets production volumes and has no import or protein indicator [@SEC-01] {VN-direct|High}. A protein-security indicator would make the dependence visible: the share of protein eaten that is made from domestic inputs ([[ch28-robust-moves]]).
- **Startups:** your first customers are food makers, *chay* makers and canteens, not shoppers. The import price is your ceiling.
- **Manufacturers:** input-cost swings in 2022 and 2026 are the business reason to qualify a second source of protein.
- **Researchers:** the first missing public good is a national protein balance by food, source and origin. Section 1.1 is a first version from FAO data. We found none published by a Vietnamese agency {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **International bodies:** the same import chains supply Vietnam's neighbours, so the exposure is regional.
