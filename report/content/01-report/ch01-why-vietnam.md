# 1. Why Vietnam: the protein people eat, and the imports behind it

**In brief.**
- Vietnamese people eat more meat each year. Meat intake was 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, against a recommendation of 50 to 80 g, and animal foods supply 42% of protein.
- About one fifth of the protein supply (19% to 23% in 2023) is imported food: soybeans for tofu and soy milk, wheat, milk, beef and offal. Meat imports are growing (978,000 t in 2025), because costs abroad are lower, not because of subsidies. Pork, eggs, fish and rice are produced at home, and about 90% of meat is domestic.
- Those farms run on imported feed. About 99% of the soy protein in feed and about 74% of the maize supply were imported in 2025, for a feed-ingredient bill of about USD 10 billion. Counting that feed, about two thirds of the animal protein people eat (61% to 71%) rests on imports (our calculation).
- Feed-price shocks land mainly on farms. Disease is the shock consumers feel: pork prices rose 57% in 2020 after African swine fever. In 2025, 79% of the virus samples typed in the north were strains the licensed vaccines do not protect against.
- Vietnam exports more than USD 11 billion of seafood a year and a great deal of cassava starch. The step that turns starch into fermentation sugar mostly happens in China.
- The case for making protein for people at home rests first on security, and it is real but narrow. Only protein made from domestic inputs reduces import dependence. At the scale demand supports, it adds a second source; it does not cut national exposure or disease risk. Industrial value and trade follow; environment and animal welfare motivate some actors.

{{kn:kn-meat-intake-2020}} {{kn:kn-soy-protein-import}} {{kn:kn-feed-import-bill}}

> **Method note.** This study is about protein for people: what Vietnam could make at home for people to eat, from what, at what cost, under which rules, and who would buy it, now and to 2050. Feed appears only as context. It is the hidden import behind the meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish that people eat, and so part of the food-security picture. The study does not treat feed ingredients (fishmeal replacements, or microbial, insect or duckweed feed) as something to make. The earlier assessment of them stays in [[app-s6-feed-market]], [[app-f6-aquafeed-feedstock-futures]] and [[app-m5-changelog]].

## 1.1 What Vietnamese people eat, and where it comes from

**People eat a lot of meat, and more each year.** The General Nutrition Survey 2019 to 2020 (22,400 households in 25 provinces) measured meat intake of 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, up from 84 g in 2010. Urban residents ate 155.3 g. The national recommendation is 50 to 80 g [@DIE-01; @DIE-02; @DIE-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Animal foods supply a growing share of protein.** Food balance sheets from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) put meat supply at 60.5 kg per person in 2023 (carcass weight), up from 41.0 kg in 2010. Total protein supply was 102.2 g per person per day, of which 43.2 g (42%) came from animal foods. The animal share was 21% in 1990 and 28% in 2000 [@DIE-07; @DIE-08] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Rice still supplies 27.0 g of protein a day and soy foods only 4.9 g [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Soy foods are still everyday food: in a 2021 national survey, 40.0% of adults had eaten dried beans, soybeans, tofu, soy milk or bean flour on the previous day [@APR-36; @BRD-01; @BRD-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Chapter 11 sets out the full picture ([[ch11-protein-diet]]).

{{kn:kn-animal-protein-share}}

**Pork intake has risen fast.** Pork consumption was reported at about 39 kg per person in 2025, up from about 30 kg in 2021 [@MAC-21] {VN-direct|Low}. Our own check from production and population gives a similar figure (about 39.5 kg carcass weight per person at a 75% carcass yield; our calculation from [@MAC-14; @MAC-19]).

**Vietnam's own farms and ponds produce meat, eggs, milk and fish at scale, and output is still growing.**

- **Meat.** In 2025 Vietnam produced 5.39 Mt (million tonnes) of pork and 2.60 Mt of poultry meat (live weight), plus 0.50 Mt of beef and 0.13 Mt of buffalo meat. Pork grew 3.9% and poultry 5.9% on 2024 [@MAC-14; @MAC-12] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Eggs and milk.** Farms produced 21.4 billion eggs (+5.3%) and 1.3 Mt of fresh milk (+5.5%) in 2025 [@MAC-14] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Fish and shrimp.** Fisheries output reached 9.95 Mt in 2025, of which aquaculture was 6.12 Mt. Farmed pangasius was 1.94 Mt, whiteleg shrimp 0.99 Mt and black tiger shrimp 0.30 Mt [@MAC-15] {VN-direct|High}. Much of the shrimp and pangasius is exported (section 1.5).
- **Animals on farms.** At the end of 2025 there were about 31.4 million pigs and 584.9 million poultry in Vietnam [@MAC-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. About 53.5 million pigs were slaughtered in 2024, according to one press report [@MAC-22] {VN-direct|Low}.
- **Still growing in 2026.** In the first half of 2026 pork output rose 4.8% and poultry 5.6% year on year [@MAC-18] {VN-direct|Medium}.

{{chart:chart-animal-protein-output}}

**About one fifth of the protein people eat is imported food.** FAO food balance sheets show the protein supply by food and how much of each food is imported. In 2023 about 19% to 23% of the protein supply was imported food, up from 11% to 13% in 2010. The low figure counts net imports and the high figure gross imports (our calculation) [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|Medium}. The OECD-FAO Agricultural Outlook, a joint projection by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) and FAO, gives a lower estimate of maize eaten as food. On that estimate the 2023 share would be 18% to 22% ([[app-r2-disagreements]], DG-363).

- **The foods people eat directly that are mostly imported** are soybeans for tofu and soy milk, wheat and milk. Beef, offal, groundnuts and dry beans were about 40% to 47% imported, up from near zero in 2010 or 2015 [@DIE-07] {VN-direct|High}. **Meat and dairy imports are a second, smaller exposure, and they are growing.** Vietnam imported 978,000 t of meat and meat products worth USD 2.0 billion in 2025, 12.2% more by value than in 2024 [@MIM-04; @MIM-05] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Most of it was poultry by-products, Indian frozen buffalo meat, frozen pork and offal [@MIM-05; @MIM-06] {VN-direct|Medium}. Dairy imports were worth USD 1.44 billion, up 27.3% [@MIM-12] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**The import share differs by food (2025).** Pork was 3.7% to 4.5% imported and poultry 11% to 17%, the higher figure counting feet and other by-products [@MIM-03; @MIM-05; @MIM-06] {VN-direct|Medium}. **Counting feed, about two thirds of the animal protein people eat rests on imports.** Domestic pork, poultry, eggs and farmed fish are raised mostly on compound feed, and most of its ingredients are imported (section 1.2). We added the protein these animals owe to imported feed to the animal foods imported directly. On that basis, 61% to 71% of the 43.2 g of animal protein per person per day rested on imports in 2023. For meat alone the share is 69% to 77%, and for all protein 40% to 46% (all our calculation) [@DIE-07; @MAC-01; @FS-23] {VN-direct|Low}. This is an attribution, not a measured flow. It treats beef, buffalo and milk as feed-free, so it is a lower bound. Inputs: `animal_protein_import_reliance.csv`; the path to 2050 is in [[ch22-protein-balance-2050]].

## 1.2 The imported feed behind it

**The farms run on compound feed, and most of the protein and grain in that feed is imported.** This is context for the study, not a target.

**Feed output.** Vietnamese feed mills made about 22 Mt of livestock and poultry feed in 2025, after 21.5 Mt in 2024 [@MAC-01; @MAC-28] {VN-direct|Medium}. This official figure excludes aquafeed (feed for farmed fish and shrimp). Estimates of aquafeed volume disagree, and we do not average them ([[app-s6-feed-market]], [[app-r2-disagreements]]).

**Imports of the two ingredients that set feed cost.** The table gives 2025 imports. DDGS are distillers' dried grains with solubles, a by-product of making ethanol from maize.

**Domestic crops cover very little.** Vietnam grew only 42,000 to 43,000 t of soybeans in 2025, on about 26,000 ha. Domestic maize output was flat to falling, at about 4.1 Mt [@MAC-04; @MAC-01] {VN-direct|High}. Even if every domestic soybean were crushed, it would supply about 2% of the crush (our calculation from [@MAC-04]). Domestic crushing capacity is growing. One Bunge and Wilmar plant at Phu My in Ho Chi Minh City (former Ba Ria-Vung Tau) leads it, with 2.6 Mt a year of capacity [@VCO-16] {VN-direct|Medium}. But crushing imported beans at home shifts imports from meal to beans. It does not reduce the dependence on imported protein.

**The import bill.** Adding four customs lines (maize, soybeans, wheat and the "animal feed and ingredients" group) gives about USD 10.2 billion of imports in 2025 (our calculation from [@MAC-07; @MAC-08; @MAC-09; @MAC-10]). This includes some milling wheat and food soybeans. The livestock department's narrower 2024 figure for feed ingredients is 22.4 Mt and USD 7.7 billion [@MAC-28] {VN-direct|Medium}. USDA's 2025 feed-ingredient table shows 23.57 Mt imported out of 28.62 Mt used, about 82% [@FS-23] {VN-direct|High}. Weighted by protein content, the imported share is about 93% (90% to 94% across the protein contents tested; our calculation) [@FS-23; @MAC-01; @DIE-12; @FBS-04; @FBS-05] {VN-direct|Medium}.

## 1.3 Exposure: feed prices hit farms, disease hits consumers

Imported feed passes price shocks abroad straight into Vietnamese farm costs. Disease adds shocks at home, and these are the shocks consumers have felt most.

- **Feed is most of the cost.** Industry sources put feed at 60% to 70% of livestock production cost, and above 70% for pigs [@MAC-24; @MAC-43] {VN-direct|Low}. For shrimp, 2026 trade press puts feed at 50% to 60% of production cost [@FM-10] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **2022.** During the price spike after the war in Ukraine began, wheat import prices reached USD 424/t in September 2022, 37% above a year earlier [@MAC-44] {VN-direct|Medium}. A review estimates that price pressure forced about 30% of small farmers to stop farming in recent years [@MAC-43] {VN-direct|Low}. Consumers barely felt it: in 2022 food prices rose 1.62% and pork prices fell 10.68% [@SEC-09] {VN-direct|High}.
- **2026.** Feed prices rose 10% to 14% between January and April 2026 (poultry association estimate), which the association attributed to the Middle East conflict. Farm-gate egg prices fell below production cost [@MAC-24] {VN-direct|Medium}. Shrimp feed makers raised prices by VND 1,200 to 1,500/kg in June 2026 [@FM-10] {VN-direct|Medium}. Consumer prices for food and food services rose 4.76% in January to August 2026 on a year earlier. We found no breakdown that separates feed, energy and the 2025 swine fever wave [@SEC-17] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Disease.** African swine fever killed or forced the culling of about 6 million pigs in 2019, more than 20% of the herd. It returned in 2025 and reached most provinces by the third quarter; about 1.2 million pigs were culled that year [@MAC-37; @MAC-01; @MAC-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. The livestock department's count for 2025 is 1.3 million; Appendix R2 gives both (DG-005).

**Disease is the protein shock consumers feel.** Consumer pork prices rose 57.23% in 2020, after the 2019 swine fever epidemic. That was 1.94 points of a 3.23% rise in the consumer price index [@SEC-08] {VN-direct|High}. Prices can also fall. Live pig prices collapsed to VND 24,000 to 35,000 per kg in May 2019, when people avoided pork, then reached a record above VND 90,000 per kg in late December [@PAN-04; @PAN-03] {VN-direct|Medium}. In November 2025, culling sales pushed live pig prices down to about VND 50,000 per kg [@MAC-01] {VN-direct|High}. So imported feed is mainly an exposure for farmers, processors and the trade balance. For consumers, the price exposure in protein food is mainly disease (our reading) [@SEC-08; @SEC-09; @MAC-43] {VN-direct|Medium}.

{{kn:kn-pork-price-2020}}

**The 2019 shock was large for pork and small for protein.** Pig output fell 13.8% in 2019, while poultry output rose 16.5% [@PAN-03] {VN-direct|High}. The government ordered about 100,000 t of pork imports for the first quarter of 2020 [@PAN-03] {VN-direct|High}. People switched to chicken, not tofu: home tofu use did not change between 2018 and 2020 ([[ch11-protein-diet]]) [@DIE-10; @DIE-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Disease is a pork-price and food-safety exposure, not a national protein shortfall (our reading) {VN-direct|Medium}.

**Swine fever is now a lasting exposure.** In September 2025 the livestock and animal health department of the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE) reported its virus typing for 2025. In the north, 158 of 200 samples (79%) were recombinant strains, which mix two virus genotypes. The three licensed vaccines "have no protective effect" against them [@PAN-33] {VN-direct|High}. The department now calls farm biosecurity the foundation of control. On this evidence, major waves stay at least as likely to 2030 as before, because the licensed vaccines do not cover the main northern strain (our reading) [@PAN-33] {VN-direct|Medium} {fx:trend}.

**The steady health cost is foodborne; bird flu in people is rare.** The Ministry of Health counted 123 human infections and 2 deaths from *Streptococcus suis* in 2025. People catch this pig bacterium mainly through raw pig blood and slaughter [@PAN-15] {VN-direct|Medium}. Provincial press reports suggest more deaths (DG-354). Vietnam has recorded 130 human cases of H5N1 bird flu since 2003, and none in 2026 to 6 September [@PAN-01] {VN-direct|High}.

## 1.4 Growth ahead: more meat per person, and more imported feed

- **People will eat more meat per person.** The OECD-FAO outlook (section 1.1) projects meat food use per person rising from 49.8 kg in 2025 to 62.2 kg in 2035 (retail weight), a rise of 25%. Poultry leads, from 17.0 to 25.8 kg; pork rises only from 28.6 to 31.5 kg [@DIE-11] {VN-direct|Medium} {fx:projection}.
- **Population adds little.** Vietnam's population is projected at 104.3 million in 2030 and 106.5 million in 2035 (UN medium variant) [@GT-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. Population will rise only about 5% between 2025 and 2035, so protein demand growth will come mostly from income and diet.
- **Official targets point the same way.** The national livestock strategy targets 30 to 32 Mt of industrial feed output and 6.0 to 6.5 Mt of carcass meat by 2030 [@MAC-23] {VN-direct|High}. If the feed target covers livestock and poultry feed only, it implies growth of about 6% to 8% a year from 2025 (our calculation; see [[app-f1-outlook]] for the scope question).
- **Feed demand follows.** USDA expects total feed demand (livestock, poultry and aquafeed) to rise from 28.6 Mt in 2025 to 31.2 Mt in 2027. This August 2026 forecast is up from 30.6 Mt in April. USDA also raised its forecast of maize imports for 2026/27 to 15 Mt [@MAC-01; @MAC-03] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **No target for local feed inputs.** We found no numerical target for localising feed raw materials in the published strategies. The livestock strategy encourages domestic feed from by-products such as brewer's grain and cassava pulp, and biological products that replace antibiotics, but sets no localisation number [@MAC-23; @REG-54] {VN-direct|High}.

On current trends, every extra tonne of feed demand will be met by imports, so more meat means more imported feed. Chapter 22 follows the protein people eat, and the feed behind it, to 2050 ([[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]).

## 1.5 Trade: an exporter of animal protein and an importer of the plant protein behind it

Vietnam exported USD 11.3 billion of seafood in 2025 (+12.4%), including about USD 4.6 billion of shrimp and USD 2.2 billion of pangasius [@MAC-15; @MAC-01] {VN-direct|High}. In January to August 2026 seafood exports reached USD 8.0 billion, and China and Hong Kong are now the largest market [@MAC-34] {VN-direct|Medium}. Vietnam also exported about USD 1.4 to 1.5 billion of animal feed in 2025 [@MAC-01; @MAC-12] {VN-direct|Medium}.

The shape of the economy is clear: **Vietnam imports plant protein and energy, turns them into animal and aquatic protein, and exports a large share of the result.** Its strength is processing. Its weak point is the input.

The same pattern appears on the carbohydrate side. Vietnam supplied about 39% of China's cassava starch imports by value in 2024 (1.56 Mt, USD 757 million, China-reported), but only about USD 0.16 million of glucose [@RGN-51; @RGN-55] {VN-direct|High}. In 2025 Vietnamese starch exports to China rose to about 2.28 Mt in 11 months, and Vietnam became China's largest cassava starch supplier [@FS-01; @FS-13] {VN-direct|High}. The step that turns starch into fermentable sugar, and sugar into higher-value products, mostly happens after the starch leaves Vietnam. [[ch04-asset-map]] and [[ch03-regional]] return to this.

## 1.6 What the case for alternative protein rests on, and what it does not

Four arguments carry different weight. All are about the protein people eat.

1. **Security of the protein people eat (strong, with limits).** About two thirds of the animal protein people eat (61% to 71% in 2023) rests on imports, directly or through feed (section 1.1). About 99% of the soy protein in feed and about 74% of the maize supply were imported in 2025 (section 1.2). Feed-price shocks in 2022 and 2026 landed mainly on farms, processors and the trade balance; the shock consumers felt came from African swine fever (section 1.3). Protein made in Vietnam for people, from domestic inputs where possible, adds a second source. This is the argument with the most evidence behind it, but the remedy is narrow.

   **Only protein made from domestic inputs reduces the dependence.** Fermentation on cassava or sugar, rice and peanut protein, and soy foods made from Vietnamese beans use domestic inputs. Their fermenters and, for precision fermentation, their strains are mostly imported [@SEC-05; @SEC-14; @FS-24; @FS-01] {VN-direct|Medium}. Alternative protein made from imported soy, pea or wheat gluten moves the dependence instead of reducing it, often from the Americas to China. China supplied about 84% of the tonnage of food plant-protein ingredients (soy isolate, textured protein and gluten) that reached Vietnam in 2025 (our calculation) [@BUY-02] {VN-direct|Medium}. The plant protein people already eat, tofu and soy milk, rests on imported soybeans (section 1.1).

   {{kn:kn-china-share-plant-protein-ingredients}}

   Protein for people is also the route that saves the most imports per tonne. In the balance model to 2050, replacing 1 t of meat protein in diets avoids about 4.5 t of soybean-meal and 9.5 t of maize imports (our calculation; [[ch22-protein-balance-2050]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}.

   The limit is scale. On the benchmark path of the demand model, new protein displaces only about 0.2% of meat protein by 2035 (our calculation; [[ch18-demand-sizing]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}. If made from domestic inputs, by 2035 it would save at most about 0.24% of the soybean-meal protein fed in 2025; made from imported soy, less (our calculation from [@MAC-04]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. It would spare about 142,000 pigs a year in 2035, while swine fever culls in 2025 were 8.4 times that number (our calculation from [@MAC-01; @PAN-03] and [[app-d8-demand-model]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}. On its own, it does not change national exposure.

   {{kn:kn-asf-culls-vs-spared}}

   **The evidence does not show that it reduces disease risk.** We found no study that measures a fall in zoonotic (animal-to-human), pandemic or antimicrobial-resistance risk from replacing meat with alternative protein. Claims that it does come from reviews and opinion pieces, several by advocacy or company authors; Appendix R2 records both positions (DG-356) [@PAN-27; @PAN-30; @PAN-31] {general|Medium}. Published research ties these risks to how animals are farmed and traded: the wildlife trade, farm biosecurity and antibiotic use [@PAN-28; @PAN-29] {general|Medium}. Vietnamese studies point the same way, to the wildlife trade, live-bird markets and farm practice, and MAE now calls biosecurity the foundation of swine fever control [@PAN-25; @PAN-26; @PAN-21; @PAN-33] {VN-direct|Medium}. The security value of protein for people is a second source that does not fail when animals fall sick.

   **Subsidised imported meat is not the problem.** We tested whether Vietnam relies on subsidised imported meat, and the evidence does not support it. The OECD finds no price support in 2022 to 2024 for US meat and milk, Brazilian poultry and beef, and Australian and New Zealand meat and milk [@MIM-15] {general|High}. Brazilian pigmeat, about a third of Vietnam's pork imports, had none in 2022 and 2023 and some in 2024 (a coefficient of 1.66) [@MIM-15; @MIM-05] {general|High}. India taxes rather than supports its buffalo meat. Where support does exist, in EU poultry and Russian pork, it works mainly through border protection at home, not through payments on exports [@MIM-15; @MIM-16] {general|High}. World Trade Organization members agreed in 2015 to end agricultural export subsidies [@MIM-17] {general|High}.

   **Imported meat is cheaper because costs differ.** Vietnamese pig farm prices in 2024 were 1.6 to 2.8 times those in Brazil, Russia, the United States and the EU (our calculation) [@MIM-15] {VN-direct|Medium}. No trade-defence case on meat or dairy has been filed [@MIM-25] {VN-direct|Medium}. On the benchmark path, new protein would displace at most about 1.6% of imported meat protein by 2035; the only real overlap is frozen pork and deboned meat bought by processors (our calculation) [@MIM-04; @QNT-01] {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}.

2. **Industrial value added (strong, but conditional).** Vietnam already processes cassava, rice, sugar, soy and seafood at scale, and exports much of its carbohydrate as starch. Two steps could stay at home: domestic textured plant protein to replace imports from China ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]), and turning exported cassava starch into fermentation sugar for food fermentation (section 1.5). This is an industrial-policy argument that fits Resolution 36 on biotechnology and the 2026 high-technology priority list ([[ch07-rules]]). It depends on costs ([[ch09-economics]]) and on a legal route to market.
3. **Environment (real, but narrower than often claimed).** Vietnam's Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) is its national climate plan. Under its business-as-usual projection, agriculture's 2020 emissions were 104.5 MtCO2e (million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent), 19.8% of the projected national total (our calculation) [@MAC-32] {VN-direct|High}. But in the 2016 inventory, livestock (enteric fermentation plus manure) was 18.5 MtCO2e, 5.8% of the national total, against 49.7 MtCO2e from rice cultivation [@MAC-33] {VN-direct|Medium}. In our judgement, the environmental case for alternative protein in Vietnam rests more on the land footprint of imported feed, manure pollution and antimicrobial use than on national methane totals. About 71.7% of antimicrobials used in Vietnam in 2015 went to animals [@MAC-36] {VN-direct|Medium}.
4. **Animal welfare (a motivation for some actors, including the publisher of this report).** The scale is large: tens of millions of pigs and hundreds of millions of poultry on farms at any time, and billions of farmed fish and shrimp. Proteins that displace animal products in food reduce these numbers. Proteins that make aquaculture cheaper could also expand it. This report does not model welfare effects; the sign depends on the use (see [[ch26-plays]]).

Consumer demand is not an argument this study makes. Part III sizes what people and firms would buy, as an input to design; it does not tell people what to eat. We note only that the traditional *đồ chay* (vegetarian food) category already exists in every supermarket we visited, and that the first thing it lacks is protein, not shelf space ([[ch02-alt-protein-today]]).

## 1.7 What this means for each reader

- **Investors:** the buyers are businesses: food manufacturers, *chay* makers and canteens ([[ch16-business-buyers]]). Demand is concentrated, and imports set the price: textured protein, isolates and gluten from China, Korea and the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) enter Vietnam duty-free [@FTR-32] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Size theses on specific uses and named buyers, not on total protein tonnage.
- **Policy makers:** the dependence of the protein people eat on imports is structural and not measured in policy targets. The national food-security resolution to 2030 (Resolution 34/NQ-CP, 2021) sets production volumes and has no import, feed or protein indicator [@SEC-01] {VN-direct|High}. MAE's 2026 plan asks to "step by step reduce import dependence by developing domestic raw-material areas", the closest official hook [@SEC-02] {VN-direct|High}. A protein-security indicator, the share of the protein people eat that is made from domestic inputs, would make the dependence visible ([[ch28-robust-moves]]). Define it by where the inputs come from, not where the processing happens.
- **Startups:** your first customers are food manufacturers, *chay* makers and canteens, not shoppers. The import price is your ceiling.
- **Manufacturers:** input-cost swings (2022, 2026) are the business reason to qualify a second source of protein, even at the same price.
- **Researchers:** the missing basic data are cheap public goods. The first is a national protein balance of what people eat, by food, source and origin, with the imported feed behind domestic meat as a memo line. Section 1.1 is a first version built from FAO data. We found no food balance published by a Vietnamese agency (open question OQ-462 in [[app-r1-open-questions]]) {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **International bodies:** the exposure of Vietnam's protein supply to imported feed is a regional issue; the same import chains supply its neighbours.
