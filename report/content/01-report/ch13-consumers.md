# 13. Consumers and new proteins: what people say and what they buy

**In one paragraph.** Asked in an online survey, urban Vietnamese are aware of plant-based meat (90%), have often tried it (42%) and are the most willing in Southeast Asia to choose it at the same price as meat (74%). Yet only 15% intend to eat less meat, the lowest of six countries, and only 3% eat plant-based meat weekly, much of which is probably traditional mock meat. What people do tells a starker story. Imported analogues entered through bars, cafés and gourmet shops from 2019 and had left the shelves we audited by 2026; they cost 12 to 18 times chicken or eggs per gram of protein. The one modern plant category that sells at scale is plant milk, priced at or below dairy and made by local incumbents: online, the best-selling plant milk sells 60 to 79% of the litres of the best-selling dairy milk. Protein sells in that aisle only as a premium niche, and a protein claim on soy milk did not lift its sales. From 2027 a sugary-drink excise may fall on sweetened plant milks but not on dairy, so new plant milks should stay at or below 5 g of sugar per 100 ml. Twenty grams of protein from a chay wrapper costs more than a whole street meal. Abroad, plant-based meat peaked in 2020 to 2021, trial did not turn into habit, price parity on its own did not create growth and meat purchases did not fall; in the region, firms that launched branded analogues mostly went quiet or moved to private label. Vietnamese respondents ask for more variety, food they know how to cook, fewer additives and safer food, not a replacement for meat. That is a brief for ingredients inside familiar foods, with a numeric protein claim (10 g per 100 g for "high protein") where the food earns it.

## 13.1 The evidence is thin, and almost all of it is stated

We built a register of every consumer study with Vietnamese data on plant-based foods, meat reduction or new proteins (26 studies, [[app-d2-consumer-evidence]]). Only one large survey gives Vietnam numbers on plant-based meat: GFI APAC's "Decoding demand", run by The Good Growth Co. in 2023 on an online panel of 5,971 people in six countries, 1,049 of them in the Hanoi region and Ho Chi Minh City [@CON-01; @CON-02; @CON-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. The rest are two commercial online panels (Rakuten Insight 2021 and 2025), one academic urban survey (Delley et al., snowball sample, n = 616) and about ten small student or convenience samples [@CON-06; @CON-07; @CON-08] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. No Vietnamese study of new proteins reaches our top quality grade (a probability sample with a full method). The one national probability survey that touches the subject, the Diet Quality Questionnaire (Gallup, 2021), asks only whether people ate soy foods or meat the day before: 40% of adults had eaten beans, tofu or soy milk ([[ch11-protein-diet]], [[ch12-chay-baseline]]) [@APR-36; @BRD-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. We found no choice experiment, auction or in-store price test for plant-based meat or milk in Vietnam, and no household purchase panel data for these categories. Marketplace sales counters, which we read for this report, are the nearest thing to purchase data (section 13.5). Every consumer number below should be read with that in mind.

Three cautions apply. First, modern analogues were almost absent from Vietnamese shops, so many respondents probably read "plant-based meat" as traditional mock meat; the Vietnamese questionnaire wording is not public (our inference) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Second, Vietnamese respondents report 43.3 meat-eating occasions a month, second only to Thailand, and an urban survey found only 4% flexitarians among meat eaters [@CON-02; @CON-08] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Demand is for more kinds of protein, not for less meat. Third, the much-quoted "5% to 63%" is a regional, hypothetical figure: 5% of all six-country respondents eat plant-based meat weekly, and 63% say they would eat it "always" or "often" if all their concerns were resolved. No Vietnam split is public [@CON-02; @ECO-24] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:stated}. It is not a forecast.

Rakuten's broader category ("plant-based alternatives", mostly plant milk) shows Vietnam with the highest trial rate of any Asian market (86% in 2021, 92% in 2025), but the share of Vietnamese triers who have tried meat alternatives stayed at 47 to 49% [@CON-06; @CON-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.

## 13.3 What would make people eat more, and why they would

Asked what would make them eat more plant-based meat, Vietnamese respondents chose affordability less often than any other country (38% against an average of 47%) and chose availability (31%), variety (29%), fewer additives (27%, highest of six) and knowing how to cook it (22%, highest of six) more often. They were the least likely to want a product that "tasted more like meat" (23%) [@CON-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Rakuten's 2025 panel agrees: "difficult to find where I live" is the top Vietnamese barrier (33%) and cost is cited by only 21%, the lowest of the six Southeast Asian markets in the survey [@CON-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.

Health is the first reason people give in every source (55% of Rakuten's Vietnamese panel say plant-based products are healthier) [@CON-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. A soy-milk maker already uses that motive: a sponsored soy-nutrition article cites the new Law on Disease Prevention and the Ministry of Health's Ten tips on nutrition [@APR-43] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Food safety is the motive specific to Vietnam: urban Vietnamese rate "a very high level of food safety" at 5.35 on a 6-point scale, against 4.59 in Switzerland, and worry about hormones and drug residues in meat [@CON-08; @CON-10] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Environmental concern is weak: it ranks sixth of seven Rakuten reasons and had a small negative effect in one Hanoi student sample [@CON-07; @CON-13] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}. Family influence is strong, and meat is "non-negotiable" at special meals [@CON-08; @CON-10] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.

Lower stated price sensitivity should not be over-read. It sits next to a revealed price gap of an order of magnitude (section 13.4), and in a Hanoi study price had the largest effect of any factor on attitudes to new proteins [@NOV-02] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}.

## 13.4 Price: what people say, pay and face

**Stated.** GFI asked respondents to choose between plant-based meat and meat at a grocery shop as the relative price moved. At parity, 74% of Vietnamese respondents chose plant-based meat; at 20% dearer, 64%; at 50% dearer, 47%; at double, 26% [@CON-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. GFI itself says this shows relative interest, not willingness to pay. For design, it says: reach roughly price parity with the meat replaced, because stated interest falls by over a third at +50%. {{kn:kn-parity-choice}}

{{chart:chart-price-parity-choice}}

**Revealed.** The imported analogue on Vietnamese shelves in September 2026 cost VND 542,000 to 622,000 per 100 g of protein, 12 to 18 times eggs and chicken ([[ch11-protein-diet]]) [@FORM-01; @COST-40; @COST-41] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The clearest evidence that urban Vietnamese pay a premium for a plant product is café oat milk: in 2023 OatSide oat milk was used in nearly 100 cafés and outlets, and drinks made with nut milks such as OatSide were listed VND 10,000 to 15,000 dearer per cup than cow-milk drinks [@CON-35] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Tested.** None in Vietnam for plant-based meat, blends or plant milk. The nearest tested Vietnamese evidence is about safety and GMO labels: a real-money auction put the premium for safer pork at 20% [@DIE-30] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested}, and another found an 84% premium for soymilk labelled non-GM [@NOV-08] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested} ([[ch14-frontier-demand]]).

**What 20 g of protein costs.** Re-reading the retail audit for demand ([[app-d3-retail-demand-signals]]), the median cost of 20 g of protein, a meal's protein, is about VND 13,000 from tofu or dried textured soy, VND 56,000 from a frozen meat analogue, VND 74,000 from a filled chay wrapper and VND 91,000 from plant milk [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. From eggs or chicken it is VND 7,000 to 9,000 and from lean pork about VND 14,000 ([[ch11-protein-diet]]). A street rice plate with meat costs VND 30,000 to 35,000 in Ho Chi Minh City [@CHY-40] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. So the protein in most packaged alternatives costs more than a whole meal. The exception is bulk: dry soy-and-gluten pieces bought online in 1 kg bags give 20 g of protein for about VND 1,800 to 6,000, the cheapest protein in our retail data (on a borrowed label protein range; our calculation) [@MKT-03; @FORM-01] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. {{kn:kn-protein-cost-serving}}

## 13.5 What people actually buy

**Plant-based meat has not found a footing.** Mintel logged no plant-based meat launches in Vietnam from 2022 to the second quarter of 2023 [@ECO-23] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Beyond Meat (2019, a craft-beer bar), OmniFoods (gourmet retail 2022; Starbucks Vietnam's summer 2023 menu), Next Meats and Heura came through restaurants and gourmet shops [@ECO-15; @ECO-16; @ECO-20] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. None was on the shelves of the 11 stores we audited in September 2026, including the two gourmet chains that stocked them in 2022; the only extruded modern analogue was one Italian brand in one gourmet store [@FORM-01; @ECO-15] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. CP's Meat Zero chay range is listed on Kamereo, a platform supplying restaurants and cafés, but it was not orderable in September 2026 and its eight listings show 386 units (80 kg) sold in all; no Meat Zero, Beyond or Omni listing appeared on Lazada or Tiki [@AIS-25; @ECR-06; @MKT-03; @MKT-05] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. We found no trace of a local startup (VMEAT) after 2022, and no Vietnam launch by a Korean startup backed by a Vietnamese fund in 2024 [@ECO-19; @BRD-18; @ECO-65] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Meanwhile incumbents extend their chay lines: Vissan launched two chay products for Vu Lan 2026 [@CHY-29] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Plant milk is the category that sells.** Vinasoy's soy-milk revenue was about VND 4,800 billion in 2025 (our sum of nine-month and fourth-quarter segment revenue), implying a branded soy-milk market of about VND 5,300 billion (USD 204 million) (our derivation from Vinasoy's 90.6% share of branded soy milk in 2024, whose basis, value or volume, is not stated; the supply chapters' figure of about 80% refers to all soy milk on an older base) [@CON-26; @CON-27; @CON-28] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Vinasoy sold 264 million litres in 2025 (up 4%); its revenue rose 14%, with Veyo nut milks up 50%, premium Fami up 36% and e-commerce sales up 135% [@AIB-05; @AIB-06] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Plant milks were 51 of the 186 products in the retail audit, across 14 brands [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Vinamilk reported double-digit growth in nut milk in early 2026 [@ECO-44] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Vietnam imported USD 3.9 billion of tree nuts in 2025 (up 40%), which USDA attributes partly to plant-milk demand among younger consumers [@APR-34; @AIB-40] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Household purchases online now show the scale. On Lazada and Tiki, read without logging in on 25 September 2026, the best-selling plant milk sells 60% and 79% of the litres of the best-selling dairy milk; domestic nut and soy milks lead, and imported oat is under 2% of the plant-milk litres read (cumulative counters on 96 listings; our calculation) [@MKT-03; @MKT-05] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. Kitchens buy differently: on Kamereo, plant milks are about 4% of milk litres and 1.5 to 2.1% of milk protein, and the plant best seller is imported Oatside oat milk, marketed for coffee (our inference: bought for coffee, not protein) [@ECR-06] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed} (`kitchen_platform_counters.csv`). Inside Vietnam's largest dairy company plant milk is still small: plant-based products were 1.30% of Vinamilk's range in FAIRR's 2024 index, with no time-bound target [@AFN-21] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}, and Vinamilk's 2025 annual report positions plant milk as a nutrition product that is "no longer seen as a substitute for animal milk" [@AIB-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:stated}.

**Search behaviour tells the same story.** From 2019 to 2026, Google search interest in *sữa hạt* (nut or seed milk) averaged about 17 to 19 times that in *thịt thực vật* (plant meat). *Sữa hạt* has roughly tripled since 2019; *thịt thực vật* peaked in 2021 to 2022, has fallen by about 60% since, and its top query is "what is plant meat?". *Thuần chay* (vegan) rose about tenfold from 2019 to 2024 [@CON-37] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-search-ratio}}

**A tax that may fall on plant milk but not on dairy.** From 1 January 2027 drinks above 5 g of total sugar per 100 ml that count as *nước giải khát* (soft drinks) under the standard TCVN 12828:2019 pay an 8% excise, rising to 10% from 2028, and since 1 January 2026 they have lost the 2% VAT cut; milk and dairy products are exempt [@FTR-01; @FTR-02; @FTR-05; @FTR-09] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Cereal milks are named in the standard's scope. Whether soy and nut milks are in scope is unresolved, and we found no official letter or ruling on it [@FTR-05; @FTR-13] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Core Fami soy milks carry 6.5 to 9.3 g of sugar per 100 ml, above the line [@FTR-17; @FTR-18] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. If the tax applies and is passed on in full, mass soy milk would cost about VND 25,200 per litre in 2028, still 24 to 44% below the 2023 cow-milk price (our calculation from a 2026 promotional soy-milk price) [@FORM-01; @CON-35] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. The finding that plant milk sells at or below dairy prices survives; the design rule for new plant milks is to stay at or below 5 g of total sugar per 100 ml. Policy otherwise leaves plant milk out: the 2026 dairy strategy (Decision 309/QĐ-TTg) does not mention it and says school milk programmes use "only fresh milk" [@FTR-31; @APR-11] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-sugary-drink-excise}}

**Plant milk did not succeed on protein, and protein now sells in it only as a niche.** Most plant milks carry 0.7 to 2.5 g of protein per 100 ml [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Vinamilk's high-protein nut milk (5 g per 100 ml, mainly pea protein), and searches for it, were the first sign that protein content itself sells in this aisle [@BUY-16; @CON-37] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Purchases now confirm it, as a premium niche. At Vinamilk's Lazada store it shows about 16,100 cases sold (about 70,300 L and 3.5 t of protein): about 11% of the Vinamilk nut-milk litres read but 29% of their protein. It costs 24% more per litre and about 37% as much per gram of protein (cumulative counters; our calculation) [@MKT-03; @MKT-04] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. A protein claim alone does not lift sales. Vinamilk's GoldSoy soy milk, sold with "up to 3.2 g protein per 100 ml", sells less than unclaimed soy milks on Lazada (3,100 cases against 14,800 for a walnut soy milk at 2.0 g, although GoldSoy costs 39% less per litre) and on the Kamereo kitchen platform (2,609 units against 5,758 and 5,828 for two dearer unsweetened soy milks) [@MKT-03; @MKT-05; @ECR-06] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. The same company promotes its high-protein dairy milk as "more easily absorbed than plant-origin protein" [@AIB-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.

## 13.6 Who buys first

The likely first buyers are urban 25 to 44 year olds who eat a lot of meat and want variety, and urban households anxious about health and food safety, not students. GFI's "Curious" segment (aware, not yet tried, leaning towards cutting red meat) is 43% of the Vietnamese sample, and about two-thirds of the region's most enthusiastic buyers are over 35 [@CON-02; @CON-03; @ECO-23] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Delley et al. place 65% of urban meat eaters in three segments (anxious, suggestible, proactive) that are open to change for health and safety reasons [@CON-08] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Students, the focus of most Vietnamese papers, have low incomes, and across the six markets 18 to 24 year olds are the least aware of plant-based meat [@CON-13; @CON-02] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}.

As a ceiling rather than an estimate: if the 3% weekly rate held for all 39.4 million urban residents, weekly eaters would number about 1.2 million (our calculation) [@MAC-19; @CON-02] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. The real number is smaller, because the panel is online adults in two cities, stated frequency overstates purchase and much of it is probably mock meat.

## 13.7 What happened abroad, and why it matters here

Vietnam can learn from markets that ran the modern plant-based experiment first. Most of this evidence is general or from comparable Asian markets ([[app-d7-global-benchmarks]]).

- **Peak and plateau.** US plant-based meat sales fell from USD 1.4 billion (2021) to USD 1.0 billion (2025) and units from 281 million to 173 million; the category's share of meat sales halved from 1.4% to about 0.7% [@GLB-01; @GLB-02] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. UK and Dutch volumes fell; Germany was flat [@GLB-08; @GLB-09; @GLB-10; @GLB-11] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-us-pbm-decline}}
- **The ceiling.** In the most favourable markets, modern plant-based meat reached 3.1% (Germany) and 4.1% (Netherlands) of pre-packaged meat volume in 2024. Frequent buyers (12 or more times a year) were 8.6% of UK and 9.7% of German households in 2024 [@GLB-09; @GLB-10; @GLB-11] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-west-ceiling}}
- **Trial without habit.** US household penetration fell from 19 to 20% to 11% while the repeat rate stayed above 60% [@GLB-01; @GLB-02]; in an earlier household panel (Neuhofer and Lusk, 2018 to 2020), 40% of buyers bought only once [@GLB-19] {general|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Price matters, parity is not enough.** Demand is price elastic (about -0.8 to -1.6 in US scanner data), yet Dutch retailers' price-parity pledges left the plant share of protein sold flat at about 42 to 43% from 2023 to 2025 while plant-based meat volume fell [@GLB-16; @GLB-03; @GLB-12; @GLB-08; @GLB-10] {general|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Little displacement.** Most US plant-based meat buyers keep buying meat, and household ground-meat purchases did not fall after a first plant-based purchase; plant milk, by contrast, displaces 0.43 to 0.93 units of dairy per unit bought [@GLB-19; @GLB-28; @GLB-22] {general|High} {dx:revealed}. An analysis of 1.2 million transactions in seven restaurants in the US, Canada and Australia (2026) found essentially no evidence that modern alternative proteins reduced meat purchases [@AFN-07] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Formats that hold up** are ingredient-like (strips, pieces, tofu); burgers lost share in the Netherlands [@GLB-09; @GLB-10; @GLB-11; @GLB-01] {general|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Asia with a vegetarian tradition did not become an analogue market.** Thai meat alternatives were USD 21 million against USD 803 million of dairy alternatives (2020); Taiwan, with about 3 million vegetarians, had USD 36 million of modern plant-based retail sales (2021); Japanese soy milk is near record output while soy meat contracted [@GLB-52; @GLB-57; @GLB-55; @GLB-56] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Beyond Meat suspended its China operations in 2025 [@AIS-45] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Where a modern analogue did sell well, it was hidden inside a familiar dish at a low price: a Hong Kong-made pork substitute (OmniPork) in dumplings at a Taiwanese chain [@GLB-59] {VN-adjacent|Low} {dx:revealed}.
- **Regional firms moved away from their own consumer brands.** Of 24 alternative-protein startups and scale-ups that entered, tested or left Vietnam and Southeast Asia from 2019 to 2026, 7 are active, 4 pivoted, 2 exited and 11 show no activity (our register, `firm_outcomes.csv`). Every pivot moved towards private label, co-manufacturing, a retail blend or non-food uses: TiNDLE to private label from 2026, GOOD Meat to a 3% cultivated blend, Vow to co-manufacturing. Restaurant-launched brands such as Karana, Float Foods and Phuture went quiet [@AIS-28; @AIS-37; @NOV-10; @NOV-12; @BRD-08; @BRD-09; @BRD-10] {VN-adjacent|Low} {dx:revealed}. TiNDLE's co-founder and former chief executive, Andre Menezes, told AgFunderNews in 2025 that there is "no real underlying consumer demand to shift away from meat" [@AIS-29] {general|Medium} {dx:stated}. In Vietnam we found no sign of activity after 2022 for any of four meat-analogue startups, while all three makers of traditional or whole-food formats (mushroom floss, cashew cheese, tempeh) are trading [@AIS-39; @AIS-42; @AIS-43; @ECO-15; @ECO-17; @ECO-19; @BRD-18; @BRD-19] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed} (`firm_outcomes.csv`; [[ch15-channels]]).

## 13.8 Words on the pack

*Chay* is the incumbent word: it appears in 87 of 186 audited product names, and it carries Buddhist and traditional associations and a cheap, low-protein reputation (our inference) [@FORM-01; @CON-37] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. *Thuần chay* (vegan) is the modern word; its search interest rose to a 2024 peak and has eased since. *Thịt thực vật* (plant meat) and *đạm thực vật* (plant protein) were media words of 2021 to 2022 and have faded [@CON-37] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Incumbents use *thực vật* (plant) for modern dairy alternatives and as a protein claim ("100% protein từ thực vật" on one frozen dumpling) [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**The protein claim is already defined.** A voluntary national standard taken from Codex, TCVN 14429:2025, sets "source of protein" at 5 g and "high protein" (*cao đạm*) at 10 g per 100 g of food, or 2.5 g and 5 g per 100 kcal [@LBL-03; @LBL-04] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. *Giàu đạm* (rich in protein), the phrase an earlier draft used, is not a defined term, so it is judged only against the rule that labels be truthful. The per 100 kcal route lets watery foods claim "high" at 4 to 5 g per 100 g, so a maker who wants the word to carry weight should print the number (for example "10 g đạm/100 g") on the front. The claim needs no registration and no advertising approval, but for function claims such as "builds muscle" we found no approval route for ordinary foods [@LBL-03; @LBL-05; @REG-02; @LBL-19] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Protein itself has had to appear on non-exempt pre-packaged labels since 1 January 2026 (Circular 29/2023, now Circular 30/2026); foods from small food businesses, which (our inference) cover most wet-market chay, are exempt [@LBL-01; @LBL-02] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-high-protein-claim}}

**The shelf already qualifies without claiming.** Of 43 audited plant-protein foods with a label value, 20 (47%) meet the "source" condition per 100 g and 7 (16%) meet "high", all seven of them frozen analogues; none carries a claim. Counting the per 100 kcal route, 27 of 38 and 13 of 38 would qualify (our calculation) [@FORM-01; @LBL-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The protein claim is an open position, not a regulatory problem.

**Fake chay does not undercut it today.** We found no confirmed case of meat in a Vietnamese chay product from 2015 to 2026; chay's live trust problems are borax, microbes and unlabelled goods ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]) [@TRU-18; @CHY-23] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. In our reading, a protein claim is credible when it rides on a registered maker, a full label and a short additive list; "verified meat-free", from a PCR screen once QUATEST 3 offers one, would be a secondary reassurance [@TRU-24; @TRU-04] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

No study has tested which words Vietnamese shoppers trust, and the one revealed test is discouraging: a protein claim on soy milk did not lift its sales (section 13.5). Our inference is that *chay* works for religious and periodic occasions, while a numeric protein claim with a food-safety signal is the more promising frame for meat eaters, provided the food is dense enough to make it (10 g per 100 g) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. The words around fermentation and cultivated meat are in [[ch14-frontier-demand]].

## 13.9 What this means for the frontier

- **Make products for the meals people already eat.** Vietnamese respondents asked for variety and cooking know-how more than any other country and for meat-like taste less than any other.
- **Price per serving against the meat replaced.** At parity or below for the mass market; stated interest falls by over a third at +50%.
- **Raise protein visibly** (10 g or more per 100 g for meat-like foods, the "high protein" level of TCVN 14429:2025; 5 g or more per 100 ml for drinks), print the number, keep ingredient lists short and signal food safety.
- **Plant milk is the proven category.** Barista milks for *cà phê sữa đá* (iced milk coffee), high-protein milks and local nut, seed and bean bases are the consumer openings with revealed demand. High-protein milks sell as a premium niche, not the volume line. A sugary-drink excise from 2027 may fall on sweetened plant milks but not on dairy, so design new plant milks at or below 5 g of total sugar per 100 ml.
- **Measure repeat, not launch.** Abroad, trial did not turn into habit; ignore launch spikes. Marketplace counters are cumulative, so read them twice, a month apart, to get a rate.

**For each reader.**
- **Startups:** do not launch premium imported-style analogues into gourmet channels expecting mass uptake; that route has been tried since 2019. In the region, brands placed on restaurant menus mostly went quiet or pivoted, while the Vietnamese survivors make familiar foods with little capital ([[ch15-channels]]).
- **Investors:** the revealed demand is in plant milk and in incumbents' chay lines; underwrite on repeat purchase and cost per gram of protein, not survey intent.
- **Manufacturers:** protein content, food safety and fewer additives are open positions on the shelf; about half of the audited plant-protein foods could already claim "source of protein". Check whether your sweetened plant milks are *nước giải khát* and plan recipes at or below 5 g of sugar per 100 ml.
- **Research bodies:** build tested-evidence capacity (sensory panels in Vietnamese dishes, choice experiments, label tests of numeric protein claims) instead of more small student surveys.
- **Policy makers:** food-safety and healthy-protein messages match consumer motives; meat-reduction messages do not, since only 15% intend to cut meat. A written ruling on whether soy, nut and cereal milks above 5 g of sugar are *nước giải khát*, before 1 January 2027, would remove an avoidable uncertainty ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).
- **International organisations and funders:** fund a Vietnamese choice experiment, an in-store price test and a label-trust test; each costs tens of thousands of USD ([[ch25-demand-to-frontier]]).
