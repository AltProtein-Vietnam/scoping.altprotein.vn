# 25. What demand asks of the frontier: product targets, plays and public goods

**In brief.**

- Part III's answer to "what demand exists for new protein in Vietnam?" is clear, even where the evidence is thin. Demand pulls new protein as an ingredient inside foods Vietnamese already eat, sold to the firms and kitchens that make them. It does not pull branded replacements for meat.
- The buyers are *chay* (traditional vegetarian food) makers replacing imported textured soy, meat processors extending pork, plant-milk makers adding protein, canteens feeding workers on about VND 25,000 to 31,000 a meal, and exporters of dumplings and other wrapped foods.
- We tested this against 80 named actors and 16 further lines of research ([[ch29-actor-check]]). The core answer holds, but the route to each buyer is narrower and more concrete than our first reading suggested.
- The rules on school menus, protein claims, blend labels, tariffs and sugary drinks are already written. Soy, not meat, is the price an extender (a plant or fungal protein that replaces part of the meat in a product) has to beat.
- Domestic textured soy is an import-substitution plant, best owned by a chay maker, a co-packer (a firm that makes products under another firm's brand) or a distributor, and financed with debt. It is not a venture bet.
- This chapter turns that into ten active target product profiles (TPP-01 to TPP-10), a demand check of the seven active plays of [[ch26-plays]] and twenty cheap moves, each with the funder or budget that would pay for it.

> **Method note.** How the actor check was run, which questions Part III answered and what each new line of research changed are in [[ch29-actor-check]] and [[app-m4-actor-check-waves]]. This chapter carries the results into the product profiles, the play check and the moves. The study is about protein for people; feed appears only as context, so the earlier feed profile, feed plays and feed move are retired here.

## 25.1 Demand findings that change what to make

**Ten findings from Part III.**

1. **The price to beat is eggs and chicken,** at VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein. Lean pork (about VND 71,000) is the ceiling for a mass meat analogue. Imported analogues sit at 12 to 18 times eggs and chicken ([[ch11-protein-diet]]) {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-protein-price-floor}}
2. **Adults do not need more protein; dishes need denser protein.** Protein sits in toppings, fillings and rolls. Chay foods have a median of 4.7 g per 100 g, against 19 g for lean pork ([[ch11-protein-diet]]). For school-age children protein is not the gap either. Published Hanoi primary-school lunches carry about 31.8 g (our calculation), and upland boarding lunches and dinners carried meat, fish or egg in 240 of 242 meals. The documented shortfalls are breakfast, calcium and vitamin A [@PMR-26; @UPL-01; @UPL-02; @UPL-03; @UPL-05; @UPL-06; @UPL-07; @UPL-08; @UPL-09; @UPL-10; @UPL-11; @UPL-12; @UPL-13; @UPL-14; @UPL-15; @UPL-16; @UPL-17] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

   Stunting (18% of children under five in 2023, 32% among ethnic minority children) is a problem of the youngest children. For them, plant products face the young-child protein-quality pattern and registration as foods for children under 36 months [@APR-27; @NQR-01; @REG-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
3. **Few want to eat less meat** (15% of urban online respondents, [[ch13-consumers]]), and meat per person is projected to rise 25% by 2035 ([[ch11-protein-diet]]) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}.
4. **What people ask for is variety, cooking fit, fewer additives and safety,** not a closer copy of meat ([[ch13-consumers]]) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:stated}.
5. **The modern plant category that sells is plant milk.** Local incumbents sell soy milk priced at or below dairy, and nut milk at premium-dairy prices. The best-selling plant-milk products (stock-keeping units, SKUs) sell 60 to 79% of the litres of the best dairy SKUs on Lazada and Tiki. Vinamilk's high-protein nut milk sells as a premium niche of about 70,300 L at its Lazada store [@MKT-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
6. **Chay is the ready channel and the wrong impact count.** It has a shelf, a cold chain, a calendar and makers already buying imported protein. But chay days are already in the meat baseline ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]) [@CHY-23]. Fake chay is a latent rumour, not a live scandal. We found no confirmed case of meat in a Vietnamese chay product from 2015 to 2026 [@TRU-22]. A laboratory test for animal DNA in chay food, using polymerase chain reaction (PCR), has been developed [@TRU-24]. Chay's live trust issues are borax, microbes and unlabelled goods [@TRU-14; @TRU-16; @TRU-18; @CHY-23] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
7. **Blends are the entry route, and soy already holds it.** One large processor lists soy protein in 22 of 67 meat formulations (33%) [@BUY-05]. Seven commercial Vietnamese sausages in one study contained soy, and in that test, sausages made with soy or peanut protein were liked equally [@HXE-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
8. **Words carry risk.** The words *nhân tạo* (artificial) and *giả* (fake) are loaded, and *độn* (padding, filler) means hidden cost-cutting. *Lên men* (fermented) helps. A declared soy ingredient in branded sausages draws no observed penalty [@NOV-69; @NOV-81; @TRU-02; @TRU-03; @NOV-63; @TRU-22] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
9. **Kitchens and factories come before shelves, and they already buy cheap plant protein.** Only an ingredient fits a canteen's protein budget of VND 7,500 to 14,000 a meal. Domestic dry soy-gluten pieces already reach kitchens at about VND 4,000 to 5,100 per 20 g of protein. The same protein costs VND 7,200 to 9,500 from firm branded tofu and VND 8,800 from eggs (our calculation from [@CPC-12; @CHN-41; @CHN-39]) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-canteen-protein-budget}}
10. **The volumes that exist are business-to-business.** On the benchmark path (the middle of the three demand scenarios), the routes create demand for about 19,000 t of protein a year by 2035. About 11,000 to 17,000 t of that would be made in Vietnam for the home market, mostly as import substitution. The routes displace about 0.2% of meat protein ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]) {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}. {{kn:kn-demand-bench-2035}}

**Six findings from the actor check that change the picture.**

- **Schools buy tofu beside meat, and the rule that mattered has expired.** Decision 1340's animal-protein target ended in 2020 [@CHN-33]. The current guidance, Decision 3958/QĐ-BYT (December 2025), sets no animal share and asks for plant protein (legumes, tofu) at least twice a week. Published menus in our audit (`school_menu_weeks.csv`) meet that in only 9 of 48 school-weeks. Plant protein appears on 15.1% of 239 lunch days, almost always as tofu beside a meat dish. The menu software that caterers use prints a standard of "animal protein at least 48%". So a plant ingredient in the plant slot competes with tofu and replaces tofu, not meat. The only school route that removes meat is a labelled blend in the minced-meat and fish-cake dishes served on 28.5% of days [@AIB-39; @PMR-10; @PMR-26] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

  The rule window is Decision 973/QĐ-TTg: a circular on *bán trú* (school lunch and midday care) from the Ministry of Education and Training (MOET), due in 2026, and digital menu tools due in 2027 to 2028 [@PMR-01] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **A sugary-drink excise will shape plant milk.** From 1 January 2027 drinks above 5 g of total sugar per 100 ml pay 8% (10% from 2028). Dairy is exempt, and whether sweetened soy, nut and cereal milks are in scope is unresolved. Core Fami soy milks carry 6.5 to 9.3 g, and the reference high-protein nut milk is also above 5 g [@FTR-01; @FTR-02; @FTR-05; @FTR-17; @FTR-18] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **There is no tariff shelter.** Textured protein, soy isolate, pea protein and gluten enter from China, ASEAN and Korea at 0% under the ASEAN-China free trade agreement and the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership. The 5%, 3% and 10% duties apply only to other origins, and Chinese, French and Canadian pea protein also enter duty-free [@FTR-32] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Retailer private label is a working route for chay.** All five Co.op Select frozen chay dishes are made by one certified co-packer, GN Foods. Lotte Mart sells its own chay label. The products are led by starch and vegetables and carry no protein claim [@CPC-15; @AIB-28; @ECO-35; @FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- **Most label rules are already in force.** A high-protein claim needs 10 g per 100 g under the voluntary Vietnamese national standard (TCVN) 14429:2025. Yet 20 of 43 audited foods already meet the source-of-protein condition per 100 g without claiming it. TCVN 13625:2023 already defines vegetarian and vegan claims. Any ingredient named in a product name must be quantified, and no standard sets a minimum meat content. The label for genetically modified organisms (GMO) applies only above 5% of ingredients [@LBL-03; @LBL-15; @LBL-06; @LBL-11; @LBL-12; @LBL-13; @LBL-10] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **Soy is the extender benchmark, not meat.** Replacing 20% of the lean in *chả lụa* (steamed pork roll) with hydrated soy saves VND 9,100 to 16,600 per kg at 2019 and 2020 hog prices, and extension pays at every annual hog price from 2019 to 2024 (our calculation). So a new extender has to beat hydrated soy at about USD 2.6 to 2.8 per kg of protein, and win on sensory tolerance or the label, not on price against pork [@HXE-01; @DIE-25; @HXE-10; @TIC-12] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

There are ten active profiles, TPP-01 to TPP-10. TPP-11, a feed profile for the retired play T2, was retired because feed is now context only; its record stays in the data file. TPP-12, hybrid fish cakes and fish balls, is a candidate profile for T11, a play that [[ch26-plays]] lists but does not rank.

A zinc-fortified soy dish base for upland school kitchens was proposed and withdrawn. The kitchens it named already serve meat almost every day, so it stays only as a research option for fortified breakfast items [@UPL-04] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**TPP-05 has a second, better-priced job.** Vietnam imported 2,536 t of egg white and egg powders in 2025, with Indian egg white powder at USD 5.90 per kg [@BUY-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Mung bean protein isolate is already a food ingredient in the United States: in 2017 the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) raised no questions on a notice that the isolate, at more than 80% protein, is generally recognised as safe (GRAS). The notice covers use at 3 to 90% by weight in baked goods, drinks, dairy alternatives, plant protein products and snacks [@SLP-11] {general|High}. At 2023 to 2025 import prices, mung beans alone cost USD 3.0 to 4.9 per kg of isolate, assuming 60 to 80% recovery. That is above Chinese pea protein at USD 2 to 3 per kg, but leaves room against egg white (our calculation) [@SLP-14; @DIE-12; @BUY-09] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

These bean costs use the prices partner countries report. On Vietnam's own reported import value they would be USD 6.0 to 8.1 per kg of isolate (DG-378 in [[app-r2-disagreements]]). The egg-white market is small: replacing a tenth of 2025 egg powder imports, tonne for tonne, takes about 250 t of isolate a year (our calculation). There the isolate also competes with the fungal binder of TPP-06, and with potato protein: in a test of 10 commercial plant protein ingredients, potato protein came closest to egg white in emulsifying, foaming and gelling. A 2025 review of US processed foods rated about 80% of sampled egg uses moderately difficult and about 20% difficult to replace (the authors give these rankings low confidence) [@CGG-23] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}. So the first buyer test should be a single function, such as gelling in a sausage, not full egg replacement (our inference).

A cheap first test for either job is a project the buyer sponsors at a university: a student team characterises rice or mung bean protein against the buyer's own pea-protein specification. A Brazilian egg producer had a Berkeley student team rebuild its plant egg powder from Brazilian crops in this way in 2020 [@AML-05] {general|Medium}.

**TPP-12 is a candidate profile, and its demand is untested.** Makers of fish cakes, fish balls and fish tofu (*chả cá*, *cá viên*, *đậu hũ cá*) would replace 10 to 30% of the fish protein with plant protein. When the product name names the fish, the label law already requires the fish share to be printed [@LBL-06; @LBL-08] {VN-direct|Medium}. A plant *chả cá chay* (vegetarian fish cake) already sells at VND 130,000 to 151,800 per kg, beside surimi fish tofu at about VND 171,000 per kg on a business-to-business platform [@FORM-01; @ECO-40; @ECR-06] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Soy isolate costs about USD 2.0 to 2.4 per kg of protein. Tropical surimi, at its 2025 export price of USD 1.67 to 2.24 per kg, costs about USD 8.8 to 14.8 per kg of protein (our calculation, assuming 20 to 25% dry matter) [@BUY-07; @SLS-05; @SLS-20] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

But no buyer asks for a hybrid. Korea, the largest market for Vietnamese surimi, points the other way: a leading Korean maker, Dongwon F&B, opened a fish-cake plant in June 2026 aimed at premium products with 80% or more fish [@SLS-01; @SLS-11] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Taste panels abroad rejected surimi gels at 30% replacement with wheat or rice protein, and at 40% with soy or pea [@SLS-07] {general|Medium} {dx:tested}. We found no Vietnamese study of fish and plant protein blends [@SLS-06] {VN-direct|Medium}.

## 25.3 The seven active plays, checked against demand

The market-pull scores of [[ch26-plays]] were set from supply-side evidence of buyers. Part III's evidence suggests two changes and no reductions. The actor check keeps both, and replaces the evidence behind T1 {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. The three feed plays checked earlier (T2, T3 and T6) were retired because feed is now context only; their rows stay in `play_demand_check.csv`.

| Play | Market pull, supply view | Demand view | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 Domestic textured plant protein | 4 | **5** | Several named buyers already buy the imported equivalent: imports under harmonised system (HS) code 2106.10 from China, India and Serbia were 12,832 t in 2025; export origin rules add no customer [@BUY-02; @TIC-12; @ORG-01] |
| T4 Cassava sugar hub | 2 | 2 | Export demand is for starch; no new protein demand [@EXP-39] |
| T5 Shared pilot and tolling | 2 | 2 | An enabler; adds a need for application and sensory trials |
| T7 High-value precision-fermentation ingredients | 2 | 2, conditional | Buyers of the target ingredient exist and sell non-GMO; the GMO label applies only above 5% of ingredients [@NOV-59; @NOV-62; @NOV-63; @LBL-10] |
| T8 Cultivated seafood research | 1 | 1 | Revealed Asian sales are tiny; the regional cultivated-seafood firms have turned to pet food and skincare for revenue, with no food product on sale [@NOV-10; @AIS-34; @AIS-35] |
| T9 Koji and fungal foods | 2 | 2 (3 if a processor pilots a fungal extender above soy parity) | Fermented reads well; the route is blends and chay ingredients, but soy already fills the extender role [@NOV-63; @NOV-56; @NOV-57] |
| T10 Mung bean and rice protein for plant drinks | 3 | **4** | Vinamilk buys pea protein for a soy-free high-protein milk that sells as a niche; the sugary-drink excise and duty-free pea protein narrow the room [@BUY-16; @MKT-03; @FTR-02; @FTR-32] |

**T1: the market pull stands, but the thesis changes.** T1 keeps a market pull of 5 because named chay makers already buy the imported product. But the pool a soy extrusion line can serve is at most about 12,800 t of HS 2106.10 imports from three low-cost origins. It is not the whole estimated food-use pool of 40,000 t of imported plant-protein ingredients. The near tripling of plant-protein imports is mostly HS 3504 protein at about USD 1 per kg, which needs other plants [@BUY-02; @TIC-12; @TIC-14] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Capital is not the barrier. Shandong makers list complete lines of 0.2 to 1.5 t an hour, with dryer, at USD 11,000 to 100,000 (list prices) [@TIC-02; @TIC-03; @TIC-07]. By our calculation a whole plant installs for about USD 0.3 to 1.4 million, and working capital of USD 0.6 to 3.5 million is the larger need {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. {{kn:kn-tvp-line-capex}}

The flour price decides the margin. There is no tariff shelter: Chinese product lands at USD 1.29 to 1.38 per kg, and India-origin textured soy lands lower still. Food-grade defatted soy flour is 63 to 85% of cost, and it comes from the same countries that export the finished product. So its delivered price decides whether the line makes or loses money (our calculation) [@TIC-12; @TIC-13; @BUY-02; @FTR-32] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. {{kn:kn-t1-flour-breakeven}}

The benchmark path supports about one line making 1 tonne an hour (1 t/h), or two 0.5 t/h lines, by 2035. So T1 is an import-substitution plant, best owned by a large chay maker, a group of chay makers, a co-packer or an ingredient distributor. It is best financed with debt and trade finance, not venture equity. Its return is a commodity spread that Chinese exporters can compress, it holds no intellectual property and its exit is a trade sale {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

**What changes in the ranking.** With the two market-pull changes and the published weights, domestic textured plant protein (T1) leads the balanced preset at 3.95, against 3.9 for the cassava sugar hub (T4). The lead is 0.05: a one-point fall in T1's market pull or defensibility returns first place to T4. T1 leads under the balanced, investor, startup and manufacturer presets.

T4 stays first or second under every preset. It leads under the policy, research and international presets, where the shared pilot and tolling play (T5) stays second. Among the seven active plays, plant-drink proteins (T10) move up to third place for investors, startups and manufacturers. The frontier bets (T7, T8) stay where they were (our calculation from `plays.csv`, `play_weight_presets.csv` and `play_demand_check.csv`) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

**A candidate that is not ranked.** [[ch26-plays]] also lists T11, hybrid fish cakes and fish balls (profile TPP-12), as a candidate it does not rank. T11's demand is untested. Its market pull is 2, and its first test is a maker trial and a sensory panel at 10 to 30% substitution (OQ-491) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Its row in `play_demand_check.csv` holds its demand-side kill tests.

**Proposed supply-score changes, not applied.** We have not changed `plays.csv`. The actor-check evidence suggests two supply-score changes for T1, recorded in the `note_v0_4` column of `play_demand_check.csv` for readers to apply with the interactive ranking in [[ch26-plays]]. They are capital efficiency from 4 to 5 and Vietnam advantage from 3 to 2. They net to zero on the balanced score: T1 stays first at 3.95, and first under the investor, startup and manufacturer presets (our calculation) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

**Demand-side kill tests** to add to the plays (`play_demand_check.csv`):

- **T1** fails if delivered food-grade defatted soy flour costs more than about USD 700 to 940 per t for a 1 t/h line at 70% utilisation (USD 590 to 900 for a 0.5 t/h line). It also fails if fewer than three buyers sign conditional orders totalling at least 2,000 t a year, or if India-origin textured soy (USD 0.98 per kg FOB, the price at the exporting port) becomes the reference price (our calculation) [@TIC-12; @TIC-13; @BUY-02] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. It fails, too, if the anchor buyer's bank will not finance the T1 supplier's receivables, through supplier finance or factoring under Circular 20/2024/TT-NHNN of the State Bank of Vietnam. Without an anchor buyer, the working capital of USD 0.6 to 3.5 million has no named source [@SLK-05; @SLK-18; @SLK-19] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
- **T7** is confined to export and business-to-business uses if purified precision-fermented proteins must carry a GMO declaration at the doses buyers use.
- **T9** fails if, at the replacement level that gives the same saving as soy, a fungal blend in industrial *xúc xích* or a coarse filling scores below the processor's own soy-extended formula in a blind test. It also fails if no processor will pay above hydrated-soy parity (about USD 1.2 per kg dry, the 2025 unit value of HS 2106.10 imports from China, at an assumed 45% protein) [@TIC-12; @HXE-10] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.
- **T10** fails if an isolate cannot reach the band between Chinese and Western pea protein with UHT stability. It also fails if host drinks cannot be made at or below 5 g of total sugar per 100 ml once plant milks are ruled inside the sugary-drink excise [@FTR-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
  - The mung bean arm fails if no buyer takes the starch co-product at a price that brings raw-material cost inside the TPP-05 band. Beans alone cost USD 3.0 to 4.9 per kg of isolate at 2023 to 2025 import prices, against USD 2 to 3 for Chinese pea protein (our calculation) [@SLP-14; @DIE-12; @BUY-09] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.
  - The rice arm fails if no rice syrup or starch maker agrees to host it within two years. Raw material alone costs USD 5.1 to 6.6 per kg of rice protein product, so it pays only as a co-product, and we found no such maker in Vietnam (our calculation) [@FS-26; @DIE-12; @SLP-01; @SLP-08] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.
- **T11** (a candidate) fails if a sensory panel or the first buyer rejects it at 15% substitution, or if no maker agrees to a trial run within 12 months. It also fails if surimi protein comes to cost less than about twice as much as plant protein, per kg of protein. Or it fails if target export buyers set a minimum fish content above the hybrid level: a leading Korean maker is moving to premium products with 80% fish or more [@SLS-11] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

## 25.4 Twenty cheap moves, and who would pay

Most of the demand gaps in Part III can be closed for tens of thousands of USD. The full list, with who, when and who would pay, is in `demand_moves.csv`; the underlying questions are in [[app-r1-open-questions]]. Every move now names a payer, or says why none was found.

Only four of the original nineteen moves have a funder whose stated scope fits and that accepts Vietnam. Two consumer-acceptance studies fit the Bezos Centre for Sustainable Protein at the National University of Singapore (NUS). Two naming and GMO rules fit Nova Vista's market-shaping programme. Nova Vista and now ACE Movement Grants exclude diet-change and culture-change work, and ReRoot Asia has no Vietnam track [@AFN-05; @AFN-15; @AFN-29; @FUF-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. {{kn:kn-funder-fit}} The rest belong to industry, to trade bodies or to national budgets.

One research funder's scope fits Vietnamese fish science, though not these moves. Coefficient Giving (formerly Open Philanthropy) runs a 2026 alternative-protein research call that treats taste as the main barrier. Its staff name flavour work on carp, tilapia, milkfish, catfish and pond loach, and ask for open, pre-competitive data [@CGG-03; @CAP-57] {general|Medium} {dx:stated}. Vietnam farms several of these, led by pangasius catfish at about 1.9 Mt in 2025 [@MAC-15] {VN-direct|High}. Both rounds have closed, decisions are due by the end of 2026, and no awards were public in October 2026.

Through public research metadata we found no Coefficient Giving-funded alternative-protein research with a Vietnamese author. The only paper tagged with this funder and a Vietnamese affiliation is a 2025 fish-welfare survey led from Tra Vinh University [@CGG-02; @CGG-14] {VN-direct|Medium}. The funder's own grant list agrees: none of its 61 alternative-protein grants (2016 to 2026) names Vietnam or Southeast Asia, and only 9 fund research or R&D [@CGG-19] {general|High}.

**Measure.**

- **Chay days (DMV-01).** First ask the Global Diet Quality Project or Gallup to tabulate the 2021 flesh-free share (7.9% of adults) by the lunar date of the recall day. Then add a chay-day question to a probability survey, one with a random sample [@APR-36; @BRD-03]. *Who would pay:* no philanthropic payer; national survey budgets (the National Statistics Office and the National Institute of Nutrition, NIN) or the Diet Quality Questionnaire of the Global Alliance for Improved Nutrition (GAIN).
- **Customs declarations (DMV-02)** for HS 3504, 2106.10, 1109 and 2102.20.10 (yeast for animal feeding), to split food from feed and name the buyers of T1 products. *Who would pay:* partly Nova Vista, or technical assistance from the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank (ADB); otherwise a would-be T1 owner.
- **Sensory and choice tests (DMV-03) and a randomised label and name test (DMV-04).** *Who would pay:* the Bezos Centre at NUS, with a Vietnamese institute.
- **One canteen and school trial with three arms (DMV-05).** The three arms are: an extender or canteen ingredient in minced dishes; a chay-day menu; and a school arm. In the school arm, one caterer that writes one menu for several schools serves a textured-soy or blended dish in the plant slot and in one minced dish, with daily published menus as controls. Log the dish species replaced, because animals spared per meal differ about 34-fold between a pork and a chicken dish (our calculation), and chicken leads only 13% of canteen protein dishes [@UPL-27; @UPL-28; @UPL-29; @UPL-30; @UPL-32; @FUF-03]. A 2026 US menu experiment supports logging the species: a chicken-style analogue cut chicken choice by 3.65 percentage points, while adding generic analogues barely moved meat choice [@CGG-11] {general|Medium} {dx:tested}. *Who would pay:* the extender arm partly fits Nova Vista's procurement lever and the Bezos Centre. Humane World Viet Nam fits the chay-day arm as implementer, but no current funder both covers Vietnam and funds diet change. The school arm has no external payer.
- **A minimart repeat test (DMV-06), the share of meat eaten as processed products (DMV-09) and a Nha Trang hotel and menu audit (DMV-10).** *Who would pay:* industry and AltProtein Vietnam; Humane World fits the hotel audit as implementer.
- **Quote requests to wrapped-food buyers (DMV-11).** Ask three EU importers of Asian frozen foods, and two brand owners already sourcing from Vietnam, to quote for one vegetable and one plant-protein product, with their certification and origin requirements [@XBA-20]. Retailer protein targets need no survey; they are published [@XBA-02; @XBA-14]. A state-funded trade-fair pavilion is a low-cost venue for the test. Under the 2026 National Trade Promotion Programme of the Ministry of Industry and Trade (MOIT), the state pays all booth rent and standard build for a Vietnam pavilion of about 16 booths at the Private Label Show in the United States (15 to 17 November 2026) [@SLK-16] {VN-direct|High}. A plant-protein or protein-upgraded chay product shown there can carry the quote requests. Whether the programme funds a pavilion at a European fair is not known. *Who would pay:* industry and trade bodies, with MOIT paying the booth at a Vietnam pavilion.
- **A soy-flour price sheet (DMV-21).** Five delivered quotes for food-grade defatted soy flour and three for Chinese plain textured soy, with the T1 breakeven. It costs tens of hours and decides the T1 kill test [@TIC-12; @TIC-13]. *Who would pay:* the would-be owner or its lender.

**Build public goods.**

- **A chay safety and composition survey (DMV-07).** Test borax, preservatives, microbes and protein first, with animal DNA as a PCR screen; the method of QUATEST 3 (Quality Assurance and Testing Centre 3) awaits accreditation. Publish with the Vietnam Food Administration (VFA), publish the methods and do not name small makers [@TRU-24]. *Who would pay:* partly the Bezos Centre, UNICEF Viet Nam or the Australian Centre for International Agricultural Research (ACIAR); otherwise food-safety budgets.
- **A standing panel of research and procurement buyers (DMV-08)** to publish specifications, minimum orders and qualification times. *Who would pay:* an industry association.

A feed carbon-footprint benchmark (DMV-12) is retired as a demand move because feed is now context only; its record stays in `demand_moves.csv`.

**Set rules (policy makers).** These moves add a demand-side reason to the policy options of [[ch27-policy-options]]. The Food Safety Law takes first comments at the National Assembly's October 2026 session and is considered for passage at the May 2027 session. So a new-food clause needs to be in the draft revised between the two sessions, in practice by early 2027 [@APR-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

Of the four label moves, two are largely law already and need enforcement and testing, not new rules. One should defend a permissive regime, and one shrinks to a single question [@LBL-06; @LBL-10; @LBL-15] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Decree 37/2026 has replaced Decree 43/2017 for goods labels, and Circular 30/2026/TT-BYT has replaced Circular 29/2023 for nutrition labels.

- **Chay claims (DMV-13).** TCVN 13625:2023 already defines vegetarian and vegan claims. But its vegetarian class allows egg, milk and honey, and it says nothing on alliums. Ask VFA to state that an unqualified *chay* or *thuần chay* (strict vegetarian) claim means the vegan criteria, with egg or milk named in a qualifier. Enforce the existing rules alongside DMV-07.
- **Blend labels (DMV-14).** Any meat named in a product name must already be quantified. Narrow the move to a voluntary industry code for generic names such as *giò lụa* (steamed pork roll) and *xúc xích*. The code would state the meat share when a plant or fungal protein replaces 10% or more of the meat. Its value is defensive, as proof against a *độn* accusation [@LBL-06; @TRU-03]. *Who would pay:* the processors.
- **Names (DMV-15).** We found no rule that reserves *thịt* (meat), *sữa* (milk) or *phô mai* (cheese). Names must only not mislead, and practice already qualifies meat words. Defend this permissive regime in the new Food Safety Law decree, and aim vocabulary guidance at state and media use.
- **GMO status (DMV-16).** The GMO label applies only above 5% of ingredients, so lactoferrin-type uses (about 0.08%) are not caught. Ask the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE) and VFA one question: is a recombinant protein identical to the native protein a detectable product of the modified gene, and may a product containing one below 5% still claim non-GMO? The answer could name the evidence a product must show: no viable production cells, no recombinant DNA and no antibiotic-resistance markers. The question is not only Vietnamese. In October 2026 an EU forum of consumer, environmental and health groups, working with industry, asked the EU for the same clarity on products made by genetically modified microorganisms in closed fermenters (contained use) [@PPE-06] {general|Medium}. *Who would pay for DMV-15 and DMV-16:* Nova Vista, or for a small Vietnamese organisation ACE Movement Grants (policy work only, deadline 18 October 2026).
- **School menus (DMV-17).** National guidance already has no animal share. Ask MOET, NIN and Ajinomoto to replace the menu software's 48% animal-protein default with targets per lunch for protein quality, zinc, iron and sodium. Also ask them to add textured soy and legume products to the software's food list, inside the MOET circular due in 2026 and the digital tools due in 2027 to 2028 [@PMR-26; @PMR-01]. A second version is a procurement standard for innovative protein in public and state-enterprise canteens. *Who would pay:* UNICEF Viet Nam's 2027 to 2031 programme for the school version; Nova Vista's procurement lever for the canteen version.
- **Customs subheadings (DMV-18)** for soy isolate, pea protein and textured protein. *Who would pay:* the own budget of the Ministry of Finance (MOF), partly the World Bank or ADB.
- **An origin note for exporters (DMV-19).** It would cover the EU cap of 20% non-originating flour and starch in wrapped foods, the 10% tolerance for imported textured soy in 2106 foods, and claiming the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP) for the UK. Imported soy protein is not the main constraint [@ORG-01]. *Who would pay:* industry and trade bodies.
- **A written sugary-drink ruling (DMV-20).** MOF to state, before 1 January 2027, whether soy milk (TCVN 12443:2018), nut and cereal milks above 5 g of total sugar per 100 ml are *nước giải khát* (soft drinks) [@FTR-01; @FTR-13]. *Who would pay:* the plant-milk makers.

## 25.5 What not to do

- Do not size a business on stated survey intent or on the published Vietnamese market sizes; neither has a revealed basis.
- Do not launch premium imported-style analogues into gourmet channels expecting mass uptake. Do not enter foodservice with an imported branded analogue through business-to-business distributors either. CP Meat Zero minced was listed on Kamereo at 1.5 to 2.1 times CP's own minced pork per kg, and none of its eight SKUs was orderable in September 2026 [@AIS-25; @CPC-12] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- Do not price a mass product above pork, a chay product above today's chay shelf, or an extender above hydrated soy without proof that it allows more undetected replacement.
- Do not lead with "meat replacement", "the future of meat", "lab" or environmental claims, and do not use *độn* or "x% meat replaced" wording. Lead with taste in familiar dishes, protein, safety and origin, and name the plant part as an ingredient.
- Do not name products *thịt nhân tạo* (artificial meat) or market cultivated or precision-fermented products to chay buyers.
- Do not start in schools with a branded or meat-replacement product. The school plant slot is small (about 1,000 t of protein a year, our calculation from an estimated 0.8 billion school lunches a year in `demand_assumptions.csv`), filled by tofu and under-used. Enter later as a caterer ingredient that beats tofu on cost, or as a labelled blend in minced dishes [@PMR-10; @AIB-39; @CHN-29; @CHN-31] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Do not start in wet markets or tourist hotels either.
- Do not pitch a fortified soy dish to upland boarding or border-commune kitchens as filling a meatless lunch. Their published lunches carry meat almost every day, and a Decree 339 lunch spent 79 to 98% of its non-rice money on pork (91 to 98% on pork and tofu together). Pitch cost per gram of protein against pork, which these kitchens already stretch with tofu [@UPL-01; @UPL-04] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- Do not present import substitution, or protein upgrades on existing chay days, to welfare or climate funders as meat displaced, animals spared or carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) avoided. They achieve none of these (our reasoning), and ACE Movement Grants will not fund diet-change work in any region [@FUF-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}.
- Do not count sales into existing chay days as meat displaced.

## 25.6 What would change our view

- A tested Vietnamese study (choice experiment or in-store trial) showing repeat buying of a modern analogue or blend at 20% or more above the meat it replaces.
- Retail or e-commerce panel data showing sustained growth of modern plant-based meat since 2024.
- A probability survey, or a lunar-date tabulation of the 2021 diet survey, showing that chay keeping is rising among young urban people. That would make added chay days a real displacement route.
- A large processor launching a declared blend with a novel protein, or a formula maker sourcing a precision-fermented protein.
- A new Food Safety Law, now expected to be considered for passage in May 2027, with a working new-food route and a clear treatment of purified fermentation proteins.
- Delivered soy-flour quotes below about USD 700 per t, which would make a domestic textured-soy line pay even at the high-cost end.
- A food-safety shock in meat followed by a measurable switch to plant proteins, which the 2019 to 2020 pork shock did not produce.

**For each reader.**

- **Startups:** sell a familiar food with little capital, and aim first for ingredient, private-label or co-packing supply to incumbents. A restaurant menu listing for your own analogue brand is not a business customer: in the region such firms mostly went quiet or pivoted. Our firm register (`firm_outcomes.csv`, counted in `firm_base_rates.csv`, our calculation) holds 24 regional and Vietnamese firms since 2019: 7 are active, 4 pivoted, 2 exited and 11 are dormant. We found no sign of activity after 2022 at any of the 4 Vietnamese meat-analogue startups, while all 3 makers of traditional formats are trading. TiNDLE (pivoted to private label), Emmay (trading) and Karana (dormant in our register) are examples [@AIS-28; @AIS-39; @BRD-08]. No founder has yet tested the ingredient route, so treat it as the hypothesis to test, not a proven path {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.
- **Investors:** T1 is a debt-financed import-substitution plant for a chay maker, co-packer or distributor, not a venture thesis. Underwrite the delivered soy-flour price, conditional orders and cost per gram of protein. Investors now say they fund offtake, contracted revenue and unit economics, which T1 and T10 can show and consumer analogues cannot [@AIS-01; @AIS-15] {general|Medium} {dx:stated}.
- **Manufacturers:** three new ingredients can go into your products: a domestic textured soy, a non-soy isolate for drinks at or below 5 g of sugar per 100 ml, and a fungal ingredient that beats your own soy-extended formula. Test blends first in industrial *xúc xích* and coarse fillings, not *giò lụa*, blind and at equal saving against your soy recipe. Keep 10 to 20% replacement in fine emulsions and 30% in coarse mince, and consider private-label chay through an existing co-packer [@HXE-01; @HXE-04; @HXE-09; @CPC-15] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.
- **Research bodies:** the tested-evidence gap (sensory, choice and label tests in Vietnamese formats) is the most useful thing you can close for the whole sector. The Bezos Centre at NUS is the one funder whose scope fits it.
- **Policy makers:** most label rules already exist, so enforce them. Defend permissive naming, answer the single GMO question, rule on plant milks and the sugary-drink excise before 2027, and put protein quality, zinc and cost into the school menu software.
- **International organisations and funders:** match the route to your unit and your rules. Welfare funders: a canteen trial with Humane World Viet Nam, aimed at chicken dishes, which lead only one protein dish in eight in our coding of caterers' menus [@UPL-27; @UPL-28; @UPL-29; @UPL-30; @UPL-32]. Research and development funders: isolate and fungal functionality, and Vietnamese blind tests. Nutrition agencies: protein quality and zinc in school food standards. The chay-day survey item belongs in national surveys.

  Market-shaping funders: canteen procurement and the naming and GMO rules. Nova Vista's programme funds procurement, advance market commitments and retailer ratio targets; it excludes whole-food, culture-change and diet-change work, and wants more policy staff, especially in Japan and Korea. On the benchmark path the displacing routes spare about 2.4 to 2.7 million animals a year by 2035, 93 to 94% of them chickens and ducks, and avoid about 0.14 Mt CO2e (our calculation, [[ch18-demand-sizing]]). So climate is a co-benefit, not the case, and corporate pledges are a weak lever: Accor's dish target is the only one we found that binds Vietnamese units [@AFN-05; @AFN-23; @FUF-03; @FUF-04; @AFN-40] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.
