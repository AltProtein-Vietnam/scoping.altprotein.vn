# 14. Fermentation, cultivated meat and blends: demand for the frontier categories

**In brief.**
- **Evidence is thinnest for the newest proteins.** For cultivated meat and precision-fermented foods, Vietnam has no tested or revealed consumer evidence, only surveys of young or online samples.
- **Asian sales of cultivated meat are small.** After six years of legal sale in Singapore, it has sold tens of thousands of servings. What reached customers were blends with 3% to 20% cultivated cells.
- **Precision fermentation sells to firms.** Vietnamese formula makers already buy one target ingredient, lactoferrin. The GMO label applies only above 5% of ingredients, so low-dose uses escape it.
- **Vietnamese already eat blends without noticing.** One large processor lists soy protein in a third of its meat recipes. Blending into familiar foods is the likeliest route for new protein.
- **Soy is the price to beat, not meat.** A new extender has to beat hydrated soy.
- **Words matter.** *Lên men* (fermented) reads as positive. *Độn* (filler) is a fraud word, so name a blend's plant part as an ingredient.

## 14.1 Cultivated meat and seafood: open attitudes, tiny sales

**Vietnamese evidence is stated only.** One Asian survey places Vietnamese respondents among the most open to cultivated meat. In a survey of young Vietnamese, about a quarter were optimists [@NOV-01; @NOV-03] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. No Vietnamese study has measured behaviour or willingness to pay.

**Tested acceptance in Asia is lower.** Chinese consumers in a choice experiment would pay less for cultivated meat than for farmed meat [@NOV-29] {general|Medium} {dx:tested}. In Japan, stated acceptance was 41% for cultured meat against 70% for plant-based meat [@NOV-30] {general|Medium} {dx:stated}. Treat Vietnamese openness as a ceiling.

**Real sales in Asia are tiny.** Two firms reported a few thousand and about 25,000 servings sold in Singapore [@NOV-10; @NOV-14] {VN-adjacent|Low} {dx:revealed}. The second has since moved to making products for others [@AIS-37] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. In 2025 a Singapore minister cited "weaker-than-expected consumer acceptance globally" [@NOV-22] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. We found no approvals in Korea, Japan, China or Thailand [@NOV-25; @NOV-13; @NOV-21] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-cultivated-sg-servings}}

**What reached customers were blends.** The only cultivated product in Asian shops is 3% cultivated chicken, at a price close to imported meat copies in Vietnam (our calculation) [@NOV-10; @FORM-01] {VN-adjacent|Low} {dx:inferred}. A cultivated steak approved in Singapore in 2026 is 10% to 20% cultivated beef [@NOV-18] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Vietnamese seafood exporters bought options, which have faded.** Vinh Hoan and Minh Phu backed or partnered with cultivated-seafood firms in 2021 and 2022, with no later news [@NOV-74; @NOV-75; @NOV-76] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Those partners now earn from pet food and skincare [@AIS-34; @AIS-35; @AIS-36] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The stakes show strategic interest, not buyer demand. Seafood exporters remain natural hosts for cultivated seafood research (play T8, [[ch26-plays]]).

## 14.2 Precision-fermented proteins: an ingredient for firms that pay for function

**A business market for premium function.** In Asia, precision fermentation reaches the market as an ingredient for firms that pay for a specific job: lactoferrin for infant formula, whey for sports drinks, and sweet proteins to cut sugar [@NOV-45; @NOV-44; @NOV-47; @NOV-48] {VN-adjacent|Low} {dx:revealed}. Two consumer launches in Singapore left no trace of sales [@NOV-43] {VN-adjacent|Low} {dx:revealed}.

**Vietnamese buyers exist and pay.** VitaDairy, Nutifood and Morinaga sell lactoferrin formulas in Vietnam [@NOV-59; @NOV-60; @NOV-61; @BUY-19] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. We found no public interest in precision-fermented proteins from any Vietnamese dairy or formula maker {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**The same buyers sell "non-GMO".** VitaDairy and Vinasoy advertise non-GMO [@NOV-62; @NOV-63] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. A Vietnamese auction found an 84% premium for soy milk labelled non-GM [@NOV-08; @NOV-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested}.

**The clash with GMO labels is narrower than it looks.** These proteins probably count as GMO products ([[ch07-rules]]). But the GMO label applies only above 5% of ingredients, or when the modified gene or its product can be detected [@REG-18; @REG-02; @LBL-10] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Lactoferrin in formula is about 0.08% of ingredients, so it would need no label (our calculation) [@LBL-25; @LBL-10] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. The clash is with voluntary "non-GMO" claims and with high-dose uses, such as a drink whose main protein is precision-fermented whey {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. {{kn:kn-gmo-premium}}

## 14.3 Fermented foods and fungi: "fermented" is a positive word

**Vietnam's food culture is built on fermentation:** *nước mắm* (fish sauce), *tương* (fermented soybean paste) and *mắm* (fermented fish). *Lên men* (fermented) is a positive marketing word, used for nut milks sold for digestion [@NOV-63; @NOV-64] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. In one sample of young people, 46% would try an energy bar made with protein from brewing by-products [@RD-32] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}.

Our inference: "fermented" is the best frame for yeast, koji and fungal protein, but not for precision fermentation at high doses, which may need a GMO label {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. A Chinese yeast protein is already marketed to Vietnamese food makers, with no public price or named user [@AIS-38; @CPC-21] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Mycoprotein has not proved itself in Asia.** Quorn's Philippine owner saw its meat-alternative sales fall from 2022 to 2024, closed plants and called it "a declining market" [@NOV-50; @AIS-44; @AIS-48] {general|Medium} {dx:revealed}. China approved its first mycoprotein in 2025, as an ingredient for firms [@NOV-49] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}.

**Mushrooms are a real bridge.** King oyster mushroom is a common meat substitute in Vietnam. One mushroom-food startup sells about 2 t a month [@AIS-39] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}. But readers criticise mushroom mock meat for heavy additives [@NOV-65; @NOV-77] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Any fungal product needs a short list of familiar ingredients.

## 14.4 Blends: the most likely entry route

A blend is a meat product in which plant, fungal or fermented protein replaces part of the meat.

**Stated interest is high but means little.** In Vietnam, 55% of an online sample were very interested in blends [@CON-02; @NOV-35; @NOV-36] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:stated}. Read this as low resistance, not demand.

**Tested evidence is more useful.** In a 2025 blind tasting in Singapore, a chicken mince with 30% to 50% plant protein beat pure chicken. Tasters liked blends more than plant-based meat, and most preferred about 75% meat [@NOV-37; @NOV-39; @NOV-40] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:tested}. In a US test, a beef and mushroom blend won blind but lost once people were told what was in it [@GLB-30] {general|Medium} {dx:tested}. In Japan and Singapore, blends sell mainly to canteens and processors [@NOV-41; @AIS-10; @AIS-06] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}. {{kn:kn-blend-preference}}

**Vietnam already eats blends.** Vissan lists soy protein in 22 of 67 meat recipes (33%), mostly in cheap lines [@BUY-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Its mainstream sausage is 58% lean pork plus soy protein and starch [@NOV-56; @NOV-57; @NOV-58] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. All seven Vietnamese sausages tested in a 2017 study contained soy [@HXE-02] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested}. Masan's noodle cup "with a real meat stick" also lists soy protein [@TRU-25] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. Mixed-protein meat products sell at mass scale without a label penalty. {{kn:kn-vissan-soy-share}}

**No rule stands in the way.** No standard sets a minimum meat content for processed meat, so a product with a 20% to 30% extender keeps its meat name. The named meat's share must be printed [@LBL-11; @LBL-13; @LBL-12; @LBL-06; @BUY-05] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.

**Soy extension already pays, so soy is the benchmark.** Hydrated textured soy costs a processor about VND 10,000 to 15,000 per kg, against VND 83,000 to 141,000 for lean pork trimmings (our calculation) [@DIE-25; @AIB-15; @TIC-12] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Replacing 20% of the lean in *chả lụa* (pork roll) saves 14% to 16% of raw-material cost. It pays at every pork price since 2019 (our calculation) [@HXE-01; @DIE-25] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

So a new fungal or fermented extender has to beat hydrated soy, at about USD 0.7 to 1.2 per kg dry. To earn more, it must let processors replace more meat unnoticed, or carry a label benefit {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Chinese soy enters duty-free, so blends will carry imported soy unless a domestic textured-protein maker wins the business (play T1, [[ch26-plays]]; [[ch16-business-buyers]]) [@FTR-32] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}. {{kn:kn-hybrid-saving}}

**Imported pork lowers the bar for some processors.** Imported frozen pork landed at about half the price of domestic pork in 2026, so processors using it save less from extension [@MIM-09; @MIM-07] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. New protein would displace at most about 1.6% of imported meat protein by 2035 (our calculation; [[ch01-why-vietnam]]) [@MIM-04; @QNT-01] {VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate} {dx:inferred}.

**Formats set the ceiling.** Coarse products such as patties and mince tolerate about 25% to 30% replacement. Fine, smooth products such as sausage and pork roll tolerate 10% to 20% [@HXE-05; @HXE-06; @HXE-08; @HXE-07; @HXE-04; @HXE-09; @HXE-12] {general|Medium} {dx:tested}. In the one Vietnamese test of pork roll, graininess cut liking, which rules out granules there [@HXE-01] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:tested}. Nutrition is not the obstacle: a 20% to 30% soy blend keeps good protein quality (our calculation) [@NQR-05; @NQR-07] {general|Medium} {dx:inferred}.

**Why blending is still the likely route (our inference).** {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:inferred}
- It avoids the "fake meat" frame: the product is still meat, and the new part is an ingredient.
- It matches what sells in Asia.
- It does not have to beat meat on price, only soy.
- Processors already extend to save money.
- No standard sets a minimum meat content.
- Blends replace meat by design, which matters for security and animal welfare ([[ch18-demand-sizing]]).

**Blending does not guarantee a buyer.** We found no Vietnamese processor interested in fungal or fermented extenders [@BUY-04; @BUY-10] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Vissan could save a large share of its profit by extending more, yet its labels suggest it does not ([[ch16-business-buyers]]) [@BUY-04; @BUY-05; @DIE-25] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}. Premium lines sell on meat content, so blends start in cheap and industrial lines [@BUY-13; @TRU-27] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.

## 14.5 Words, trust and religion: avoid "artificial", "fake" and "filler"

- **"Artificial" and "fake" are loaded.** The press uses *thịt nhân tạo* (artificial meat) for both cultivated and plant-based meat. A leading outlet framed cultivated meat as a "nightmare", with no safety evidence [@NOV-69; @NOV-74; @NOV-81; @NOV-71] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}.
- **"Fake" also means fraud.** Fake-egg rumours hurt egg producers in 2025, and police broke a fake beef ring in 2026 [@NOV-73; @NOV-72] {VN-direct|High} {dx:revealed}. One news site uses *thịt giả* (fake meat) for both new foods and fraud [@TRU-22; @TRU-10] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. A product marketed as "just like real meat" invites that link.
- ***Độn* (filler) is a fraud word.** Advice columns teach readers to spot starch-padded pork roll. Sellers advertise *không độn* (no filler), including no soy filler [@TRU-02; @TRU-03; @TRU-27] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.
- **Declared soy draws no penalty.** We found no story treating declared soy in branded meat products as fraud [@TRU-22] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Products that name a culinary plant, such as a chicken and shiitake sausage, sell as healthy [@TRU-32; @TRU-26] {VN-direct|Low} {dx:revealed}.
- **Trust in processed meat is low in 2026,** after borax prosecutions and diseased pork in school meals [@TRU-07; @TRU-20] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. The scandals are not about plant protein.
- **No Vietnamese name has been tested.** In Singapore, "cultivated meat" was the preferred English name [@NOV-32; @NOV-82; @NOV-80; @LBL-20] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:stated}.
- **Halal is workable for export.** Singapore and Malaysia accept cultivated meat as halal under conditions [@NOV-52; @NOV-53] {VN-adjacent|Medium} {dx:revealed}.
- ***Chay* status is unresolved.** Cell-based products are very unlikely to be sold as *chay*; animal-free fermented proteins are an open question (our inference) {VN-direct|Low} {dx:inferred}.

## 14.6 What this means for the frontier categories

**What to make, in order.**
1. **Ingredients for blends.** Fungal, fermented or plant proteins that replace 10% to 20% of the lean in smooth products and up to 30% in mince. Start with factory sausage and coarse fillings such as meatballs and dumplings; move to pork roll later, using a fine paste, not granules. Price near hydrated soy for extending, or against egg and milk protein for binding ([[ch16-business-buyers]]).
2. **Functional precision-fermented proteins for business buyers**, such as lactoferrin, for export or specialty nutrition (play T7, [[ch26-plays]]).
3. **Cultivated meat and seafood as research**, or with a seafood exporter (play T8). There is no domestic consumer demand to design for before the mid-2030s.

**Labels.** Avoid *nhân tạo* (artificial), anything like *giả* (fake), *độn* (filler) and "x% meat replaced". Name the plant part as a culinary ingredient, such as *nấm hương* (shiitake) or *đạm nấm* (mushroom protein). Lead with *lên men* (fermented) for fermentation. Test names before launch.

**For each reader.**
- **Startups:** supply processors and caterers with an ingredient, not a consumer brand. Price per kilogram of protein against hydrated soy. Check the GMO status of any high-dose product before launch.
- **Investors:** discount stated acceptance. Do not count the seafood exporters as co-investors for a food product. Judge extenders against the processor's own soy recipe, and look for written intentions to buy.
- **Manufacturers:** soy already saves you money. Pilot a fungal extender only if it replaces more meat unnoticed or carries a better label. Test it in a coarse format first.
- **Researchers:** run a blind test of a fungal extender against the processor's soy recipe, at equal saving, with an informed arm and a label test. The food technology group at Ho Chi Minh City University of Technology, which wrote both existing Vietnamese sausage studies, is an obvious partner [@HXE-01; @HXE-02] {VN-direct|High}.
- **Policy makers:** keep the permissive naming rules in the new Food Safety Law [@APR-07; @LBL-06] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. Narrow the GMO question to whether an identical protein made by microbes counts as a detectable GMO product [@LBL-10; @LBL-08; @LBL-09] {VN-direct|Medium} {dx:revealed}. A voluntary industry code to state the meat share under generic names would defend makers against the filler charge.
- **International organisations:** support a national survey on new foods that includes rural and older people. All current Vietnamese data are urban and young.
