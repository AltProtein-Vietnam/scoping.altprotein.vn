# 2. The alternative-protein economy today

**In brief.**
- Vietnam already eats and makes a great deal of non-animal protein, but almost none of it comes from the technologies the global alternative-protein sector talks about.
- The real economy is soy milk (one firm holds about 80% of it), tofu, and industrial *đồ chay* (traditional vegetarian food). Meat and frozen-food processors make *đồ chay* on the same lines as meat.
- Our September 2026 audit of 186 products in 11 stores found that most "alt-protein" shelf products are low in protein.
- Every locally made product with 10 g or more of protein per 100 g runs on imported textured soy, soy concentrate or wheat gluten (partly inferred from labels), much of it from China.
- Modern plant-based meat brands are nearly absent, and we found no Vietnamese company making fermented protein ingredients for food.
- Novel protein is already sold into animal feed in Vietnam. Feed is context for this study, not its subject.

---

## 2.1 The incumbent non-animal protein economy

Soy milk, tofu and *đồ chay* make up most of Vietnam's non-animal protein economy.

| Segment | Scale and leaders | Main protein input | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soy milk | Vinasoy (Quang Ngai Sugar): more than VND 4,100 billion of soy-milk revenue in 2024, about 80% market share (self-reported); 390 million L/yr capacity (2022) | Whole soybeans (imported) | [@ECO-41; @IND-48; @IND-50] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Tofu and tofu skin | Mostly artisanal; a few industrial makers (ToFu House, capacity 150 to 500 t/month); craft villages for *tàu hũ ky* (dried tofu skin) | Soybeans (mostly imported) | [@IND-51; @FORM-14] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Industrial *đồ chay* | Meat and frozen-food processors (Vissan, CJ Foods Vietnam with Cau Tre and bibigo, SG Food, Cholimex), specialists (An Nhien, Au Lac, Thoai An, LC Foods), retailer private labels | Mung bean, vegetables and starch; or imported textured vegetable protein (TVP), soy concentrate and wheat gluten | [@ECO-30; @ECO-33; @FORM-01] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Plant milks and yoghurts | Vinamilk (11 plant-milk products, several blended with cow's milk), TH true NUT (contains fresh milk), Nutifood, Vinasoy's Veyo; imports such as 137 Degrees (Thailand) and Oatside | Imported soybeans, oats, nuts; imported pea protein in high-protein lines | [@ECO-43; @ECO-45; @FORM-01] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Microalgae | Small spirulina producers selling health supplements | Spirulina | [@ECO-52; @ECO-53] {VN-direct\|Low} |

This incumbent economy works under existing food law without difficulty ([[ch07-rules]]). It is also the natural first customer for any new protein ingredient made in Vietnam.

## 2.2 What the shelf is made of: the September 2026 retail audit

AltProtein Vietnam logged 186 products (stock-keeping units, or SKUs) in 11 modern and premium stores. The stores were in Nha Trang (6 September 2026) and Ho Chi Minh City (16 and 20 September 2026). We recorded ingredient lists for 120 products and protein values for 78 [@FORM-01]. It is a convenience sample, not a market survey: it left out wet markets, dedicated chay shops, online-only brands and food service. Full method and tables are in [[app-s2-retail-audit]].

**Most of the shelf is not a protein product.** The two largest groups are plant milks (27% of SKUs) and filled wrappers such as spring rolls, dumplings and buns (25%). We looked at 43 unique ready-to-eat or ready-to-cook foods with a label value. **Their median protein is 4.7 g per 100 g; 53% have under 5 g and only 16% reach 10 g** [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.

**Two tiers, little in between.**
- *Tier 1:* starch-and-vegetable filled wrappers with mung bean filling, at 1.5 to 5.7 g protein per 100 g (median 4.0 g). CJ's current labels for *há cảo chay* (vegetarian dumplings) and *chả giò rế chay* (vegetarian net spring rolls) list no soy protein among the ingredients. The allergen line still declares wheat (gluten) and soy. Mung bean is the main protein ingredient [@FORM-22; @FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.
- *Tier 2:* gluten- or TVP-based analogues at 12 to 25 g per 100 g hydrated (median 19 g), and dried TVP or gluten slices at 53 to 67 g as sold. For three analogues, we inferred the base from the protein level [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.

{{chart:chart-retail-protein-archetypes}}

**High protein means imported protein.** All 10 locally made products with 10 g or more protein per 100 g use soy protein, TVP, soy concentrate or wheat gluten (for three, almost certainly). Local foods with no imported protein ingredient (8 products) top out at 4.6 g [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}. We could identify the main protein ingredient for 170 SKUs. **Of these, 77% rely on an imported protein ingredient and a further 11% partly do** (soybeans count as imported, since about 98% are) [@FORM-01; @FORM-09] {VN-direct|Medium}.

**China is a major supplier of the concentrated ingredients.** In 2023 China reported these exports to Vietnam, at about USD 1.6 to 1.7 per kg [@FORM-05; @FORM-06; @FORM-07] {VN-direct|High}:
- about 7,957 t of protein concentrates and textured protein (harmonised system, or HS, code 2106.10);
- 10,028 t of protein substances including soy isolate (HS 3504);
- 10,338 t of wheat gluten.

By 2025 textured protein shipped at USD 1.24 per kg free on board (FOB, the price at the exporter's port). It landed at about USD 1.29 to 1.38 per kg duty-free (our calculation, assuming freight of 3% to 8% and port costs of USD 15 to 40 per t; [[ch09-economics]]) [@TIC-12; @BUY-02; @FTR-32] {VN-direct|Medium}. The trade database estimates Vietnam-reported quantities for these codes, so they should not be used. Values are more reliable (for example USD 31.1 million of wheat gluten imported in 2023) [@FORM-03] {VN-direct|High}. These codes also supply meat processors (for sausages and *giò chả*, Vietnamese pork roll), so they do not measure the alternative-protein market.

**No domestic TVP.** We found no commercial TVP extrusion in Vietnam, and the country's soybean crushing is feed-grade only ([[ch05-industrial-base]]) [@FORM-10; @FORM-11; @FORM-35] {VN-direct|Medium}.

**Price per gram of protein.** Protein is cheapest in the least processed formats. Prices per 100 g of protein were [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium}:
- tofu: median about VND 66,000; dried TVP slices: median about VND 65,000;
- hydrated gluten or TVP analogues: median about VND 112,000;
- filled wrappers: median about VND 369,000;
- the only imported "modern" analogues on shelf (an Italian soy burger and sausage): VND 542,000 to 622,000, about five times local gluten or TVP analogues.

For comparison, eggs and chicken breast retail at about VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein [@COST-40; @COST-41] {VN-direct|Medium}.

{{chart:chart-retail-price-per-protein}}

**Chay versus meat.** In the one high-confidence same-brand pair, Vissan's *chả giò rế chay* cost 38% less per 100 g than Vissan's meat version [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium}. Other pairs rest on low-confidence price reads. Most chay wrappers hold 3 to 5 g of protein, so a cheaper pack is not cheaper protein. The audit did not capture protein values for the meat versions, so we cannot yet compare price per gram of protein.

**Other supply-side observations.**
- "Chay" is not the same as vegan: five SKU rows contain or may contain egg or dairy [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.
- Korean-owned CJ Foods Vietnam accounts for 20 of 186 rows. Imported finished products are concentrated in premium stores, while mass retailers carry almost only Vietnamese-made products [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.
- No label showed high-moisture extrusion, mycoprotein, precision-fermented or cultivated ingredients. Fermentation appears only in plant yoghurts and cultured cashew cheeses, apart from one imported nutritional-yeast SKU and yeast extract used as flavouring [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.

**What this means for supply.** The largest gap visible on Vietnamese shelves is a domestic protein ingredient. That means textured soy or soy-gluten granules and slices, and a protein that can lift the 4 g-protein wrapper category. The natural first customers are named in [[ch26-plays]] and [[app-s2-retail-audit]].

## 2.3 Modern plant-based meat: nearly absent

- The Mintel product-launch database logged **no plant-based meat launches in Vietnam from 2022 to the second quarter of 2023**, as reported by the Good Food Institute's Asia Pacific office (GFI APAC) [@ECO-23] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- GFI's global company database lists only two Vietnamese companies, both plant-based, founded in 2019 and 2023 [@VCO-21] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- Imported brands arrived through restaurants and gourmet shops, not mass retail. Examples are Beyond Burger at BiaCraft in Ho Chi Minh City in August 2019, OmniFoods on a Starbucks Vietnam menu in summer 2023, and Next Meats, Heura and others through gourmet importers [@ECO-15; @ECO-16; @ECO-20] {VN-direct|Medium}. No brand announced a formal national entry, so there were no formal exits either.
- The only products labelled "Plant Based" in mass retail were Chinese canned soy luncheon meats. They cost about three times as much per 100 g as Vissan's Vietnamese canned chay beef [@FORM-01; @ECO-40] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- Domestic startups are few and small: Emmay (Ho Chi Minh City, mushroom and mycelium products, about 1.5 to 2 t a month in 2023), Kashew (cultured cashew cheese, 2020), Ong Tempeh, and a handful of others with no confirmed current scale [@ECO-12; @ECO-13; @ECO-49; @ECO-51] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- Published market-size figures for Vietnamese plant-based meat ("about VND 1,000 billion", "USD 500 million within several years") give no traceable method and should not be used [@ECO-17; @ECO-18; @ECO-19] {VN-direct|Low}.

## 2.4 Fermentation, algae, duckweed and cultivated meat: almost nothing yet

- **Fermentation for food.** We found no Vietnamese company making koji protein, mycoprotein or precision-fermented ingredients. Tempeh (Ong Tempeh, which also sells *Rhizopus* starter) is the only commercially made fungal-fermented protein food we could verify [@ECO-51] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Microalgae and duckweed.** Spirulina is small and sold as a supplement. One source claims more than 70% of spirulina-derived products sold in Vietnam are imported (2023) [@ECO-53] {VN-direct|Low}. We found no commercial duckweed producer [@ECO-57] {VN-direct|Low}.
- **Cultivated meat and seafood.** Vietnam has no cultivated-meat company or facility. The links are corporate and point outwards:
  - Vinh Hoan (the pangasius exporter) holds a minority stake in Avant Meats through Vinh Technology, and joined Shiok Meats' 2021 round [@ECO-58; @ECO-59; @ECO-60] {VN-direct|Medium}.
  - Minh Phu (shrimp) signed a memorandum with Shiok Meats in July 2022 for a joint research centre in Vietnam. We found no sign it went further. UMAMI Bioworks merged with Shiok in 2024 and took over its assets, and UMAMI's partner list names no Vietnamese firm [@ECO-60; @ECO-61; @ECO-63; @VCO-22; @VCO-23] {VN-direct|Medium}.
  - VinaCapital Ventures invested in Korea's INNOHAS (plant-based meat) in July 2024 to help it enter Vietnam. INNOHAS shows no Vietnam activity two years later [@ECO-65; @VCO-31] {VN-direct|Low}.

## 2.5 Feed: context, not the subject of this study

Novel protein is already sold into Vietnamese animal feed, but this study treats feed only as context. Entobel, held in Singapore, makes black soldier fly larvae meal in Vietnam. It has run a 1,000 t a year plant in Dong Nai since 2019. A 10,000 t a year plant in Ho Chi Minh City (former Ba Ria-Vung Tau) opened on 23 November 2023 [@ECO-01; @ECO-02; @ECO-03; @ECO-06; @VCO-18] {VN-direct|High}. The pangasius exporter Vinh Hoan agreed in February 2024 to buy at least 15,000 t of Entobel insect protein over 2025 to 2027 (about 5,000 t a year) [@ECO-05] {VN-direct|High}. Chinese microbial protein, a by-product of glutamic-acid (monosodium glutamate, MSG) fermentation, is imported and sold for pig, poultry, fish and shrimp feed [@ECO-69; @FM-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. The full record of feed actors is in [[app-s1-directory]] and [[app-s6-feed-market]], and [[ch08-capital]] covers how the Entobel deal was financed.

## 2.6 The support ecosystem is thin

GFI APAC includes Vietnam in regional consumer surveys but has no Vietnam-specific programme [@ECO-24; @ECO-73] {VN-direct|Medium}. GFI's database lists no Vietnamese research actors [@VCO-21] {VN-direct|Medium}. The support we found for founders was generic, for example women-entrepreneur programmes that backed Emmay [@ECO-13; @ECO-14] {VN-direct|Medium}. We did not check the activity of non-governmental organisations, vegetarian associations or plant-based events in Vietnam. This is a gap, not a finding of absence (see [[ch30-unknowns]]). The demand research found the organised vegan movement small and mainly online, and no national vegetarian association with a public membership figure ([[ch12-chay-baseline]]).

## 2.7 Reading the market

1. **The protein gap is inside the existing categories.** The biggest near-term supply opportunity in food is not a new category. It is more protein, and more local protein, in existing ones: textured protein for specialist chay makers, and cheap protein to lift 4 g wrappers.
2. **Incumbents are the customers and the co-packers.** An Nhien, Au Lac, Thoai An, LC Foods, Cholimex, CJ Foods Vietnam, Vissan and private-label makers already buy imported protein ingredients. Vinamilk and Vinasoy buy imported pea protein and soybeans.
3. **No one has tested the food rules.** Novel protein in food has no Vietnamese producer to test the rules ([[ch07-rules]]).
4. **Price anchors are low.** Chay sells at or below meat prices. Tofu and dried TVP give protein at about 1.5 to 2 times the price of egg and chicken protein. A new ingredient has to fit that structure.

**For each reader.**
- **Investors:** there is almost no plant-based venture deal flow; the investable scale is in ingredients. A "protein upgrade for đồ chay" thesis sells to incumbents, as suppliers or buyers of the business.
- **Startups:** you can name your first customers today. Start there, not with a consumer brand.
- **Manufacturers:** a domestic TVP or soy-gluten supplier would reduce dependence on a single import origin. Clearer protein labelling is a competitive opening.
- **Researchers:** measured (not label) protein and amino-acid data for common chay products are missing and cheap to produce.
- **Policy makers and international bodies:** a census of Vietnamese companies and a chay nutrition audit are low-cost public goods.
