# 2. The alternative-protein economy today

**In brief.**
- Vietnam already eats and makes a lot of non-animal protein, but almost none of it comes from the technologies the global sector talks about.
- The real economy is soy milk, tofu and factory-made *đồ chay* (traditional vegetarian food).
- Our 2026 audit of 186 products found that most of the shelf is low in protein. Every local product with 10 g or more of protein per 100 g runs on imported soy or wheat gluten.
- Modern plant-based meat is nearly absent, and no Vietnamese company makes fermented protein ingredients for food.

---

## 2.1 The incumbent non-animal protein economy

Soy milk, tofu and *đồ chay* make up most of Vietnam's non-animal protein economy.

| Segment | Scale and leaders | Main protein input | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Soy milk | Vinasoy: about 80% market share and VND 4,100 billion of revenue in 2024 (self-reported) | Whole soybeans (imported) | [@ECO-41; @IND-48; @IND-50] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Tofu and tofu skin | Mostly small workshops and craft villages; a few factories | Soybeans (mostly imported) | [@IND-51; @FORM-14] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Factory-made *đồ chay* | Meat and frozen-food makers (Vissan, CJ Foods Vietnam, SG Food, Cholimex), specialists (An Nhien, Au Lac, Thoai An, LC Foods) and store brands | Mung bean, vegetables and starch; or imported textured soy and wheat gluten | [@ECO-30; @ECO-33; @FORM-01] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Plant milks and yoghurts | Vinamilk, TH true NUT, Nutifood, Vinasoy; some imports | Imported soybeans, oats, nuts and pea protein | [@ECO-43; @ECO-45; @FORM-01] {VN-direct\|Medium} |
| Microalgae | Small spirulina makers selling supplements | Spirulina | [@ECO-52; @ECO-53] {VN-direct\|Low} |

This economy works under existing food law without difficulty ([[ch07-rules]]). It is also the natural first customer for any new protein ingredient made in Vietnam.

## 2.2 What the shelf is made of: the September 2026 retail audit

In September 2026 we logged 186 products in 11 modern and premium stores in Nha Trang and Ho Chi Minh City [@FORM-01]. It is a convenience sample, not a market survey. It left out wet markets, chay shops, online brands and food service.

**Most of the shelf is not a protein product.** The two largest groups are plant milks (27% of products) and filled wrappers such as spring rolls and dumplings (25%). Among 43 foods with a protein value on the label, **the median was 4.7 g per 100 g. Only 16% reached 10 g** [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.

**Two tiers, little in between.**
- *Tier 1:* filled wrappers with mung bean filling, at 1.5 to 5.7 g of protein per 100 g [@FORM-22; @FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.
- *Tier 2:* meat copies based on gluten or textured soy, at 12 to 25 g per 100 g; dried slices reach 53 to 67 g [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.

{{chart:chart-retail-protein-archetypes}}

**High protein means imported protein.** All 10 locally made products with 10 g or more of protein per 100 g use imported soy protein, textured soy or wheat gluten. Local foods with no imported protein ingredient top out at 4.6 g. Overall, 77% of products rely on an imported protein ingredient [@FORM-01; @FORM-09] {VN-direct|Medium}.

**China is a major supplier.** In 2023 China reported exporting to Vietnam about 8,000 t of protein concentrates and textured protein, 10,000 t of soy isolate and similar proteins, and 10,300 t of wheat gluten [@FORM-05; @FORM-06; @FORM-07] {VN-direct|High}. By 2025 Chinese textured protein landed at about USD 1.29 to 1.38 per kg, duty-free (our calculation; [[ch09-economics]]) [@TIC-12; @BUY-02; @FTR-32] {VN-direct|Medium}. These ingredients also go into sausages and *giò chả* (Vietnamese pork roll), so trade figures overstate the alternative-protein market.

**No domestic textured soy.** We found no commercial extrusion of textured soy in Vietnam, and soybean crushing is feed-grade only ([[ch05-industrial-base]]) [@FORM-10; @FORM-11; @FORM-35] {VN-direct|Medium}.

**Price per gram of protein.** Protein is cheapest in the least processed products. Median prices per 100 g of protein were [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium}:
- tofu and dried textured soy: about VND 65,000;
- hydrated gluten or soy meat copies: about VND 112,000;
- filled wrappers: about VND 369,000;
- imported "modern" meat copies: VND 542,000 to 622,000.

Eggs and chicken breast cost about VND 34,000 to 45,000 per 100 g of protein [@COST-40; @COST-41] {VN-direct|Medium}.

{{chart:chart-retail-price-per-protein}}

**Chay versus meat.** In one same-brand pair, Vissan's chay spring rolls cost 38% less per 100 g than its meat version [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|Medium}. But most chay wrappers hold only 3 to 5 g of protein, so a cheaper pack is not cheaper protein.

**Other findings.** "Chay" is not the same as vegan: some products contain egg or dairy. Imported products sit mainly in premium stores. No label showed fungal, precision-fermented or cultivated protein [@FORM-01] {VN-direct|High}.

**What this means for supply.** The largest gap on the shelf is a domestic protein ingredient. That means textured soy for meat copies, and a protein that can lift the 4 g wrapper category ([[ch26-plays]]).

## 2.3 Modern plant-based meat: nearly absent

- A product-launch database logged **no plant-based meat launches in Vietnam from 2022 to mid-2023** [@ECO-23] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- Imported brands such as Beyond Burger and OmniFoods arrived through restaurants and gourmet shops, not mass retail [@ECO-15; @ECO-16; @ECO-20] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- The only products labelled "Plant Based" in mass retail were Chinese canned soy luncheon meats, at three times the price of Vietnamese canned chay [@FORM-01; @ECO-40] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- Domestic startups are few and small: Emmay (mushroom and mycelium products), Kashew (cashew cheese) and Ong Tempeh, among others [@ECO-12; @ECO-13; @ECO-49; @ECO-51] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- Published market sizes for Vietnamese plant-based meat give no traceable method, so we do not use them [@ECO-17; @ECO-18; @ECO-19] {VN-direct|Low}.

## 2.4 Fermentation, algae, duckweed and cultivated meat: almost nothing yet

- **Fermentation for food.** We found no Vietnamese company making koji protein, fungal protein or precision-fermented ingredients. Tempeh is the only commercial fungal-fermented protein food we could verify [@ECO-51] {VN-direct|Medium}.
- **Microalgae and duckweed.** Spirulina is small and sold as a supplement [@ECO-53] {VN-direct|Low}. We found no commercial duckweed producer [@ECO-57] {VN-direct|Low}.
- **Cultivated meat and seafood.** Vietnam has no cultivated-meat company or facility. The links point outwards. Vinh Hoan, the pangasius exporter, holds a minority stake in Avant Meats [@ECO-58; @ECO-59; @ECO-60] {VN-direct|Medium}. Minh Phu, the shrimp firm, signed a research memorandum with Shiok Meats in 2022, but we found no sign it went further [@ECO-60; @ECO-61; @ECO-63; @VCO-22; @VCO-23] {VN-direct|Medium}.

## 2.5 Feed: context, not the subject of this study

Novel protein is already sold into Vietnamese animal feed. Chinese microbial protein, a by-product of making monosodium glutamate (MSG), is imported for pig, poultry, fish and shrimp feed [@ECO-69; @FM-12] {VN-direct|Medium}. This study treats feed only as context.

## 2.6 The support ecosystem is thin

The Good Food Institute (GFI), the main global advocacy group for alternative protein, has no Vietnam programme. Its database lists no Vietnamese research actors [@ECO-24; @ECO-73; @VCO-21] {VN-direct|Medium}. The founder support we found was generic [@ECO-13; @ECO-14] {VN-direct|Medium}. We did not check non-governmental groups or vegetarian associations, so this is a gap, not a finding ([[ch30-unknowns]], [[ch12-chay-baseline]]).

## 2.7 Reading the market

1. **The protein gap is inside existing categories.** The near-term opportunity is more protein, and more local protein, in what already sells: textured protein for chay makers, and cheap protein to lift 4 g wrappers.
2. **Incumbents are the customers and the co-packers.** Chay specialists, frozen-food makers and store brands already buy imported protein ingredients. Vinamilk and Vinasoy buy imported pea protein and soybeans.
3. **No one has tested the food rules.** No Vietnamese producer has yet put novel protein into food ([[ch07-rules]]).
4. **Price anchors are low.** Chay sells at or below meat prices. A new ingredient has to fit that structure.

**For each reader.**
- **Investors:** there is almost no plant-based venture deal flow. The investable scale is in ingredients sold to incumbents.
- **Startups:** you can name your first customers today. Start there, not with a consumer brand.
- **Manufacturers:** a domestic textured-soy supplier would reduce reliance on one import origin.
- **Researchers:** measured protein and amino-acid data for common chay products are missing and cheap to produce.
- **Policy makers and international bodies:** a census of companies and a chay nutrition audit are low-cost public goods.
