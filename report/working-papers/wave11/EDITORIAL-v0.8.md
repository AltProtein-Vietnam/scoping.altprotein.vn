# Version 0.8 editorial brief: protein for people, in clear language

Today is 2026-10-01. This brief sets the editorial decisions for version 0.8 of "Alternative protein in Vietnam: a supply-side scoping study". Every editor follows it. Where it conflicts with older wording in a page, this brief wins. `CLAUDE.md` and `STYLE.md` still apply in full (tokens, tags, citations, no dashes, British spelling, province and ministry names).

The owner asked for three things:

1. Test more food-security hypotheses (imported and subsidised meat, disease and pandemic risk, and others). This is research wave 11 (`working-papers/wave11/BRIEF.md`). **Page editors do not add wave 11 findings.** The consolidating editor folds them in afterwards, mainly in chapter 1 section 1.3, chapters 20 and 23, the summaries and the briefs.
2. Focus the study on protein for **human consumption**, now and in the future. Reduce and prune feed arguments, and stop treating fishmeal replacement and aquafeed or feed ingredients as a production output.
3. Make the text easier to understand, following the GOV.UK clear-language guidance (section 4 below).

## 1. The new scope (use this wording or close to it)

> This study is about protein for people: what Vietnam could make at home for people to eat, from what, at what cost, under which rules, and who would buy it, now and to 2050. Feed appears only as context. It is the hidden import behind the meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish that people eat, and so part of the food-security picture. But the study no longer treats feed ingredients (fishmeal replacements, or microbial, insect or duckweed feed) as something to make. Version 0.7 assessed them; that record stays in Appendices S6 and F6 and in the changelog (Appendix M5).

Short form for summaries: "Protein for people first; feed only as context."

Insect protein stays a benchmark and incumbent only. Diet change is still not a goal the study sets: Part III sizes what people and firms would buy; it does not tell people what to eat. Market-size forecasts stay out of scope.

## 2. What changes in substance

### 2.1 The case for alternative protein (chapter 1 section 1.6 and every summary)

Reorder and reword the arguments so they are about the protein people eat:

1. **Security of the protein people eat (strong, with limits).** Vietnam's meat, eggs, milk and farmed fish rest on imported feed (about 99% of the soy protein in feed and about 74% of maize supply imported, 2025), and disease shocks such as African swine fever hit pork supply and prices. Protein made in Vietnam for people, from domestic inputs where possible, adds a second source. Limit: at the scale the demand evidence supports (about 0.2% of meat protein displaced by 2035 on the benchmark path), it does not change national exposure on its own. (The consolidating editor will add wave 11 evidence on imported meat, disease and other exposures here.)
2. **Industrial value added (strong, but conditional).** Domestic textured protein to replace imports from China; turning exported cassava starch into fermentation sugar for food fermentation at home.
3. **Environment (real, but narrower than often claimed).** Unchanged in substance.
4. **Animal welfare (a motivation for some actors).** Unchanged in substance; remove the clause about displacing forage fish in feed.

Keep the chapter 22 finding that the food route saves the most imports per tonne: replacing 1 t of meat protein in diets avoids about 4.5 t of soybean-meal and 9.5 t of maize imports (our calculation). That is now a central reason why protein for people matters for security.

## 4. Clear language (GOV.UK standard, adapted)

Source: GOV.UK "Writing to GOV.UK standards: clear language" and the A to Z style guide (accessed 2026-10-01). The study is read by specialists, but research shows specialists also prefer plain English, and the text must survive machine translation into Vietnamese.

1. **Short sentences.** Aim for 15 to 20 words. Split any sentence over 25 words. One idea per sentence.
2. **Short paragraphs.** At most five sentences. Use bullet lists for three or more parallel items.
3. **Front-load.** Put the main point first: in each section, paragraph and bullet. Headings should say what the section finds where possible ("Feed is context, not a target"), not only its topic.
4. **Active voice.** "We found no ...", "The ministry issued ...", not "No ... was found", "... was issued by ...". Passive is fine when the actor does not matter.
5. **Plain words.** buy (not purchase), about (not approximately), use (not utilise or leverage), help (not assist or facilitate), start (not initiate or commence), enough (not sufficient), need (not require), show (not demonstrate), so (not therefore, at the start of a clause), before or after (not prior to or subsequent to), about or on (not regarding or in relation to), now (not currently or at present), many or most (not the majority of).
6. **Words to avoid (GOV.UK list):** agenda, advance (as improve), collaborate, combat, commit or pledge, counter, deliver (use make, give, provide, supply), deploy, dialogue, empower, facilitate, focus (use work on, concentrate on), foster, impact (as a verb), incentivise, initiate, key (as important), land (as achieve), leverage (unless financial), liaise, overarching, progress (as a verb), promote, robust, slim down, streamline, strengthen (as a vague verb), tackle, transform, utilise, drive (as cause), going or moving forward, in order to, one-stop shop, ring-fence. "Hub" stays only inside defined names (play T4, candidate hubs in Appendix F5); explain it on first use as a cluster of plants on one site.
7. **Avoid nominalisations.** "We assessed" not "an assessment was carried out"; "decide" not "make a decision"; "use" not "the use of".
8. **Explain abbreviations and codes on first use on each page**, then use the short form: "Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE)", "textured vegetable protein (TVP)", "harmonised system (HS) code 3504". The ones readers know (EU, USD, UK, US, GDP, VAT) need no explanation. Avoid an abbreviation used only once or twice: write the words.
9. **Specialist terms:** explain in plain English on first use (for example "titer, the concentration of product in the fermenter"; "DIAAS, a measure of protein quality"; "tolling, renting time on someone else's fermenter"). Link to the glossary (`[[app-r3-glossary]]`) where helpful.
10. **No negative contractions** (do not use "can't", "don't"); the report uses no contractions at all.
11. **Must and need:** use "must" only for legal requirements; use "need" or "has to" otherwise.
12. **Numbers:** keep them, with units and year. Do not stack many numbers in one sentence: move detail to a table or the appendix.
13. **Summary boxes:** chapter openings labelled `**In one paragraph.**` become `**In brief.**` followed by either one paragraph of at most five sentences, or three to six short bullets. Keep the main numbers and findings; move detail into the body if it is already there, or cut it if the body already says it.
14. **Tables:** keep tables; shorten cell text where it is a long sentence. Do not change the number of columns in a row (the validator checks it). Escape the tag pipe inside tables (`{VN-direct\|High}`).
15. **Tone:** neutral, no hype, no exclamation, no rhetorical questions in headings unless they are the chapter's question.

## 6. Shared headline wording (use these so pages agree)

Numbers below come from `data/demand_outputs.csv`, `data/balance_outputs.csv` and the existing chapters; they are model outputs (`{VN-direct|Low} {fx:estimate}` in futures text). Use whichever sentences a page needs; adapt the wording, keep the numbers.

- **Scope line:** "Protein for people first; feed only as context."
- **Why Vietnam (one paragraph):** Vietnamese people eat more meat each year: meat intake was 136.4 g per person per day in 2020, against a recommendation of 50 to 80 g, and animal foods supply 42% of protein. Most of the meat, eggs and farmed fish people eat is produced in Vietnam (about 90% of meat on FAO food balance sheets, 2023), on imported feed; most milk is imported (about 30% self-sufficiency, 2023): about 99% of the soy protein in feed and about 74% of the maize supply were imported in 2025, for a feed-ingredient import bill of about USD 10 billion. Disease shocks such as African swine fever hit pork supply and prices. The case for making more protein for people at home is the security of that supply, industrial value and trade; environment and animal welfare are motivations for some actors.
- **What it could make first:** domestic textured plant protein to replace imports from China (T1); a cassava-starch-to-fermentation-sugar hub so a food-fermentation step stays in Vietnam (T4); and shared pilot and tolling fermentation, which most fermentation plays need first (T5). Koji and fungal foods (T9) and mung bean and rice protein for plant drinks (T10) are worth proving. Precision fermentation fits only as contract-made, high-value ingredients (T7); cultivated seafood is research for this decade (T8).
- **Who would buy it:** unchanged from version 0.7 (firms, not shoppers; about 19,000 t of protein a year by 2035 on the benchmark path, of which about 11,000 to 17,000 t made in Vietnam; about 0.2% of meat protein displaced).
- **What to do first (policy):** add a new-food clause to the Food Safety Law between the October 2026 first reading and the vote considered in May 2027; clarify the GMO status of purified precision-fermented proteins; write a proportionate novel-food procedure into the law's implementing decree; share the expensive parts (a food-grade pilot fermentation line and a protein-quality laboratory).
- **What to do first (firms):** get delivered quotes for food-grade soy flour, win conditional orders from chay makers, and test extenders against the soy recipe buyers already use.
- **By 2050 (trend):** on trend, the meat protein Vietnamese people eat rises from about 1.01 Mt in 2025 to about 1.45 Mt in 2050 (our model; about 27 to 36 g per person per day on a supply basis), and the imported soybean meal behind it from 7.2 Mt to about 10.4 Mt. Replacing 1 t of meat protein in diets avoids about 4.5 t of soybean-meal and 9.5 t of maize imports (our calculation), so protein for people is the route that saves the most imports per tonne. At the scale demand supports (benchmark path), new protein displaces about 0.4% of meat protein in 2050; it adds a second source rather than replacing meat.
- **Seven no-regret moves:** a national protein balance; a new-food route with deadlines; shared food-grade pilot capacity; a protein-quality laboratory and price series; residue-carbon rules and a by-product atlas; climate-proof siting; and process-engineering skills.
- **The vision (chapter 24, `{fx:vision}`):** By 2050 Vietnam feeds a larger, richer and older population from a protein supply that is measured, more diverse and more secure. It publishes a yearly protein balance of what people eat and where it comes from. Domestic makers supply most of the plant-protein ingredients that food makers now import. New routes (upgraded chay, declared blends, canteen dishes, plant drinks and fermented foods) deliver about 67,000 to 172,000 t of protein a year, of which about 59,000 to 147,000 t is made in Vietnam, much of it on Vietnamese carbohydrate and side streams (the benchmark and stretch paths of the demand model). Vietnam has a working new-food route, open food-grade pilot capacity and a protein-quality laboratory, and supplies fermentation sugar and contract fermentation for food to the region. Meat stays the main protein; new protein adds a second source rather than replacing it. The vision is reviewed at every five-year plan.
- **Scenario axis:** "stress on imported protein" (the feed behind domestic meat, and imported protein foods), crossed with whether Asia's fermentation market stays open or falls under Chinese price dominance. World names unchanged: A Regional workshop, B Comfortable price-taker, C Security build-out, D Squeezed importer.
