# Other food-security exposures for the protein people eat, and whether alternative protein for food reduces them

Line: L3, Other food-security exposures, and whether alternative protein for food reduces them (wave 11). Source prefix: SEC. Date: 2026-10-01.

## Verdict

The counter-hypothesis largely holds. Alternative protein for human food made from imported soy, pea, gluten or mung bean moves Vietnam's import dependence (from the Americas to China or Myanmar) and does not reduce it by any amount that matters: at the study's benchmark scale the net saving of imported soy protein is at most about 0.2% of what Vietnam feeds, and only routes on domestic inputs (fermentation on cassava or sugar with domestic urea; rice, peanut, seaweed or duckweed protein) reduce dependence at all. Of the other exposures tested, wild-capture fish is the protein food under most pressure (stock down 22% since 2000 to 2005, a policy cut of about 27% by 2030, climate a further 3 to 12% of catch potential by 2050), and alternative protein for food does not touch it at the study's scale. Consumer price evidence shows the largest protein-food shock since 2008 came from domestic disease (pork up 57% in 2020), while the 2022 imported-feed shock barely reached consumer meat prices (2026 is not yet clear); imported feed is mainly a producer and industry exposure. Vietnam's official food-security frame (Resolution 34/NQ-CP to 2030) is production volumes, rice first, more animal food, and has no import, protein or alternative-protein indicator. Yield class: **added a constraint** (the security case must be restricted to domestic-input routes) and **changed a conclusion** in part (who the import exposure protects, and how strongly ch01 can claim security for alternative protein as such).

## Headline findings

1. **Only domestic-input routes reduce dependence.** Of 12 food routes classified, tofu and soy milk, imported and locally made textured soy, gluten, pea and mung bean products rest on imported protein; fermentation on cassava or sugar, rice, peanut, seaweed and duckweed protein rest on domestic inputs, with imported equipment and, for precision fermentation, imported strains and patents [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-05; SEC-14; TIC-12; FS-24; FS-01].
2. **The net soy saving is tiny even when it is positive.** 1 kg of pork or poultry protein embodies about 1.9 to 3.4 kg of imported soybean-meal protein (our calculation from the balance model's calibration). At the 2,350 t of meat protein displaced in 2035 (D-BENCH), a soy-based substitute saves minus 0.2 to 5.6 kt of soy protein and a domestic-input substitute 4.5 to 8.0 kt, at most 0.24% of the 3,312 kt of soybean-meal protein fed in 2025 [VN-direct, Low, estimate 2035].
3. **Food plant-protein imports are as concentrated as feed imports, on a different origin.** In 2023 every protein-relevant import line had a top-three origin share of 73 to 95% (HHI 2,549 to 4,053); China supplied about 84% of the 2025 tonnage of imported soy isolate, textured protein and gluten, and Myanmar 60% of dried pulses by value (2023) [VN-direct, High, SEC-05; BUY-02].
4. **About 8% of the protein Vietnamese people eat is already imported plant protein.** Soy foods (4.94 g) and wheat (3.27 g) supplied 8.2 of 102.2 g of protein per person per day in 2023, about 305 kt of protein a year; the incumbent alternative protein (tofu, soy milk) is part of the Americas soy dependence [VN-direct, High, DIE-07; MAC-09].
5. **Wild fish is under more pressure from fishing and policy than from climate to 2050.** Marine standing stock was about 3.95 Mt in 2016 to 2020, down 22.1% from 2000 to 2005, while capture rose 17% from 2016 to 2024; policy cuts capture about 27% by 2030; FAO projects a 2.8 to 11.7% fall in catch potential by 2050 (two models, RCP2.6 and RCP8.5) [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-06; NTS-08; SEC-07, projection 2050].
6. **Consumers felt disease more than imported feed.** Pork prices rose 57.23% in 2020 (1.94 points of a 3.23% CPI rise); in 2022, the world feed-price year, foods rose 1.62% and pork fell 10.68%. 2026 is mixed: food and food services rose 4.76% (January to August) with no item breakdown found [VN-direct, High, SEC-08; SEC-09; SEC-17].
7. **Official food security has no import or protein indicator.** Resolution 34/NQ-CP (2021) sets 2030 volume targets (paddy at least 35 Mt, carcass meat 6.0 to 6.5 Mt, fresh milk 2.6 Mt, 23 billion eggs, aquatic 9 to 10 Mt), asks for more meat, milk, eggs and fish in diets, and treats imports only as a "flexible" management tool. Milk is furthest from target (1.37 Mt planned for 2026, about 53%) [VN-direct, High, SEC-01; SEC-02].
8. **MAE now names import dependence, for livestock inputs.** MAE's 2026 plan asks to "step by step reduce import dependence by developing domestic raw-material areas", the closest official hook for a protein-security indicator [VN-direct, High, SEC-02].
9. **China writes microbial protein into food security, mainly for feed and on domestic carbon.** The 2024 "big food view" opinions seek "calories and protein from plants, animals and microbes", promote microbial cell protein for feed, industrialise new foods "steadily and cautiously" and push sugar from non-grain biomass; the 2026 No. 1 document keeps biomanufacturing and drops "new food resources" [general, High, SEC-04; SEC-03].
10. **Livestock heat risk is becoming power risk.** The livestock department told large farms in June 2026 to secure power and water against heat; fermentation plants share the same dependence on power and cooling [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-11].

## Detailed findings

### 1. Vietnam's official food-security framing

**The governing text.** Resolution 34/NQ-CP of 25 March 2021, "on ensuring national food security to 2030", implements Politburo Conclusion 81-KL/TW (29 July 2020). We read the signed PDF from the government portal (pages 1 to 8 of 11, as page images; pages 9 to 11 assign tasks to ministries and were not read in full) [VN-direct, High, SEC-01].

- **Definition.** Food security is written as *an ninh lương thực* "including staple food and other food" (*bao gồm lương thực, thực phẩm*). The overall objective is "enough staple food and food for domestic consumption in every situation, and a part for export" [VN-direct, High, SEC-01].
- **Threats named.** Climate change, natural disasters, environmental pollution and "increasingly severe, unpredictable transboundary epidemics" (*dịch bệnh xuyên biên giới*), plus industrialisation and urbanisation. Imports, trade disruption and feed are not named as threats [VN-direct, High, SEC-01].
- **2030 targets are production volumes.** 3.5 million ha of rice land; at least 35 Mt of paddy a year; vegetables and beans (*rau đậu*) on 1.2 to 1.3 million ha, 23 to 24 Mt; fruit 16 to 17 Mt; carcass meat 6.0 to 6.5 Mt; fresh milk 2.6 Mt; 23 billion eggs; aquatic products 9 to 10 Mt [VN-direct, High, SEC-01, projection 2030 (official target)].
- **Nutrition targets push towards more animal food.** The resolution aims to "reduce the share of rice and increase the use of meat, milk, eggs, fish, vegetables and fruit", raise energy intake above 2,500 kcal per person per day, cut under-5 stunting below 19% and underweight below 10.5% [VN-direct, High, SEC-01, projection 2030 (official target)].
- **Diversification is about products and nutrition, not sources.** Viewpoint 5 prioritises science and technology "to diversify staple food and food products, ensuring balanced nutrition and food safety" (*đa dạng hóa sản phẩm lương thực, thực phẩm*). Aquaculture is to be diversified by species and method; coastal capture is to be reduced and offshore capture developed "sustainably" [VN-direct, High, SEC-01].
- **Imports appear only as a management tool.** The resolution asks for a "flexible" export and import management mechanism "in line with the market", combined with circulation and national reserves for emergency relief, and for international cooperation on trade and standards. It sets no import, feed, soybean, maize, protein or self-sufficiency-ratio indicator (we read all target and task paragraphs on pages 1 to 8) [VN-direct, High, SEC-01].

**Progress against the protein-food targets (our calculation).** Aquatic output was already 9.95 Mt in 2025, inside the 9 to 10 Mt band [MAC-15]. Eggs reached 21.4 billion in 2025 against 23 billion [MAC-14]. Fresh milk reached 1.3 Mt in 2025 [MAC-14], and MAE's 2026 plan aims for "over 1.37 Mt" [SEC-02], about 53% of the 2.6 Mt target for 2030 (1.37 / 2.6). Milk is the domestic protein food furthest from its food-security target, which makes dairy imports (line L1) the clearest food-side import exposure that official policy already recognises by implication [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-01; SEC-02; MAC-14].

**MAE's current framing (2025 to 2026).** MAE's report on 2025 and its 2026 plan (official letter 6034/BNNMT-KHTC, 26 August 2025) restates "firmly ensuring food security and nutrition security" (*an ninh lương thực, an ninh dinh dưỡng*) as part of the sector's general objective. In its livestock tasks it adds "step by step reduce import dependence by developing domestic raw-material areas", an operational form of the feed self-reliance language already in Resolution 19-NQ/TW and Decision 150 (F5.1.2), with credit and land incentives for firms that develop raw-material areas and factories. The 2026 plan targets 9.2 Mt of live-weight meat, about 24 Mt of industrial feed and fisheries output of 9.88 Mt (aquaculture 6.13 Mt, capture 3.75 Mt). It also asks for tighter quarantine of imported animals and animal products, "especially smuggled livestock, poultry and animal products" [VN-direct, High, SEC-02, projection 2026 (official plan)]. The same report gives first-half 2025 agro-forestry-fishery imports of USD 24.01 billion (up 15.1%), of which livestock products USD 2.11 billion (up 21.5%) and aquatic products USD 1.6 billion (up 33.4%), against exports of USD 33.84 billion; it prints the surplus as "USD 983 million", which does not match its own figures (the difference is USD 9.83 billion, our calculation). The livestock-product import growth is a lead for line L1 [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-02].

**Policy discourse.** A 2024 article in the Journal of State Management (*Tạp chí Quản lý nhà nước*) on food security in Vietnam defines food security partly as avoiding "dependence on imported food sources" (*phụ thuộc vào nguồn lương thực nhập khẩu*), lists climate change, shrinking staple-crop area, low rice incomes and changing diets as the main problems, and does not mention feed imports, soybeans, maize or protein (read through a summarising fetch tool) [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-16]. We found no mid-term review of Resolution 34 (one targeted search, 1 October 2026).

**Reading.** The official food-security frame is production volume, rice first, with animal-source food to be increased. Protein appears only through meat, milk, eggs and fish volumes. Import dependence appears in 2025 to 2026 as a livestock-input concern (raw-material areas), not as a food-security indicator. We found no official text that names alternative protein, plant protein, microbial protein or novel food as a food-security instrument (we searched Resolution 34 and MAE's 2026 plan; F5 already records the absence across 98 other targets). The study's proposed protein-security indicator (ch01 section 1.7: the share of the protein people eat made from domestic inputs) therefore has no hook in Resolution 34, but has one in MAE's "reduce import dependence" task [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-01; SEC-02].

### 2. Concentration of origins and routes

**Method.** We pulled Vietnam-reported imports for 2023 (the latest year Vietnam has reported to UN Comtrade; a 2024 query returned no records) for eight protein-relevant lines and computed origin shares and the Herfindahl-Hirschman index (HHI, the sum of squared percentage shares, 0 to 10,000; three equal origins give 3,333) (our calculation) [VN-direct, High, SEC-05]. Raw extract and results: `raw/ct_vn_imports_2023.json`, `raw/ct_vn_2023_concentration.csv`.

- **Feed inputs and food plant-protein inputs are similarly concentrated.** Every line has an HHI above 2,500 and a top-three share of 73 to 95%. Soybeans come from two countries (Brazil and the United States, 88.6% in 2023; 89.4% in 2025 on customs data [MAC-09]). Food plant-protein ingredients come mostly from one: on partner-reported 2025 tonnages, China shipped 53,976 t of the 64,248 t of HS 3504, 2106.10 and 1109 that reached Vietnam, about 84% (our calculation from [BUY-02] as tabulated in ch16 section 16.1) [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-05; MAC-09; BUY-02].
- **The two dependences sit on different routes and different politics.** Soy, meal and maize cross the Pacific or round the Cape from the Americas; wheat comes mainly from Australia; food protein ingredients come from China over a land border or a short sea route; mung bean and other pulses from Myanmar. Alternative protein for food made from imported ingredients would add weight to the China and Myanmar legs, not the Americas leg [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-05; GEO-40].
- **Recent route shocks hit exports more than protein imports.** The 2024 Red Sea crisis raised container rates from Vietnam to the US East Coast from USD 2,600 (December 2023) to USD 4,100 to 4,500 (January 2024) and nearly tripled rates to Hamburg; the article we read discusses delayed raw-material imports in general but not soy, maize or feed [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-10]. Vietnam's soy and maize do not normally pass Suez (F2.1.7 reading), so the Red Sea is a cost shock to Vietnamese exports, not a protein-supply shock (our reading) [general, Low, GEO-41]. The 2026 Middle East shock reached Vietnam through energy, fertiliser and freight (already in F2.1.7, SHK-013).
- **For L1.** We did not pull meat (HS 0201 to 0210) or dairy (HS 0401 to 0406) origins; L1 covers them. If L1 finds origins as concentrated as the lines above, the conclusion here extends to animal-protein food imports.

**Reading.** Concentration is a property of almost everything Vietnam imports for protein, food or feed. A switch from imported feed protein to imported food protein does not reduce concentration; it moves it from the Americas to China. This supports the counter-hypothesis for import-based routes.

### 3. Climate exposure of domestic animal and aquatic protein

What chapter 20 and F2.5 already establish is not repeated: heat-stress days, salinity area, subsidence, the 1.1 Mt of aquaculture output at annual flood risk, and pangasius flood exposure. This section adds wild fish and the mechanism of exposure.

**Wild fish is the protein food under the most pressure, and the pressure is mostly not climate.**

- Fish and seafood supplied 11.12 g of 102.2 g of protein per person per day in 2023 (10.9% of all protein and 25.7% of animal protein supply; FAOSTAT food balance sheets, supply not intake) (our calculation from `demand_protein_intake.csv`) [VN-direct, High, DIE-07].
- The standing stock of marine fish resources in Vietnamese waters was about 3.95 Mt in 2016 to 2020, 22.1% below 2000 to 2005 and 9.4% below 2011 to 2015 (NSO, citing the national stock assessments) [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-06]. Capture output still rose, from 3.26 Mt (2016) to 3.83 Mt (2024), 94% of it marine [VN-direct, High, SEC-06]. Catches rose while the stock fell.
- Policy will cut capture faster than climate: the fisheries strategy sets 2.8 Mt by 2030 against 3.83 Mt in 2025, a fall of about 27% in five years (our calculation) [NTS-08; MAC-15]. MAE's 2026 plan still sets 3.75 Mt [VN-direct, Medium, NTS-08; MAC-15; SEC-02, projection 2030 (official target)].
- Climate alone takes less, on the only Vietnam-specific projection we found. FAO Technical Paper 627 gives the change in maximum catch potential in Viet Nam's exclusive economic zone by 2050 (2046 to 2055) relative to 2000 (1996 to 2005): minus 6.8% (RCP2.6) and minus 11.7% (RCP8.5) in one model (DBEM), and minus 2.8% and minus 4.3% in another (a size-based food-web model). By 2100 under RCP8.5 the models diverge: minus 40.8% against minus 6.5% [general, High, SEC-07, projection 2050 and 2100 (published model results)]. Each figure is FAO's own average across the climate models that drove the run; the published ranges around them are wide (about 9 to 15 points at mid-century), so a small gain is inside the range for the food-web model under RCP2.6. We do not average the two fisheries models (see disagreements).
- FAO 627 also ranks Viet Nam the most vulnerable country for freshwater aquaculture and among the most vulnerable for brackish-water aquaculture, because of high production and exposure [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-07].

**Livestock heat is becoming a power-reliability exposure.** The Department of Livestock Production and Animal Health told farmers in June 2026 to secure electricity and water, "especially for large-scale farms", to cope with heat [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-11]. A press headline reports 700 finishing pigs dead at three farms in Luc Nam commune, Bac Ninh, after a sudden night-time power cut (headline and search snippet only; the page returned HTTP 403) [VN-direct, Low, SEC-12]. As production moves into closed, cooled houses (ch20), the climate risk for pork and poultry shifts from ambient heat to the reliability of power, which fermentation plants share (ch09 cooling penalty).

**Does alternative protein for food reduce these exposures?**

- *Wild fish:* not at the study's scale. The demand routes in ch18 displace meat and chay protein, not fish, and we found no Vietnamese evidence of demand for fish analogues. The capture cut is a policy decision; the gap it leaves will be filled by aquaculture (which needs imported feed protein) or imports (our reading) [VN-direct, Low].
- *Aquaculture and livestock climate risk:* no material difference. At about 0.2% of meat protein displaced in 2035 (ch18), herd and farm numbers do not change [VN-direct, Low, estimate 2035].
- *Heat and power:* shifts rather than removes. A fermentation plant in southern Vietnam needs chilled water and continuous power (ch09); a closed pig house needs ventilation power. Both fail in a long power cut. We found no data comparing their exposure per kg of protein.

**Reading.** The largest protein-food price shock for Vietnamese consumers since 2008 came from domestic animal disease (2020), not from imported inputs. The 2022 imported-feed shock barely reached consumer meat prices; it landed on farm margins, where the study already records smallholder exit (SHK-009). 2026 is not yet clear: farm-gate egg prices fell below cost while feed rose, but consumer food and food-service prices rose 4.76% in January to August, and we found no item breakdown that separates feed, energy, the 2025 ASF wave and food-service costs. So, on the evidence of 2020 and 2022, imported feed is mainly a **producer and industry** exposure (margins, farm exit, trade balance), while the **consumer** price exposure for protein food is mainly disease (line L2). This does not weaken the import case in ch01; it changes whom it protects [VN-direct, Medium, SEC-08; SEC-09; MAC-24; MAC-43].

**Export restrictions.** Already in the package: domestic inflation predicts export restrictions better than world prices [GEO-42], and Argentina raised meal export duties in 2022 [GEO-39]. New here: China, the main origin of Vietnam's food plant-protein ingredients and a large protein importer itself, lists "diversify agricultural imports" (*推进农产品进口多元化*) as a 2026 policy task [general, High, SEC-03]. We found no export restriction on soy protein, gluten or textured protein by China in the sources we read; the evidence gap is noted, not a finding of absence.

**Does alternative protein for food reduce price-shock exposure?** Not at the study's scale. A 57% pork price rise is a national supply event; the benchmark path delivers about 19,000 t of protein a year by 2035 (ch18), against about 25 g of meat protein per person per day (FBS 2023, about 0.93 Mt of meat protein a year at 101.6 million people, our calculation). In 2019 to 2020 consumers switched to chicken, not tofu (ch11). Imported-ingredient routes also import price risk: their main input price (soy flour, gluten, isolate) moves with the same soy complex [VN-direct, Low, estimate 2035].

### 5. The counter-hypothesis: does alternative protein for food only move the dependence?

**Hypothesis tested.** Alternative protein for human food made from imported soy, pea or wheat gluten, or with imported equipment, strains and sugar, only moves Vietnam's import dependence; it does not reduce it.

**5.1 The incumbent alternative protein is already import-based.** Soybeans eaten as food (tofu, soy milk and other soy foods) supplied 4.94 g of protein per person per day in 2023 and wheat 3.27 g, together 8.2 g or 8.0% of total protein supply (FAOSTAT food balance sheets; supply, not intake) [VN-direct, High, DIE-07]. Vietnam imports 98.4% of its soybean supply and all its wheat (ch01). At the 2025 population of 101.6 million, that is about 183 kt of soy protein and 121 kt of wheat protein a year, about 305 kt in all (our calculation). Tofu and soy milk, Vietnam's real non-animal protein economy (ch02), already rest on the Americas soy chain.

**5.3 Does a soy-based food substitute at least shrink the soy import per unit of protein eaten?** A little, sometimes. Using the balance model's own 2025 calibration (feed conversion, dressing yields, soybean-meal inclusion scaled to the 7.2 Mt fed), 1 kg of pork protein embodies about 1.9 to 2.4 kg of imported soybean-meal protein and 1 kg of poultry protein about 3.4 kg (our calculation, `sec_calc.py`; pig low end counts compound feed only). A soy food delivers 1 kg of protein from 1.0 to 2.0 kg of soy protein, depending on how much bean protein the process keeps (our assumption, no source) [VN-direct, Low, estimate]. These figures are of the same order as ch22's 4.5 t of soybean meal (about 2.1 t of soy protein at 46%) avoided per t of meat protein; ours are higher for pork and poultry because we apply whole-herd feed conversion to all feed and ignore imported meat.

Applied to the study's demand path (D-BENCH, 2035: about 2,350 t of meat protein displaced, ch18):

| Case | Imported soy protein no longer fed to animals | Imported soy protein used by the substitute | Net change | Share of 2025 soybean-meal protein fed (3,312 kt) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Substitute made from imported soy | 4.5 to 8.0 kt | 2.35 to 4.7 kt | minus 0.2 to plus 5.6 kt saved | minus 0.01% to 0.17% |
| Substitute made from domestic inputs (fermentation on cassava or sugar, rice or peanut protein) | 4.5 to 8.0 kt | 0 | 4.5 to 8.0 kt saved | 0.14% to 0.24% |

(our calculation, `sec_calc.py` and `sec_calc_output.txt`) [VN-direct, Low, estimate 2035].

Two further limits. First, displacement is only about 12% of the protein the routes deliver (2.35 of 19 kt, ch18); most delivered protein replaces imported ingredients or adds to the diet, so it has no feed-saving effect at all. Second, the 19,000 t headline counts protein delivered, and on the study's lead food play (T1) the protein input is imported soy flour, so "made in Vietnam" (11,000 to 17,000 t, ch18) is not the same as "from domestic inputs". The demand model does not split routes by input origin.

**5.4 Verdict on the counter-hypothesis.** Supported for every import-based route: alternative protein for food made from imported soy, pea, gluten or mung bean moves the dependence (often from the Americas to China or Myanmar) and does not reduce it in any amount that matters. It is refuted only in a narrow sense: per kg of meat protein displaced, a soy-based food uses less imported soy protein than the animal did, but at the study's scale the saving is at most about 0.2% of the soybean-meal protein Vietnam feeds. Only routes on domestic inputs (fermentation on cassava or sugar with domestic urea; rice, peanut, seaweed or duckweed protein) reduce dependence, and their equipment and, for precision fermentation, their strains and intellectual property are imported. For the protein people eat, the security case for alternative protein therefore rests on domestic-input routes, not on alternative protein as such [VN-direct, Medium].

### 6. How peer governments frame alternative protein inside food security

Already in the package and not repeated: Singapore's Food Story 2 (alternative protein left out of the food-security target) [VIS-25; HSC-36]; the EU Protein Action Plan of July 2026, titled for resilience and strategic autonomy [VIS-15]; Japan's and Korea's food-security and food-tech plans [VIS-13; VIS-14; VIS-29]; China's 2025 No. 1 Central Document, which called for "new food resources" [PRC-29]; Saudi Arabia's single-cell protein plant plans [FTG-07]. New here:

- **China's "big food view" opinions (State Council General Office, Guobanfa [2024] No. 46, 12 September 2024).** We read the Chinese text on gov.cn, which is marked "abridged" (*本文有删减*). The guiding principle is to "seek calories and protein from plants, animals and microbes" (*向植物动物微生物要热量、要蛋白*) and to "explore new food resources", "on the basis of ensuring grain supply". Section (6), "develop biological agriculture and open up new food resources", says: actively develop synthetic biology; "steadily and cautiously" (*稳慎*) industrialise new foods; develop the food fermentation industry; speed up research on **sugar from non-grain biomass**; widen sources of new **feed** protein and "promote the application of microbial cell protein" (*推广应用微生物菌体蛋白*); develop algae foods. Targets are qualitative: a diversified food supply system "formed" by 2027 and "fully built" by 2035, with no protein volume. It also asks to "explore building a big-food monitoring and statistics system" and to guide consumers to "eat less oil and more soy, poultry and milk" [general, High, SEC-04, projection 2027 and 2035 (official targets, qualitative)].
- **China's 2026 No. 1 Central Document (dated 3 January 2026, released 3 February 2026).** It keeps "build a diversified food supply system", "actively develop forest foods and biological agriculture" and "speed up innovation in key technologies of agricultural biomanufacturing". It does not repeat the 2025 phrase "new food resources" (we searched the full text for 新型食品). It also lists "diversify agricultural imports" (*推进农产品进口多元化*) and "consolidate and raise soybean capacity" [general, High, SEC-03].
- **Gulf states.** Abu Dhabi announced an agri-food technology cluster in June 2024, framed by the statement that the UAE relies on food imports for 90% of its needs, with press-reported targets of AED 90 billion of added GDP and 60,000 jobs by 2045 [general, Low, SEC-15]. We read no primary Gulf strategy text.

**Reading.** Peers frame alternative protein inside food security in two different ways. Large importers of food (Gulf states) treat it as a way to make food at home. Large producers that import feed (China, the EU) treat new protein mainly as a way to replace imported **feed** protein from **domestic** resources: China writes microbial cell protein into food security for feed ("promote"), while new foods for people are to advance "steadily and cautiously", and it pairs both with non-grain sugar so that the carbon is domestic too. Singapore, the most active regulator, took alternative protein out of its food-security target. Vietnam's position (a net agri-food exporter, with agro-forestry-fishery exports of USD 33.84 billion against imports of USD 24.01 billion in the first half of 2025 [SEC-02], and a feed importer) is closest to China's and the EU's, and those peers do not count alternative protein made from imported inputs as security [general, Medium, SEC-03; SEC-04; VIS-15; VIS-25].

**Implication for China as competitor (ch20 axis).** China's text promotes microbial protein for feed and fermentation on domestic sugar, which adds weight to the "Chinese price dominance" end of the fermentation-market axis already in ch20 and ch23.

## Next-wave candidates

1. **Input origin of each food route made in Vietnam** (deeper; partly desk). Declaration-level customs data for HS 1208.10, 2304 food grade, 3504, 2106.10 and 1109 by importer, plus three phone calls to textured-soy and chay makers, would show how much "made in Vietnam" protein uses domestic inputs. Would change the ch18 domestic share and the security claim for play T1. Desk work cannot close the importer split; a data purchase or calls can.
2. **Capture, aquaculture and imports in the fish people eat** (different kind; desk partly). The food balance sheet does not split capture from aquaculture. FAO FishStat plus the VHLSS fish expenditure rows could give a first split; MAE fisheries statistics would close it. If capture supplies most fish eaten at home, the 27% policy cut by 2030 is the largest protein-food supply change in the study's horizon and deserves a ch01 line.
3. **Resolution 34 mid-term review and successor** (different kind; desk did not close it). One targeted search found no mid-term review; a question to MAE's planning department would. A review or successor draft would show whether import dependence or protein will enter the post-2030 frame.
4. **Power reliability for farms and plants** (different kind; needs data request). Outage hours by province for rural feeders and industrial parks (EVN) would test whether fermentation plants are less exposed than closed animal houses.
5. **China export controls on protein ingredients** (deeper; desk). We found none; a check of China's export licensing lists would close it as a clean negative or a risk.

Further desk work on the counter-hypothesis itself would be noise: the arithmetic is bounded by the study's own scale, and the sign is clear.
