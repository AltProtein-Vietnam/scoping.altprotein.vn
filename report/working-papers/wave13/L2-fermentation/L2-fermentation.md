# GFI fermentation solutions, screened for Vietnam

Line: L2 fermentation (wave 13). Source prefix: SLF. Date: 2026-10-15.

## Verdict

Most of GFI's 16 fermentation entries are tools Vietnam would buy, not build: 7 are `global frontier, monitor`, 3 `already covered`, 2 `not for Vietnam now`, 3 `strengthens existing` and 1 `new candidate public good`. The most useful result is a correction: Decree 43/2026 rewrote the licence route for using Vietnamese genetic resources, including microbes from fermented foods. Licences now come from the provincial chairman in about 19 working days, not after a 90-day ministry council, but the 1% revenue benefit-sharing floor is unchanged. The line also adds two constraints: bulk fats by fermentation cannot beat palm or coconut oil even in an ideal plant, and E10 has removed idle ethanol plants as protein retrofit candidates. It proposes one modest candidate public good, a characterised Vietnamese food-strain set (SLF-C1, balanced score 3.30), which the lead may instead fold into T9. Yield class: *added a constraint* (with one correction and one candidate); no conclusion or ranking of the plays changes.

## Headline findings

1. **The access route for Vietnamese strains changed in January 2026, and the study still describes the old one.** Under Decree 43/2026 the provincial People's Committee chairman issues the licence; the council forms in 3 working days and reports in 10, and the decision follows in about 19 working days from a complete file. The registration step and commune contract certification are gone, contracts follow a standard form, and licences run up to 5 years. Benefit sharing stays at no less than 1% of annual product revenue [VN-direct, High, SLF-02; REG-60].
2. **Vietnam's main microbial collection is a real asset.** VTCC holds over 15,000 strains, 3,869 documented to international standards and available to domestic users. It sells strains and identification services, but publishes no prices or licence terms [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-01].
3. **Strains from Vietnamese ferments are studied for flavour and starters, not protein, and none has published safety characterisation.** We found 41 works on microbes of *bánh men*, *tương*, *nem chua* and similar foods, and no genome or toxin screen of Vietnamese food *A. oryzae* or *Rhizopus* [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-03].
4. **One Vietnamese screen found a high-protein yeast, without safety data.** A *Pichia kudriavzevii*-like isolate from northern fermented foods reached 62.3 to 66.62% protein and 14.03 to 16.12 g/L of dry cells in flasks; no safety, RNA or deposit is reported [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-04].
5. **Bulk fermentation fat is not for Vietnam.** An ideal, "not achievable" microbial-oil plant sells at USD 1.81 per kg at about 8,000 t a year and USD 1.20 at about 48,000 t [general, High, SLF-08]. Palm oil was USD 1.12 per kg and coconut oil USD 1.85 per kg in August 2026 [general, High, FM-01]. Fermentation fats that sell are low-dose flavour fats made under contract in China [general, Medium, SLF-09].
6. **E10 has taken the ethanol plants.** In May 2026 domestic plants made about 25,000 m3 a month against 92,000 to 100,000 m3 of demand; imports of about 75,000 m3 a month could cost about USD 1 billion a year [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-13]. No ethanol plant is a realistic protein retrofit before 2030 (our inference).
7. **The retrofit GFI cites stalled.** Superbrewed Food paused its ethanol-plant retrofit in 2024, sold the site and moved first output to a contract manufacturer in Europe [general, Medium, SLF-12; SLF-14]. This supports the study's contract, tolling and second-hand equipment routes (T5, T7, RM-15).
8. **A domestic food fermenter exists outside the amino-acid makers.** YERA makes yeast protein for food in Nghe An, with VND 100 billion invested (founder's claim); its scale is unpublished [VN-direct, Low, RDL-01]. It and Emmay are T5's most likely first domestic users.
9. **T5 now has a foreign cost anchor and nearby competition.** An open, food-grade 50 to 5,000 L pilot plant with drying in Estonia carries a construction contract of close to EUR 2 million (scope unclear) [general, Medium, SLF-11]. Singapore and Thailand already offer pilot capacity (RGN-10, RGN-36).
10. **P6 can reuse a public model.** GFI and Hawkwood Biotech publish a downloadable dataset of fermentation cost models; biomass-protein costs converge around USD 4 to 6 per kg and microbial oils cost USD 1.5 to 19.6 per kg [general, Medium, SLF-10; advocacy].

## Scope and method

This line screens the 16 entries of the Good Food Institute (GFI) Solutions Database that the wave 13 brief assigned to fermentation [GSD-01]: GFI-006, GFI-007, GFI-009, GFI-010, GFI-033, GFI-043, GFI-045, GFI-048, GFI-058, GFI-063, GFI-064, GFI-066, GFI-067, GFI-084, GFI-089 and GFI-104. We read the full text of each entry before judging it. For each we asked: is it in scope, is it already covered by the study, is there a Vietnam-specific reason to act, and what is the verdict.

Package pages read first: ch26-plays, ch25-demand-to-frontier (25.2 to 25.5), ch10-technology-fit, ch28-robust-moves, ch05-industrial-base, ch09-economics, app-s5-facilities, app-s8-labs-talent, app-s12-costs, app-f3-frontier-tech, ch07-rules section 7.10, app-s9-regulation section S9.16, and the data files plays.csv, robust_moves.csv, policy_options.csv, engineering_suppliers.csv, pilot_labs.csv and open_questions.csv.

What the package already establishes, so we do not repeat it:

- Aerobic, sterile fermentation is captive inside Vedan, Ajinomoto and Daesang (about 11,000 to 34,000 m3, modelled). No food-grade contract fermentation or open pilot fermenter was found (ch05, S5.1.9). This is why T5 and RM-04 exist.
- Fuel ethanol plants are anaerobic and E10 (mandatory from 1 June 2026) is pulling idle plants back to fuel; about 80% of E10 ethanol was still imported in mid-2026 (ch04, S5.1.3).
- Fungal biomass costs USD 4.05 to 14.7 per kg of protein in the study's feed-grade stack; capital is 54 to 67% of cost (ch09).
- Access to Vietnamese genetic resources, including microbes from fermented foods, needs a licence and benefit sharing of at least 1% of product revenue (ch07 section 7.10; S9.16). PO-011 proposes a simplified procedure and model contracts for public microbial collections. OQ-434 asks whether Decree 43/2026 changed the Decree 59/2017 procedure.
- No local maker of sanitary stainless fermenters was found (S5, S8.8); OQ-031 is open.
- RM-15 asks for clear rules for second-hand fermentation equipment; F3.6.4 records distressed assets abroad.

## Detailed findings

### A. Strains, culture collections and access rules (GFI-045, GFI-010, GFI-063, GFI-033, GFI-104)

**A1. The Vietnam Type Culture Collection is a real but modest asset.** The national microbial gene centre at VNU Hanoi's Institute of Microbiology and Biotechnology (*Trung tâm Nguồn gen Vi sinh vật Quốc gia*, the Vietnam Type Culture Collection, VTCC) holds over 15,000 strains of bacteria, actinomycetes, yeasts and filamentous fungi. About 3,869 are documented to international standards and available to domestic users, and 2,082 are listed in the Global Catalogue of Microorganisms. It is a member of the World Federation for Culture Collections and sells pure cultures, deposit and identification services. Its stated mission includes strains from fermented foods [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-01]. The page gives no prices, no per-group counts and no mention of material transfer or benefit-sharing terms. So the collection exists and supplies strains, but we found no published food-safety characterisation (genomes, toxin-gene screens) of its food strains.

**A2. Decree 43/2026 rewrote the access route; the study's description is out of date.** We read the official text of Decree 43/2026/ND-CP (26 January 2026, in force on signature), Chapter III, which amends Decree 59/2017 [VN-direct, High, SLF-02]. Compared with the 2017 text [VN-direct, High, REG-60]:

| Step | Decree 59/2017 as written | As amended by Decree 43/2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Who licenses commercial access | Ministry (then MONRE, now MAE) | Chairman of the provincial People's Committee (new Art 6.2) |
| Registration of access | Required first, 10 days (Art 9) | Article 9 rewritten (it now covers revocation), so the registration step no longer appears; Articles 12 to 20 of Decree 59/2017, including the old dossier and appraisal rules, are repealed (Art 40.2.đ) |
| Contract certification | Commune People's Committee, 3 working days (Art 11) | Article 11 rewritten: the contract follows a standard form, Form 12 of Appendix IV, with no commune certification. A Dong Nai provincial procedure list also records the commune-level procedures as abolished (search snippet only, Low) |
| Appraisal and decision | Council review up to 90 days, decision within 15 days (Art 13, repealed) | Council formed within 3 working days, meets and reports within 10, file passed on within 2, decision within 3 (new Art 6.4): about 19 working days from a complete file (our calculation) |
| Licence term | Set in the licence (Art 16, repealed) | Up to 5 years (new Art 6.5) |
| Benefit sharing | At least 1% of annual product revenue; at least 2% of transfer or IP value; 30% to a state-managed provider, 70% to the state budget (50/50 for other providers) (Art 22) | Article 22 not amended or repealed, so the rates stand |
| Reporting | Not changed in substance | Every 2 years on use of the resource (new Art 25) |

Microorganisms are still not excluded, and "derivatives" are still defined (Art 3.6, not amended). So the cost of using a Vietnamese strain commercially is unchanged (at least 1% of product revenue), but the time and the number of offices involved fell sharply. Chapter 7 section 7.10 and Appendix S9.16 still describe the 90-day council and the commune contract step. This partly closes OQ-434 and partly delivers PO-011 (a simplified procedure and a model contract now exist, although not one designed for strain supply from collections). We could not read Appendix IV (the forms), which was not in the PDF we retrieved.

**A3. Vietnam's traditional ferments are studied, but for flavour and starters, not protein.** An OpenAlex search found 41 works on microbes from Vietnamese traditional ferments (*bánh men*, rice-wine starter cakes; *tương*, fermented soybean paste; *nem chua*, fermented pork; and others). In the 15 we screened, most study lactic acid bacteria in *nem chua* or the moulds and yeasts of *bánh men* [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-03]. Examples: a high-amylase *Rhizopus arrhizus* isolated from *bánh men* by VNU Ho Chi Minh City (2025) [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-05]; the yeasts of *tương*, dominated by *Pichia kudriavzevii* and *Millerozyma farinosa*, with a new *Moniliella* species, from a 14-year series [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-06]; and a 2008 culture-independent survey of *bánh men* microbial diversity [VN-direct, Low, SLF-07, metadata only]. *Rhizopus* is a tempeh mould and one of the non-*Fusarium* genera GFI names for biomass fermentation [general, Medium, GSD-01], so these starters hold candidate food moulds with a long history of use.

**A4. One Vietnamese screen for protein strains exists, and it skipped safety.** Phenikaa University screened 36 yeasts from fermented foods of northern Vietnam. The best, YPI-Y2, close to *Pichia kudriavzevii* (99.77% ITS identity), reached 62.3% protein in dry cells and 14.03 g/L of dry biomass, rising to 66.62% and 16.12 g/L when optimised on fructose at 30 °C and pH 6.5 [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-04]. The abstract reports no safety, RNA or toxicity testing and no deposit in a culture collection. This is exactly the GFI-045 step (screening local strains for protein) done at flask scale; the missing step is the safety and characterisation package that T9 already names as a kill test.

**A5. What this means for a "local strain library" public good.** The assets exist (VTCC, FIRI's industrial collection, the VAST collections, a research base on traditional starters), and the access route is now faster. What does not exist is a set of food strains with the safety data a buyer or regulator would ask for: whole-genome sequence, toxin-gene and antibiotic-resistance screens, and a documented history of food use. The 1% benefit-sharing floor stays. A public good that characterises 20 to 50 food moulds and yeasts already used in Vietnamese ferments, and offers them on a standard licence, would lower the cost of T9 and of any domestic fungal food. We judge it a component of T9 and PO-011 rather than a new play, because the market pull comes only through T9 (see section D, candidates).

### B. Fats by fermentation (GFI-006, GFI-058)

**B1. Bulk fermentation fat cannot beat palm or coconut oil, even in an ideal plant.** A peer-reviewed model of the best possible heterotrophic single-cell oil plant gave a lipid selling price of USD 1.81 per kg at about 8,000 t a year, falling to USD 1.20 per kg at about 48,000 t. A thermotolerant strain cut it only to USD 1.15, and selling the whole cell to USD 0.81. The authors say this ideal case is "not achievable in reality" [general, High, SLF-08]. World prices in August 2026 were USD 1,117 per t for palm oil, USD 1,853 per t for coconut oil and USD 2,264 per t for palm kernel oil; the 2025 averages were USD 1,007, 2,480 and 2,093 [general, High, FM-01].

| Fat | USD per kg | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Palm oil, August 2026 | 1.12 | World Bank Pink Sheet [FM-01] |
| Coconut oil, August 2026 | 1.85 | World Bank Pink Sheet [FM-01] |
| Palm kernel oil, August 2026 | 2.26 | World Bank Pink Sheet [FM-01] |
| Microbial oil, ideal plant, 8,000 t a year | 1.81 | Karamerou et al. 2021 [SLF-08] |
| Microbial oil, ideal plant, 48,000 t a year | 1.20 | Karamerou et al. 2021 [SLF-08] |

So a real fermentation fat plant in Vietnam, on cassava glucose at USD 300 to 500 per t (the study's assumption in cost stack A), would sit above coconut oil and well above palm oil (our inference) [VN-direct, Low, inferred]. Vietnam's own fat assets for plant-based foods are coconut and imported palm, which line L1 covers.

**B2. Where fermentation fats are selling, they are low-dose flavour fats made by existing microbial-oil plants.** Nourish Ingredients' Tastilux, a *Mortierella alpina* biomass used at "very low inclusion rates", is made at commercial scale by CABIO Biotech, a Chinese producer of arachidonic acid for infant formula [general, Medium, SLF-09]. Search snippets report a US flavour-safety clearance in 2025 and a Melt&Marble Series A of EUR 7.3 million with launches planned from 2026 [general, Low, SLF-09 context; snippet only]. This is the T7 model: high value, low dose, made under contract in China. Vietnam has no microbial-oil fermenter. The link to T4 is weak: a fat plant would be a sugar buyer, but at palm-oil prices it cannot pay for the sugar and the capital.

**Verdict for GFI-006 and GFI-058:** bulk fats, `not for Vietnam now`; specialty animal-like flavour fats, `global frontier, monitor` (Vietnam would buy them, through the T7 contract route if a Vietnamese team develops one). Signal to watch: a fermentation fat sold below USD 2 per kg, or a Vietnamese plant-based maker buying one.

### C. Facilities: co-manufacturing, retrofits, directories and blueprints (GFI-048, GFI-089, GFI-064, GFI-066, GFI-043, GFI-084)

**C1. E10 has taken the idle ethanol plants off the table for protein.** In May 2026 Viet Nam News reported that the six domestic ethanol plants made about 25,000 m3 a month against E10 demand of 92,000 to 100,000 m3, that imports of about 75,000 m3 a month would be needed for at least the first year, and that ethanol imports could cost about USD 1 billion a year. Only "about 2 to 3 plants" ran stably; the others were restarting or converting technology [VN-direct, Medium, SLF-13]. This matches the package's August 2026 picture (S5.1.3, FS-09, IND-20). We found no report from September or October 2026 of any idle plant being offered for another use. With a USD 1 billion import gap to fill, fuel is the best-paying use of every ethanol plant, including the two longest idle (VN-direct, Low, inferred). The package's position holds and is now stronger: no Vietnamese ethanol plant is a realistic protein retrofit before 2030.

**C2. The precedent GFI cites for ethanol-plant retrofits stalled.** GFI-089 names Superbrewed Food as retrofitting bioethanol plants for protein [general, Medium, GSD-01]. Superbrewed's bacterium makes protein in an anaerobic (oxygen-free) process, so it could in principle use non-aerated ethanol tanks. But in February 2024 it paused its Minnesota ethanol-plant retrofit and moved first commercial output to one of Döhler's plants in Europe; the attraction of the ethanol site was its clarified sugar feedstock, not its tanks [general, Medium, SLF-12]. By April 2024 it had sold the Little Falls, Minnesota site and was looking for another US site [general, Medium, SLF-14]. So the one named retrofit became contract manufacturing. That supports the study's route: contract or tolled capacity first (T5, T7), and second-hand equipment from distressed foreign plants (RM-15, F3.6.4) rather than conversion of Vietnamese fuel plants.

**C3. Other retrofit categories in Vietnam.** Breweries remain the only clear stock of idle food-grade buildings: Heineken's Quang Nam plant (now Da Nang), the smallest of its six, has been suspended since 12 June 2024; we found no report of a sale or conversion in 2025 or 2026 (search results only) [VN-direct, Low, IND-25]. Brewery tanks are anaerobic and not built for aseptic work, so the value is the permitted site, utilities and wastewater plant, which suits solid-state koji rooms (T9) more than aerobic fermentation (our inference). Juice and dairy lines suit plant drinks (T10, line L1).

**C4. A domestic food fermenter outside the amino-acid makers exists, but its scale is unknown.** Yeast Era (YERA) makes yeast protein for human food in its own factory in Nghe An. The founder reported VND 100 billion invested, mostly own capital (about USD 3.8 million at 26,000 VND per USD, our calculation), four patents and products for sports nutrition [VN-direct, Low, RDL-01; press, company claims]. Press describes the process as rice starch converted to protein biomass in 16 to 24 hours (search snippets only, Low). We found no fermenter volume, capacity or food-safety certificate. Chapter 5 says aerobic capacity is "captive inside three amino-acid makers"; YERA, and Emmay's fermented shiitake products (AIS-39), qualify that. They are the most likely first domestic users, and possibly small hosts, of T5.

**C5. Shared food-grade pilot plants are being built elsewhere, which gives T5 a cost anchor and competition.** Estonia's state-owned Metrosert awarded a construction contract of close to EUR 2 million for an open-access, food-grade pilot plant with 50 L, 500 L and 5,000 L fermenters and downstream processing to dry powder on one site, due to open in early 2027. The article does not say whether the contract covers equipment as well as building [general, Medium, SLF-11]. Search results also show a GEA-built open precision-fermentation and biomass scale-up line at NIZO in the Netherlands (operation from 2027) and a food manufacturing licence for ScaleUp Bio's Singapore pilot plant of up to 10,000 L (snippets only, Low). The package already cites ScaleUp Bio and Thailand's EECi (RGN-10, RGN-36). For T5, this means two things. First, a 50 to 5,000 L food-grade line with drying is a single-digit-million-euro project in Europe, which is the right order for T5's "pilot-scale capital, not estimated" (our inference, Low). Second, the users T5 hopes to attract can book time in Singapore and Thailand now; T5 must win on cost, feedstock (cassava glucose) and proximity.

**C6. Directories and blueprints: Vietnam is a taker, and the global gap is partly filled.** GFI now runs a contract-manufacturing database (named in GFI-048) [general, Medium, GSD-01], and GFI APAC runs an ecosystem database with co-manufacturers, pilot facilities, equipment suppliers and consultants. Its tables did not load for us, so we could not check for Vietnamese entries [general, Low, SLF-15]. We found no open-access model facility blueprint from GFI as of October 2026 (search results only, Low). The package's `engineering_suppliers.csv` and S8.8 already serve as the Vietnamese directory. Adding Vietnamese co-packers and the FIRI and HCMC pilot centres to GFI APAC's database would cost one form submission; it is housekeeping, not a finding.

**C7. Local fabrication of food-grade vessels: still not found.** Three searches in Vietnamese and English for local makers of stainless fermenters, pilot bioreactors and large brewery tanks returned only foreign suppliers and second-hand listings [VN-direct, Low, search record]. Web search is weak on Vietnamese small engineering firms, so this does not close OQ-031; a call to the Vietnam Mechanical Enterprises Association is still the cheapest way. The established equipment makers already sell precision-fermentation lines (for example GEA's 2026 line for NIZO, snippet only), so a Vietnamese "bioreactor company" (GFI-084) has no Vietnam-specific reason to exist.

### D. Cost models and data (GFI-009, GFI-067)

**D1. P6 can start from a public model instead of building one.** GFI and Hawkwood Biotech publish a techno-economic insights page with a downloadable dataset of published fermentation cost models and a compilation of incumbent product prices. It reports that biomass-protein production costs in published models converge around USD 4 to 6 per kg, and that microbial oils cost USD 1.5 to 19.6 per kg in published models, "far above commodity oil and fat prices"; the licence terms are not stated [general, Medium, SLF-10; advocacy]. The package already cites the same partners' APAC site-selection model (RGN-01). P6 (open cost model and price series) would cost less if it adapted this dataset to Vietnamese inputs (power, steam, labour, cassava glucose, capital charge) from S12, and spent its effort on the part nobody else supplies: a public series of Vietnamese food protein-ingredient prices.

**D2. A data lake (GFI-067) is premature in Vietnam.** It pools process data across many manufacturers. Vietnam has no food-protein fermentation producers to pool beyond YERA and a few small firms. `not for Vietnam now`.

### F. Candidate: SLF-C1, a characterised Vietnamese food-strain set (public good)

We propose one candidate, and only as a public good. The lead may prefer to fold it into T9 and PO-011 as a component; the evidence supports either.

- **What.** Characterise 20 to 50 moulds and yeasts already used in Vietnamese ferments and held in public collections (VTCC, FIRI, the VAST institutes): *Aspergillus oryzae* from *tương*, *Rhizopus* and *Amylomyces* from *bánh men*, and protein-rich yeasts such as the *Pichia kudriavzevii* isolates. For each: whole-genome sequence, toxin-gene and antibiotic-resistance screens, growth on cassava glucose and okara, and protein content. Publish the data and supply the strains on one standard licence with pre-agreed benefit-sharing terms under the Decree 43/2026 route.
- **Why Vietnam.** The collections and a research base on traditional starters exist (A1, A3). One Vietnamese screen has already found a yeast with 62 to 67% protein, without safety data (A4). We found no genome or toxin characterisation of Vietnamese food *A. oryzae* or *Rhizopus* strains: an OpenAlex search returned 4 records, none on this [VN-direct, Low, SLF-03]. Licensing is now fast (about 19 working days), but benefit sharing of at least 1% of product revenue still applies (A2). T9's first kill test is "strain safety data cannot be obtained". Strains with a documented history of use in Vietnamese food also fit P1's traditional-food lane.
- **Gap it fills.** None of P1, P4, P5 or P6 supplies strains with safety data, and PO-011 covers only the procedure. Against T9 it scores higher on technical readiness (4 against 3), because sequencing and screening are routine, but equal or lower on market pull.
- **Scores (calibrated against T9 and T5).** Market pull 2 (users are a handful of startups and labs: T9 entrants, YERA, Emmay, Phenikaa); Vietnam advantage 3 (local starters and collections, but every fermenting country has its own); technical readiness 4; route clarity 4 (no product approval needed; the access route is now short, but terms for collection strains are untested); capital efficiency 4 (research-grant scale; not estimated, so not 5); defensibility 3 (local strains with Vietnamese use history cannot be imported, but foreign koji starters can); public value 3. Balanced score 3.30 (our calculation: 2×15 + 3×15 + 4×15 + 4×15 + 4×15 + 3×10 + 3×15 = 330, divided by 100). Public goods are not scored in `plays.csv` in version 0.9, so the score is for comparison only: it would sit between T10 (3.05) and T5 (3.45).
- **Kill tests.** Fewer than three firms ask for strains within a year of release; the screened food strains carry toxin-gene clusters (cyclopiazonic acid, 3-nitropropionic acid) or acquired resistance with no clean alternatives; MAE or the provinces will not accept standard benefit-sharing terms for collection strains.
