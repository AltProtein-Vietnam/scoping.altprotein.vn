# The Protein Project (EU): publications screened for Vietnam

Line: L7 The Protein Project, EU (wave 13). Source prefix: PPE. Date: 2026-10-15.

## Verdict

The Protein Project is a Brussels advocacy group, funded by "two large philanthropic foundations" it does not name. Its eight publications are mostly EU diet, livestock and fermentation policy, and four of the eight screen out or are already covered. Two methods transfer. The soy-dependency scenario analysis can be run on numbers the study already holds. It confirms chapter 22: an EU-style 20% cut in imported soy protein by 2035 is far out of reach in Vietnam, because Vietnamese demand grows 30% while EU demand is flat, and the in-scope levers deliver only about 9% to 16% of the cut (our calculation). The legume roadmap method does not fit feed soybean, but it does fit Vietnam's food soybean: the beans behind tofu and soy milk are 97.5% imported, a named buyer breeds non-GM varieties, and the 2010 soybean area would equal 76% to 95% of today's food use. Yield class: **added a candidate or route** (PPE-C1, conditional on a farm-economics test we could not run) and **added a constraint** (domestic food beans cost about 1.4 to 2.3 times imports). The rest is noise for Vietnam.

## Headline findings

1. **Who runs it and who pays.** The Protein Project is a five-person Brussels group (two co-founders, two project leads, a policy lead). It says it is "made possible by the generosity and strategic investments of two large philanthropic foundations", and names neither. We found no legal form, board or independence statement on its site [general, Medium, PPE-02]. Its soy report credits in-kind support for one fellow from the School for Moral Ambition and #SustainablePublicAffairs, and peer reviewers from CEPS, EPC, EIT Food, IDDRI, KU Leuven, TABLE and Wageningen University and Research [general, High, PPE-03].
2. **Eight publications, May 2025 to October 2026.** Five are its own: a fermentation forum brief, an EAT-Lancet policy brief, a soy scenario analysis, a livestock field report and a fava bean roadmap. One is a Systemiq report written for it, one a World Economic Forum briefing it contributed to, and one a Dutch opinion piece [general, High, PPE-01].
3. **The soy scenario method is a target-first back-calculation.** It builds an EU protein matrix from FAO food balance sheets (2023), sets a target (soy protein imports from 15.1 Mt to 12.1 Mt by 2035, minus 20%), and solves backwards for the effort each of four levers must make. It then grades each effort against literature ranges. In the balanced scenario, diet change carries 40% of the cut, and the four inputs are 1.6 Mha of protein crops, 11.2 Mt of side streams, 2.2 Mt of "innovative protein" and a 10.3% shift away from animal products [general, High, PPE-03; PPE-04, projection 2035].
4. **The method runs for Vietnam on the study's own data, and the answer confirms chapter 22.** Imported soy protein was about 3.50 Mt in 2025 (3.31 Mt fed as meal, 0.19 Mt eaten as beans). A 20% cut below that by 2035 needs 1.74 Mt of protein against the trend, because trend need grows 30%. Domestic soybean at the 2010 area gives 7.6% to 9.7% of the cut, food routes on the stretch demand path 1.1%, feed efficiency 36.6% and the S-ALT what-if 12.1% (all our calculation) [VN-direct, Low, PPE-04; MAC-04, estimate 2035].
5. **The EU method leaves out the lever that matters most in Vietnam's model.** It has no feed-conversion or formulation lever, no prices or costs, and no trade response. Its "innovative protein" levels come from a GFI Europe and Systemiq market projection the study's rules would not use [general, High, PPE-04].
6. **The legume roadmap is a coordination method, not a model.** It combines a taskforce of 19 organisations and over 90 expert consultations, a barrier map from seed to shelf, a farm-economics model of three farm types for 2025 to 2040 (FADN data, case studies, CAP payments), and policy asks tied to named EU instruments [general, Medium, PPE-05].
7. **That method fits Vietnam's food soybean, not its feed soybean.** About 540 kt of soybeans were used as food in 2025 against 42 to 43 kt grown at home. The 2010 area (205 kha) at 2.0 to 2.5 t/ha would give 410 to 510 kt, 76% to 95% of food use, but only 3% to 4% of the 2035 meal need (our calculation) [VN-direct, Low, AQF-20; MAC-04; AIB-06, estimate 2035].
8. **A buyer exists and already breeds for it.** Vinasoy's research centre holds 2,072 soybean lines. Its VINASOY 05 variety yields above 2.5 t/ha at Cu Jut in Lam Dong (former Dak Nong). Its parent QNS plans to invest in soybean raw-material areas in 2026, but publishes no tonnage of domestic beans [VN-direct, Medium, AIB-06].
9. **Cost is the constraint.** Domestic beans sold at 25,000 to 30,000 VND/kg in 2024 against 13,000 to 15,000 VND/kg for imports [VN-direct, Low, AQF-20]. Canadian-origin beans, the likely non-GM food grade, landed at USD 686/t in 2023, only 2% to 9% above Brazilian and US beans. Domestic beans therefore cost 1.4 to 1.7 times Canadian beans, or 1.7 to 2.3 times the import price in the press source (our calculation, different years) [VN-direct, Low, SEC-05; AQF-20].
10. **We found no Vietnamese farm-economics study of soybean or mung bean in rotations.** One OpenAlex search of 32 Vietnam-affiliated works found soil and emission studies of rice-upland rotations, not crop margins [VN-direct, Low, PPE-13]. The farm-economics step of the roadmap cannot be run with data the study holds.
11. **The fermentation forum asks the EU the question PO-016 asks Vietnam.** NGOs including BEUC, Greenpeace and EEB, with industry, want clarity on the legal status of products of GM microorganisms in contained use, on residual GM material such as antibiotic-resistance markers, and on harmonised GM labels [general, Medium, PPE-06].
12. **A precedent for the soybean import risk.** The United States embargoed soybean exports from June 1973 until contracts were reinstated by 1 October 1973. Japan, then 97% import-dependent with 90% from the US, answered by financing a new origin in Brazil's Cerrado, not by growing more at home [general, Medium, PPE-12]. Vietnam's soybeans came 89% from Brazil and the United States in 2025 [VN-direct, High, MAC-09].

## 3. The soy-dependency scenario analysis: how it was built

**Data foundation.** FAOSTAT food balance sheets for the EU-27 (2023 revision), in volume and protein, with FAOSTAT crop yields (2024 revision). The authors build a protein supply and utilisation matrix by commodity subgroup: production, imports, exports, stock change, feed and food protein. Self-sufficiency is production over supply [general, High, PPE-04].

**A stress test.** A separate run sets all feed and food trade to zero. Livestock can then use only EU-grown feed. The EU would lose about 15% of beef and dairy, about 40% of pork and up to 60% of poultry and eggs, about 40% of livestock output in all [general, High, PPE-03; PPE-04].

**Four levers, each one parameter with three literature levels.**

| Strategy | Parameter | Business as usual | Moderate | High | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Domestic protein crops | Extra area, Mha | 0.61 | 1.63 | 2.45 | Five crops weighted by protein yield per ha |
| 2 Side streams to feed | Mt used (3.0% to 3.8% protein) | 0.5 | 15.5 | 19.7 | Food-waste literature; swill rules |
| 3 "Innovative protein" | Mt product (33% protein) | 0.1 | 1.49 | 3.06 | GFI Europe and Systemiq market projection for 2035 |
| 4 Diet change | % cut in all livestock products | 0.3 | 10 | 20 | EAT-Lancet extrapolation; Dutch target |

Source: PPE-04, Table A1 [general, High].

**Solving backwards.** Step 1 runs each scenario forward at those levels to get each lever's share of the cut (balanced scenario: 20.9%, 22.0%, 16.5%, 40.7%). Step 2 fixes the target (3 Mt of crude protein, 20%) and uses an iterative solver to find the input each lever needs to deliver its share. Two synergy rules apply: side streams count 1.3 times when fermentation is active, and protein that fills the food gap left by diet change earns a 50% soy credit. Verified results fall between 19.9% and 21.1%. Each required input is then graded from "comfortably feasible" to "beyond reach" against Table A1 [general, High, PPE-04].

**Results.** Business as usual cuts soy protein imports from 15.1 to 14.62 Mt, which the report's figure labels minus 1% but is minus 3.2% (our calculation; DG row 3). The agriculture scenario needs 3.0 Mha and 25.1 Mt of side streams, both beyond reach. The innovation scenario needs 4.4 Mt of innovative protein and a 32.8% diet shift, both beyond reach. The balanced scenario needs 1.6 Mha, 11.2 Mt, 2.2 Mt and 10.3%, with contributions of about 0.6, 0.5, 0.6 and 1.2 Mt. Farmland falls about 10.4% [general, High, PPE-03, projection 2035]. Wider effects on trade, jobs, health and nature are scored by judgement, not modelled.

**Weak points.**
- "Feasible" means "inside a literature range", not a cost, a price or a probability.
- The target is set first, so the analysis says what each lever must do, not what it will do.
- Diet change carries the largest share, and lever 3 rests on a market projection.
- There is no feed-efficiency lever and no trade or price response.
- Small inconsistencies: a 1-in-8 rotation in one place and 1-in-6 in another; an appendix placeholder ("Update with final report"); the BAU label above.

**Could the method be run for Vietnam with data the study already has?** Mostly yes. The study already built the same matrix from FAO food balance sheets in wave 11 (`protein_supply_by_source.csv`, `self_sufficiency_by_food.csv`) and a stronger, forward balance model with four scenarios (`tools/balance_model.py`). Its attribution of 61% to 71% of animal protein to imports is a wider version of the EU stress test, because it also counts imported maize (PPE-I09: already covered). What it lacks is a farm-level crop margin and any estimate of food-grade side streams. We ran the back-calculation on the study's numbers (`tools/vn_backcalc.py`; output in `tools/vn_backcalc_output.txt`).

**Vietnam on the EU method, 2035 (all our calculation)** [VN-direct, Low, estimate 2035]

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Imported soy protein, 2025 | 3.50 Mt (feed 3.31; food beans 0.19, 5.5%) |
| EU-style target, 2035 (20% below 2025) | 2.80 Mt |
| S-BASE trend, 2035 (food beans held at 2025) | 4.55 Mt (+30%) |
| Cut needed against trend | 1.74 Mt protein, 3.8 Mt soybean-meal equivalent |
| Domestic soybean at 2010 area (205 kha), 2.0 to 2.5 t/ha | 0.13 to 0.17 Mt net, 7.6% to 9.7% of the cut |

Inputs: soybean meal 46% protein and beans 36% (balance model); 540 kt of food beans and 98.4% imported [MAC-04]; 42.5 kt domestic output [MAC-04]; 205 kha in 2010 [AQF-20]; 2.5 t/ha for VINASOY 05 [AIB-06]; about 4.5 t of meal avoided per t of meat protein displaced (ch22). The levers were not run together in the balance model, so the shares should not be added.

**Reading.** The EU needs four levers near their limits to cut 20% with flat demand. Vietnam would need about 1.74 Mt against a rising trend, and its in-scope levers reach about 9% to 16%. That supports chapter 22's position: hold the 2050 soybean-meal need to about 7 to 9 Mt as an ambitious range, and do not set a soybean goal. The useful additions are a feasibility-graded lever table for app-f4 and one food-side point: the only in-scope lever with weight is domestic soybean, and its weight is on the food side.

## 4. The legume roadmap: how it was built, and whether it transfers

**Method** [general, Medium, PPE-05]:
1. **One crop as the test case.** Fava bean, chosen for fit to EU rotations, food and feed use, and a value chain mature enough to scale.
2. **A taskforce.** 19 organisations across inputs, farming, processing, distribution, retail, research and civil society, plus over 90 expert consultations (the executive summary says 20 actors and 60+ experts; DG row 4). Participation does not mean endorsement; the final text is the project's synthesis.
3. **A barrier map** across research, farming, processing and market, leading to seven "strategic outcomes".
4. **Policy asks tied to named instruments:** the Common Agricultural Policy, a protein-crop strand in the Common Market Organisation with producer organisations and multi-year contracts, a legumes observatory for prices and volumes, public procurement, and EIB finance for regional processing hubs.
5. **A farm-economics model** (RegenAgIQ, supported by Systemiq and Wageningen University and Research). Three farm types (large arable farms in western and eastern Europe, 100 ha; a mixed crop and livestock farm, 50 ha) are run for 2025 to 2040. Each compares a cereal and oilseed rotation with one where fava replaces oilseed rape, for feed, dry food or fresh food, with and without the seven outcomes. Data: FADN, case studies in Germany, France and Poland, Yieldgapmap and CAP payments.
6. **A demand outlook** by analogy (Denmark's wholegrain partnership; quinoa's growth) and macro effects.

**Results.** Production rises from about 1,326 kt (2025) to 2,878 kt (2040), our reading of the figure labels. That needs about 360,000 more hectares on about 30,000 farms and avoids 350 kt of protein-meal imports, about 1% of the EU total [general, Medium, PPE-05, projection 2040]. The health and climate effects (EUR 42 million a year; 7 Mt CO2e a year) could not be traced to a method and are claims.

**Does it transfer to Vietnam?**
- **For feed: no.** Chapter 22 already shows domestic soybean cannot move feed imports: even 300 kha at 2.5 t/ha gives 5.6% of the 2050 bean-equivalent need [VN-direct, Low, AQF-20]. Feed is context only.
- **For food: yes, as a question worth one test.** Soy foods carry about 181,000 t of protein a year (2023), on beans that are 97.5% imported [VN-direct, High, DIE-07]. That is nearly ten times what new routes deliver in 2035 on the benchmark path (ch22). Food use was about 540 kt of beans in 2025 against 42 to 43 kt grown [VN-direct, Medium, MAC-04]. At the 2010 area of 205 kha [VN-direct, Medium, AQF-20], domestic beans would equal 76% to 95% of food use (our calculation).
- **A buyer and a breeding base exist.** Vinasoy (owned by QNS) runs the Vinasoy Soybean Applied Research Centre (VSAC), founded in 2013, with 2,072 soybean lines and varieties for acid-sulphate and saline soils. VINASOY 05 averages above 2.5 t/ha at Cu Jut. QNS plans to "continue to invest in raw-material areas (sugarcane, soybean)" in 2026 through purchase prices, seed, fertiliser and mechanisation [VN-direct, Medium, AIB-06]. Vinasoy markets non-GM soy milk (ch14), and a real-money auction found a large premium for non-GM soy milk [VN-direct, Medium, NOV-08]. ToFu House in Hung Yen runs pilot domestic soybean fields (app-s1) [VN-direct, Medium, FORM-36].
- **Land is moving out of rice.** The national land-use plan releases 654 kha of rice land by 2030 [VN-direct, Medium, NTS-15]. The crop strategy sets no soybean target [VN-direct, Medium, NTS-22]. MAE's 2026 plan asks to reduce import dependence through domestic raw-material areas, framed for livestock inputs [VN-direct, High, SEC-02]. We read the 4-page body of the MARD action plan for the crop strategy (Decision 811/QD-BNN-TT, 2024); its task annex was not in the file.
- **Cost is the constraint.** See headline 9. The non-GM premium on imports looks small: in 2023 Canadian beans cost USD 686/t, Brazilian USD 627/t and US USD 673/t (Vietnam-reported CIF unit values; our calculation from the SEC-05 extract). So the domestic bean competes with imported non-GM beans at about two thirds of its price.
- **The farm-economics step cannot be run from desk data.** We found no farm-margin study of soybean or mung bean in Vietnamese rotations [VN-direct, Low, PPE-13]. Mung bean production and prices are an open question since wave 1 (OQ-094).

**Deep evaluation: candidate PPE-C1.** Full row in `data_candidates.csv`. Summary:

| Field | PPE-C1 Domestic food-grade soybean and mung bean for soy foods and plant drinks |
|---|---|
| What | Contract-grown non-GM food soybean (then mung bean) for soy milk, tofu and plant drinks; step one a buyer-led taskforce and a farm-economics test of two or three farm types |
| Why Vietnam | Largest directly eaten imported plant protein; a buyer that breeds and contracts; rice land being released; MAE's raw-material hook |
| Must be true | Farm margin at least equal to maize or vegetables at a price buyers pay; contract yields of 2.0 t/ha or more (national about 1.3 t/ha, PCV-007); drying and storage near fields |
| Kill tests | Margin below maize or rice at any accepted price; no conditional contracts for 5,000 t a year from Vinasoy and two others (our threshold); imported non-GM beans below two thirds of the contract price for two years; yields below 2.0 t/ha for three seasons |
| First customers | Vinasoy; ToFu House and other tofu makers; plant-drink makers (T10 buyers) |
| Horizon | 1 to 5 years |
| Scores (pull, advantage, readiness, route, capital, defensibility, public value) | 3, 2, 4, 5, 4, 2, 4 (total 24; T1 26, T10 21, T9 21) |

**Why these scores.** Market pull 3: buyers exist and market non-GM, but how much they buy domestically is unknown (OQ-463). Vietnam advantage 2: domestic beans cost 1.4 to 2.3 times imports. Readiness 4: a known crop with varieties, but a large yield gap. Route clarity 5: an ordinary crop and food. Capital efficiency 4: little fixed capital. Defensibility 2: imported non-GM beans can undercut. Public value 4: it is the only route we found that raises the domestic-input share of the protein people already eat at a scale that registers. It beats T1 (made from imported soy flour) and T10 on that criterion. It fills a gap: no play covers domestic raw material for protein foods.

**What it is not.** It does not reduce feed imports, and it is not new food. It is a security move for the soy foods people already eat, and it lives or dies on farm margins.

## 5. The fermentation publications

**Fermenting common ground (PPE-06).** A ten-month, closed-door forum under the Chatham House rule. NGO forum members included BEUC, Greenpeace, EEB, EPHA, PAN and Fern; research and industry dialogue partners included Wageningen University and Research, Bio Base Europe Pilot Plant, EuropaBio and fermentation firms. It ran from a shared factual baseline (a "target product" framework for classical, biomass and precision fermentation) to five themed sheets [general, Medium, PPE-06]. Points for Vietnam:
- **GMO rules.** The forum wants a robust EFSA assessment kept. It also wants clarity on the legal status of products made with GM microorganisms under contained use, guidance on residual GM material, and harmonised GM labels. This is the question PO-016 and DMV-16 put to MAE and MOH. The forum adds what to test: no viable production cells, no recombinant DNA and no antibiotic-resistance markers (related GFI-063, food-safe selection markers).
- **Farmers in the chain.** It asks for co-owned regional fermentation plants near agricultural hubs and rural-development money for small on-farm classical fermentation. That supports T4 and RM-10 (fermentation sited next to starch plants) and T9 (koji and fungal foods).
- **Method.** A neutral NGO and industry forum is not a route we found in Vietnamese food rule-making, which runs through ministry drafting and public comment. Verdict for the forum format: not for Vietnam now (PPE-I12; our judgement, Low).

**Systemiq report (PPE-09).** Its medium scenario assumes fermented products replace 3% of EU animal protein by 2040 and would save 20 Mt CO2e, 11 Mha and 1.5 billion m3 of water a year. The volumes come from an S-curve substitution model in a forthcoming Systemiq and GFI Europe report, with tipping points of 2028 to 2030 for biomass fermentation and 2035 to 2040 for precision fermentation [general, Medium, PPE-09, projection 2040]. That is a market projection the study does not use. For scale only: 3% of animal protein in 2040 is about seven times the share the study's benchmark path reaches in Vietnam by 2050 (0.41% of meat protein; ch22), on a different market. Its sugar-beet deep dive is the EU analogue of T4 (already covered).

- **World Economic Forum briefing (PPE-10).** General. Its enablers (shared application contexts, pre-competitive data, contracts for difference) are already in RM-12 and P6. It cites a company-commissioned cultivated beef cost study, which is a company claim.

## 7. A precedent the soy report cites

The soy report opens with the 1973 US soybean embargo. We read the source it points to. After an El Nino year cut the Peruvian anchovy harvest, soybean prices spiked, and the US embargoed soybean and cottonseed exports in June 1973. Contracts were reinstated by 1 October 1973. Japan then imported 97% of its soybeans, 90% from the US. It created JICA in 1974 and financed farming in Brazil's Cerrado for about 20 years. The European Community began oilseed subsidies [general, Medium, PPE-12].

For Vietnam: soybeans came 45.8% from Brazil and 43.6% from the United States in 2025 [VN-direct, High, MAC-09], and the 2023 origin index (HHI) was 4,053 [VN-direct, High, SEC-05]. The register of shocks has no soybean export restriction. This adds one (proposed SHK-021). The lesson from Japan is that an importer's lasting answer was a second origin, not home production. That fits RM-21 (import monitoring) better than any production move.
